Blood Gases Around The Circulation.

Describe the role of the lungs in blood gas regulation.

The lungs play an important role in ventilation, ensuring that the equilibrium of O2 and CO2 within the blood remains stable

What is meant by pulmonary transit time?

  • Pulmonary transit time refers to the amount of time it takes for blood to travel from the pulmonary artery (through the lungs) to the pulmonary veins, where it returns to the heart
  • NOTE: The pulmonary circulation begins when blood enters the pulmonary arteries from the right side of the heart, and it ends when the oxygenated blood returns to the heart via the pulmonary veins to be pumped into the systemic circulation

Recall the time that blood in the pulmonary circulation spends in direct gas exchange with the lungs.

  • 0.75 seconds PULMONARY TRANSIT TIME (PvCO2/PvO2 and PaCO2/PaO2).
  • NOTE: The time taken for CO2 gas exchange is even shorter than that of O2 gas exchange. This is because it is more soluble in plasma than O2, meaning it creates more of a concentration gradient

Describe the importance of the pH scale.

  • The pH scale increases mathematical convenience, since the proton [H+] concentration within the blood is so low — Sørensen scaled the data using a -log10 transformation
  • NOTE: H+ = 0.00000004 Eq/L Na+ = 140 Eq/L and K+ = 4 Eq/L
  • NOTE: The inverse function of log is (10x), using this we can calculate [H+] from pH. [ H+] = 10^-pH

What is an acid?

  • An acid is any molecule that has a loosely bound H+ ion that it can donate
  • NOTE: H+ ions are also called protons (because an H atom with a +1 valency has no electrons or neutrons)

What is a base?

A base is an anionic (negatively charged ion) molecule capable of reversibly binding protons (to reduce the amount that are ‘free’)

Why must the acidity of the blood be regulated?

The acidity of the blood must be tightly regulated, as marked changes will alter the 3D structure of proteins — i.e enzymes, hormones, protein channels The body produces significant amounts of acid that needs clearing, but

what proportion of this is respiratory acid, and how much is metabolic acid ?

  • 99% respiratory acid
  • NOTE: Respiratory acid = carbonic acid, Metabolic acid = HCl, lactic acid etc.

Why is CO2 considered to be the measure of respiratory acid levels?

Because CO2 combines with water to form carbonic acid — CO2 + H2O → H2CO3

Describe the nature of the buffering capacity of the blood.

The blood has an ENORMOUS buffering capacity that can react almost IMMEDIATELY to imbalances

What is meant by corrective compensation?

Corrective compensation refers to the body's compensatory mechanisms to correct imbalances in blood — i.e in pH

Recall the two forms of corrective compensation.

  • Changes in ventilation (respiratory)
  • Changes in HCO3- and H+ retention/secretion in the kidneys (metabolic)

Describe how changes in ventilation can regulate the pH of the blood.

Changes in ventilation can stimulate a RAPID compensatory response to change CO2 elimination and therefore alter pH

Describe how changes in metabolism can regulate the pH of the blood.

Changes in HCO3- and H+ retention/secretion in the kidneys can stimulate a SLOW compensatory response to increase/decrease pH

Describe the difference between metabolic and respiratory corrective compensation.

Respiratory corrective compensation has a much faster speed of effect than metabolic compensation

Describe the difference between the suffixes “-osis” and “-aemia”.

-Aemia describes the condition of the blood at a snapshot in time, - osis describes the process/circumstances that will push things in a particular direction

Acidosis And Alkalosis.

Describe the four components to consider when assessing arterial blood gas (ABG) results.

  • Type of imbalance? — Acidosis (or acidaemia) / Alkalosis (or alkalaemia) / Normal
  • Aetiology of imbalance? — Respiratory (acidosis or alkalaemia) / Metabolic (acidosis or alkalosis) / Mixed (respiratory and metabolic) / Normal
  • Any homeostatic compensation? — Uncompensated / Partially compensated / Fully compensated
  • Oxygenation? — Hypoxaemia / Normoxaemia / Hyperoxaemia

Describe which measurements on an arterial blood gas measurement will allow us to obtain each of these components.

  • Type of imbalance? — pH
  • Aetiology of imbalance? — PaCO2, Base excess
  • Any homeostatic compensation? — Base excess, PaCO2
  • Oxygenation? — PaO2

Recall the physiological range for pH.

pH 7.35-7.45

Recall the physiological range for PaCO2.

4.7-6.4 kPa

Recall the physiological range for base excess (BE).

-2-2

Recall the physiological range for PaO2.

  • 10-13.5 kPa
  • NOTE: Hyperoxaemia is rarely a problem

Reporting An Arterial Blood Gas Measurement.

What is Sorensen’s equation?

  • pH = −log10[H+]
  • [H+] = 10^-pH

Recall the units of concentration used in this equation.

Moles/Litre (mol/L)

Arterial Blood Gas Measurements.

Describe how hypoventilation can affect the pH balance of the blood.

  • Under normal conditions, CO2 is readily expelled from the blood into the alveoli at a relatively fast rate
  • Hypoventilation leads to this rate of CO2 expulsion decreasing,

which in turn decreases the concentration gradient between the lungs and the blood, slowing down CO2 efflux

Describe how hyperventilation can affect the pH balance of the blood.

  • Under normal conditions, CO2 is readily expelled from the blood into the alveoli at a relatively fast rate
  • Hyperventilation leads to this rate of CO2 expulsion increasing,

which in turn increases the concentration gradient between the lungs and the blood, increasing CO2 efflux

(see lecture diagram / notes)

Recall a cause of metabolic acidosis.

Diarrhoea

Describe a mechanism of how metabolic acidosis may arise via diarrhoea.

  • Diarrhoea causes watery faeces and loss of bicarbonate (HCO3-)
  • Decreased bicarbonate levels means that more carbonic acid (H2CO3) will dissociate to make up for this lost HCO3-
  • However by nature of the equation, more H+ will be produced too — H2CO3 → H+ + HCO3- i i i i
