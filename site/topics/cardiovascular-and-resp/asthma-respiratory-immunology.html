Overview

What is asthma?

  • Asthma is a chronic respiratory condition that affects the airways in the lungs, making them inflamed, swollen, and more sensitive to certain triggers
  • This inflammation causes the airways to narrow, leading to difficulty in breathing

How many people in the UK are currently receiving treatment for asthma?

5.4 million people in the UK are currently receiving treatment for asthma

How many children are currently affected by asthma?

1.1 million children are currently affected

How many people in the UK die of an asthma attack every day?

~3 people per day

How much does the NHS spend annually treating asthma?

~£1 billion annually

Recall five cardinal features of asthma.

  • Wheeze / Dry cough / Dyspnoea
  • Episodes (attacks) / persistent symptoms (precipitated by exertion, viral colds, allergen exposure) — i.e on high pollution days
  • Atopy / allergen sensitisation — ›85-90% of patients have allergic asthma
  • Reversible airflow obstruction — narrowing of airways that can be resolved
  • Airway inflammation — Eosinophilia — Type 2 – Th2 lymphocytes – CD4+ cells

Recall the main type of asthma.

Allergic asthma

What is allergic asthma?

Allergic asthma is a type of asthma triggered by allergens — It is the most common form of asthma, especially in children and young adults

Describe the structure of a physiological airway.

  • The innermost layer is the mucosal layer which mainly contains epithelial cells, used to move mucus and provide a barrier against pathogens — types include goblet cells, ciliated cells, alveolar cells
  • The next layer is the submucosa which contains connective tissue and extracellular matrix
  • Then, the smooth muscle layer helps the airway to constrict and dilate
  • The outermost layer is the serosa adventitia which provides mechanical support and anchors the airway within the thorax

Airway Structure.

Describe the pathogenesis of allergic asthma.

  • A specific trigger leads to inflammation and that causes airway remodelling
  • This means there are changes to the smooth muscle, extracellular matrix and epithelial layers within the airways
  • This often leads to narrowing of the airways which causes dyspnoea
  • The main perpetrator in this pathway are eosinophils

Recall three potential triggers in allergic asthma.

  • Pollen
  • Dustmites
  • Mold
  • Cigarette smoke

Which type of cell is the main perpetrator of inflammation in allergic asthma?

Eosinophils

Pathogenesis Of Allergic Asthma.

What is a local allergen challenge?

  • A local allergen challenge is a diagnostic or research procedure used to evaluate the immune and physiological response of a specific area in the body to an allergen
  • In the case of asthma, the allergen is introduced to the airways, and the response is observed — the allergen is delivered using a bronchoscope

Describe the importance of a local allergen challenge for allergic asthma.

The local allergen challenge helps us to identify allergies to specific triggers and assess the severity of the response to the trigger

Airways After Local Allergen Challenge.

What is the carina?

The carina is a piece of cartilage that is situated at the point where the trachea bifurcates into the two bronchi

Carina.

Describe the role of bronchoconstriction in asthma.

  • Bronchoconstriction in asthma is usually secondary to the presence of a trigger or irritant
  • Usually, air flow within the airways is unobstructed, and is laminar

After bronchoconstriction, the airways become narrower and air flow becomes turbulent which causes wheezing sounds

This means that bronchoconstriction generates the wheeze observed in asthma

What is a wheeze?

A wheeze is a high-pitched sound that occurs during breathing, usually when exhaling

What causes wheezing sounds?

Wheezing sounds occur because narrowed air passages cause air to move more rapidly through the airways, creating turbulence that generates audible vibrations

What is stridor?

Stridor is an abnormal, high-pitched sound that occurs when breathing, typically caused by partial obstruction or narrowing of the upper airway

Describe three differences between stridor and wheeze.

Stridor is usually heard during inhalation, whereas wheezing is usually heard during exhalation • Stridor usually indicates upper airway obstruction, whereas wheezing suggests lower airway obstruction • Stridor often requires urgent intervention as it indicates an inhalation problem which can be fatal, while wheezing, although significant, usually requires management of underlying bronchoconstriction or inflammation • NOTE: Landmark that separates upper and lower airways is the carina

Airways In Asthma.

Describe what happens to the airways during an asthma attack.

  • Generally, the walls of the airways become inflamed and thickened
  • This is due to airway remodelling that occurs in response to a trigger/irritant which causes narrowing of the lumen of the airways

What is the predominant inflammatory infiltrate in asthma?

Eosinophilia

Describe the role of eosinophils in asthma.

  • The predominant inflammatory infiltrate in asthma is eosinophilia
  • They contribute to airway inflammation, damage, and remodelling by releasing toxic granules, cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, TNF-a), and lipid mediators (leukotrienes)

What is reversible airflow obstruction?

Reversible airflow obstruction describes narrowing of airways that

can be resolved Describe how asthma is generally diagnosed.

Spirometry is the diagnostic test of choice for asthma — We are looking for FEV1/FVC ratio, and the shape of the curve

What is spirometry?

Spirometry is a pulmonary function test that measures how much air a person can inhale and exhale, and how quickly they can exhale

What is forced vital capacity (FVC)?

The amount of air that can be forcibly exhaled after taking the deepest breath possible

What is FEV1?

  • The amount of air that can be forcibly exhaled in one second — i.e a snapshot of FVC
  • FEV1 = Forced Expiratory Volume in 1 second

What is peak expiratory flow?

This is the maximum speed at which a person can exhale air, and it provides information about the degree of airway obstruction

Spirometry - Fvc, Fev1 And Peak Expiratory Flow.

What is a normal FVC?

3-5 L

What is a normal FEV1/FVC ratio?

Anything below 0.70 is unusual

What is the difference between an obstructive and a restrictive disease?

  • An obstructive disease leads to problems with air leaving the lungs — This is more related to the bronchi
  • A restrictive disease leads to problems with the lungs filling up with air — This is more related to the parenchyma/functional tissue of the lungs

Describe how FEV1/FVC ratio is affected in asthma.

  • In asthma, FEV1/FVC ratio is typically reduced
  • This is because there is usually lower airway obstruction which leads to problems with exhalation, and hence air is exhaled at a slower rate

What is affected more by asthma, inspiration or expiration?

Expiration

Why is exhalation affected more than inhalation in asthma?

This is all to do with the pressure gradients involved in the breathing cycle

Recall the pathological values for FEV1/FVC ratio required to diagnose asthma.

  • ‹0.7 (adults)
  • ‹0.8 (children)

Other than FEV1/FVC ratio, what else must we look at on a spirometer trace to diagnose asthma?

  • The shape of the trace
  • It is important to understand whether a patient has reached their peak expiratory flow (PEF)

What is upper/large airway fixed obstruction?

  • Upper airway fixed obstruction refers to a blockage or narrowing of the upper part of the airway that does not change with the phases of the breathing cycle — i.e it remains consistently obstructed during both inhalation and exhalation
  • It can significantly obstruct the inhalation and exhalation curves on the spirometer

Recall two ways upper airway fixed obstruction can come about.

  • Endotracheal tumour
  • Large airway foreign body

Describe how we can distinguish between asthma and upper/large airway fixed obstruction on a spirometer.

  • Asthma can be characterised by the lack of changes on the inspiratory (lower half) of the trace, but impact on the expiratory (upper half) of the trace
  • In upper/large airway fixed obstruction, both inspiratory and expiratory traces are affected

Upper/Large Airway Fixed Obstruction Spirometry.

Recall three different factors that may affect one’s risk of developing asthma.

  • Genetic susceptibility
  • Environmental exposures — i.e allergens, infections, pollution
  • Allergy
  • Genetic susceptibility can increase allergic sensitisation to environmental exposures which can lead to allergy → reversible airflow obstruction → inflammation

Factors Affecting Risk Of Asthma.

Describe how genome-wide association studies are used to assess asthma susceptibility.

GWAS are used to identify the genes which are likely to be implicated with the phenotypic expression of asthma in individuals — i.e IL33, GSDMB

What is Type 2 immunity?

Type 2 immunity is an immune response that is primarily involved in defending the body against extracellular parasites (such as helminths or parasitic worms) and is also central to the allergic response and some autoimmune conditions • It is characterised by the activation of certain immune cells, cytokines, and antibodies that work to eliminate threats in a manner that differs from the Type 1 immune response, which is more focused on fighting intracellular pathogens like viruses

Describe how Type 2 immunity works to propagate allergic asthma.

  • Exposure to an antigen occurs — i.e trigger = pollen, dustmite, cigarette smoke
  • The antigen is recognised by an antigen-presenting cell (APC) — in the lungs, this is usually a dendritic cell
  • The APC presents MHC II (along with the antigen) on its surface which interacts with the TCR on CD4 T cells
  • Because it recognises MHC II, the naïve CD4 T cell differentiates → Th2 cells which produces the IL-4, IL-5 and IL-13 cytokines
  • The IL-4, IL-5 and IL-13 induce VCAM-1 expression, mast cell proliferation, IgE synthesis, mucin secretion, and eosinophilic airway inflammation

Recall the three cardinal cytokines of asthma.

  • IL-4
  • IL-5
  • IL-13

What is the role of IL-4 in allergic asthma?

  • IgE production from B cells
  • IgE binds to mast cells using the which can “activate” them against the Fc epsilon receptor I (FcεRI) antigen

When the IgE is then bound to antigens, these IgE are cross-linked and degranulated and this leads to release of inflammatory mediators such as histamine and serotonin What is the role of IL-5 in allergic asthma?

Eosinophil production

What is the role of IL-13 in allergic asthma?

Mucus production

Which cytokine is mainly responsible for eosinophilia in asthma?

IL-5

Inflammation In Allergic Asthma.

What is atopy?

  • Atopy refers to a genetic predisposition to develop allergic diseases such as asthma, eczema (atopic dermatitis), hay fever (allergic rhinitis), and food allergies
  • Individuals with atopy have an increased likelihood of producing IgE antibodies in response to environmental allergens, even those that are typically harmless, like pollen, dust mites, or pet dander

Describe how we can test for allergic sensitisation to specific allergens.

Blood tests or skin prick tests can be done to assess for allergy to specific antigens — Total IgE alone is not sufficient to define atopy

What is an aeroallergen?

  • An aeroallergen is an allergen that is found in the air and can trigger allergic reactions when inhaled
  • NOTE: Aeroallergens are more common than food allergens in asthma

Skin Prick Test.

What is eosinophilia?

Eosinophilia is the presence of too many eosinophils in the body

Describe three ways we can test for eosinophilia.

  • A normal blood test should suffice — ›300 cells/mcl is abnormal (in a patient with suspected/confirmed asthma)
  • An induced sputum sample may also be collected to test for eosinophil count — ›3% eosinophils is abnormal — NOTE: patient inhales a nebulised solution, usually hypertonic saline, to stimulate the production of sputum (phlegm) for analysis
  • Exhaled nitric oxide

Describe the significance of testing for eosinophilia in asthma.

Eosinophilia is often present in allergic asthma, so it can be helpful in directing treatment

What is a fraction of exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO)?

Fractional concentration of exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) is a quantitative, non-invasive and safe method of measuring airway inflammation and is an indirect marker of T2-high eosinophilic airway inflammation in asthma

Describe the significance of a fraction of exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) exam.

FeNO has a role in aiding asthma diagnosis, predicting steroid responsiveness and assessing adherence to inhaled corticosteroids

Describe how we interpret the results of FeNO.

  • Higher FeNO levels indicate greater airway inflammation and are suggestive of Type 2 inflammation
  • A decrease in FeNO levels after starting treatment indicates a reduction in airway inflammation and suggests that the treatment is working

Describe how FeNO levels may vary during asthma and inflammation.

  • Physiologically, airway epithelial cells typically release little to no NO, leading to lower FeNO levels
  • During airway inflammation, activated epithelial cells release higher amounts of NO, leading to higher FeNO levels

Recall the cut-offs for FeNO levels in adults.

  • ‹20-25 ppb = Normal
  • 20/25-50 ppb = Elevated
  • ›50 ppb = High

Recall the cut-offs for FeNO levels in children.

  • ‹15-20 ppb = Normal
  • 15/20-35 ppb = Elevated
  • ›35 ppb = High

Nitric Oxide Levels In Inflammation.

Describe the components of a clinical assessment of asthma.

  • History, examination and assessment/confirmation of wheeze when acutely unwell are all necessary
  • However, we now need to perform objective tests to diagnose asthma

Recall three objective tests that may be performed to diagnose asthma.

  • Airway obstruction on spirometry - FEV1/FVC ratio ‹ 0.7 (adults), ‹ 0.8 (children)
  • Reversible airway obstruction - Bronchodilator reversibility › 12% — improvement in airflow after usage of bronchodilator
  • Exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) › 35ppb (children), › 40ppb (adults) – in a treatment naïve patient

What is bronchodilator reversibility?

This is when there is an improvement in airflow after usage of bronchodilator

Describe the criteria that need to be met for asthma diagnosis in young people.

Diagnose asthma in children and young people (aged 5 to 16) if they have symptoms suggestive of asthma and: — FeNO level of 35 ppb or more and positive (expiratory, above y- axis) peak flow variability OR — obstructive spirometry and positive bronchodilator reversibility

Order Of Tests In Asthma Diagnosis.

Recall three major principles involved in the management of asthma.

  • Reduce airway eosinophilic inflammation
  • Acute symptomatic relief
  • Severe asthma → steroid sparing therapies

Recall two ways we can reduce airway eosinophilic inflammation.

  • Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) — these are good as we can minimise side effects; effects are localised to airways
  • Leukotriene receptor antagonists — leukotrienes are inflammatory mediators — less effective than ICS

Recall two ways we can achieve acute symptomatic relief.

  • Beta-2 agonists (smooth muscle relaxation)
  • Anticholinergic therapies (smooth muscle relaxation) — REMEMBER: acetylcholine is the main neurotransmitter at the neuromuscular junction
  • NOTE: There is an attempt to combine symptomatic relief with treatment of eosinophilic inflammation as patients fail to take both once their symptomatic relief has been achieved

Normal Vs. Asthmatic Airway.

What is a steroid-sparing therapy?

  • Steroid-sparing therapy refers to treatments or strategies that reduce the need for corticosteroids in managing chronic inflammatory or autoimmune diseases
  • The goal is to minimise the side effects and risks associated with long-term corticosteroid use while still effectively controlling the disease

Recall three steroid sparing therapies we can use in the management of asthma.

  • Biologic targeted to IgE — Anti-IgE antibody
  • Biologics targeted to airway eosinophils — Anti-interleukin-5 antibody — Anti-interleukin-5 receptor antibody
  • Biologics targeting IL-4 & IL-13 — Anti-interleukin 4Ra antibody

Describe the mechanism of action of inhaled corticosteroids (ICS).

  • The predominant effect of inhaled corticosteroids is to dampen airway eosinophilia — this tackles the underlying inflammation and reduces production of pro-inflammatory cytokines
  • They also help to reduce mucus production and smooth muscle relaxation

Describe the steps in the asthma treatment ladder from suspected to diagnosed adult asthma.

  • The moment you think a patient has got asthma, you prescribe regular inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) so that you're targeting the underlying eosinophilic inflammation
  • Then, additional therapies can be supplemented with the corticosteroids in more severe cases of asthma
  • This includes adding LABA, higher doses of ICS, referral to specialist care

Describe the steps in the asthma treatment ladder from suspected to diagnosed paediatric asthma.

Similar to adult management, the moment you think a child has got asthma, you prescribe regular inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) so that you're targeting the underlying eosinophilic inflammation

Describe two of the most important aspects of asthma management.

  • Optimal device and technique
  • Clear asthma management plan
  • Adherence to inhaled corticosteroids

What is a SMART?

  • Single inhaler Maintenance And Reliever Therapy
  • SMART uses a single combination inhaler to deliver an ICS and quick- acting LABA to patients — This helps to achieve both symptomatic relief and treatment of underlying inflammation which are both integral to the management of asthma — It provides “symptom-driven delivery of ICS”

What is a LABA?

  • LABA stands for Long-Acting Beta Agonist, which is a type of inhaled medication used to treat asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)
  • LABAs are bronchodilators that relax the muscles around the airways to keep them open

What is LTRA?

  • LTRA stands for leukotriene receptor antagonist, a class of drugs used to treat asthma, allergic rhinitis, and exercise-induced asthma
  • NOTE: Leukotrienes are inflammatory mediators

Describe how SMART can be used to improve adherence to asthma treatment.

  • Often times, patients can forget to use their inhaled corticosteroids (ICS)
  • This is because they fail to see or fully understand why they are taking them
  • Unlike LABAs which provide near-immediate symptomatic relief, inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) tackle the underlying inflammation process in asthma, which often doesn’t have immediate effects
  • This means that patients are more likely to forget to take ICS as they don’t see the immediate benefits unlike with LABAs
  • SMART combats this issue, as the LABA is combined with ICS

Describe the UK death rate of asthma in 10-24 year olds compared worldwide.

The UK has the 3rd highest death rate from asthma in 10-24 year olds

Describe the UK mortality rate of asthma in 10-14 year olds compared worldwide.

The UK has the 2nd highest asthma mortality rate for children aged 10-14 years per 100,000 population

Describe how the immune system is impaired in asthma.

  • Because the Type 2 immunity is so over-stimulated in asthma due to repeated exposure to triggers, Type 1 immunity, which is used to fight infection (i.e antiviral immunity), is suppressed
  • This leads to decreased IFN -a, b, gamma → Reduced anti-viral responses and increased viral replication result in a prolonged illness

What is anti-IgE antibody therapy?

Anti-IgE antibody therapy is a targeted treatment that aims to reduce allergic responses by neutralising immunoglobulin E (IgE), a key molecule in allergic and inflammatory pathways

Describe how anti-IgE antibody therapy can be used in the management of asthma.

  • Anti-IgE monoclonal antibodies bind to and capture circulating IgE to prevent their interaction with mast cells and basophils to stop allergic cascade
  • IgE production can also decrease with time when patients are given the anti-IgE antibody

What is Omalizumab?

Omalizumab is a monoclonal anti-IgE antibody used primarily to treat allergic asthma, particularly in cases that are difficult to control with conventional therapies

Describe the cost of Omalizumab.

  • Min 75mg 4 weekly = £1,665 /patient/year
  • Max 600mg 2 weekly = £26,640 /patient/year
  • It is very expensive, so its use must be clinically justifiable

When is Omalizumab used?

(see lecture diagram / notes)

When there is severe, persistent allergic (IgE mediated) asthma in patients ›6 years who need continuous or frequent treatment with oral corticosteroids

It is generally introduced when patients are on the maximal dose of inhaled corticosteroids

Describe the efficacy of Omalizumab.

There is a general reduction in exacerbations from patients on Omalizumab when compared to placebo — Only approx. 60% of patients are eligible — Of those, only approx. 50-60% respond to Omalizumab
