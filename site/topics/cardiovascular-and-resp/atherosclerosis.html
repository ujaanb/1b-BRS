Overview

What is coronary heart disease?

Coronary Heart Disease (CHD) is a condition characterised by the narrowing or blockage of the coronary arteries, which supply oxygen- rich blood to the heart muscle

What percentage of global deaths does coronary heart disease account for?

In 2020, coronary heart disease accounted for 32% of global deaths

Describe the world disease burden of coronary heart disease.

  • The developing world is approaching the developed world for control of infections and for diet and lifestyle
  • However, the world disease burden of coronary heart disease is increasing and has rapidly increased since the 1990s

Describe three modifiable risk factors for coronary heart disease.

  • Smoking
  • Lipid intake
  • Blood pressure
  • Diabetes
  • Obesity
  • Sedentary lifestyle

Describe three non-modifiable risk factors for coronary heart disease.

  • Age
  • Sex
  • Genetic background

Risk Factor Multiplication.

Explain the changes in the epidemiology of coronary heart disease risk factors over the last decade.

  • Reduced hyperlipidaemia — as a result of statin treatment
  • Reduced hypertension — as a result of anthypertensive drugs
  • Increased obesity → increased diabetes
  • New improvements in diabetes treatment have doubtful effect on macrovascular disease
  • Changing pathology of coronary thrombosis possibly related to altered risk factors

What is atherosclerosis?

  • Atherosclerosis is a chronic condition in which fatty deposits, known as plaques, build up on the inner walls of arteries If risk factors are general, why is atherosclerosis focal?
  • This is to do with the fact that atherosclerosis only has its effects

Recall the five main cell types involved in the pathophysiology of atherosclerosis.

  • Vascular endothelial cells
  • Platelets
  • Monocyte-macrophages
  • Vascular smooth muscle cells
  • T lymphocytes

Describe the physiological role of vascular endothelial cells.

  • Barrier function — i.e to lipoproteins, preventing them from leaking through
  • Leukocyte recruitment during infection or injury via cytokine/chemokine release

Vascular Endothelial Cells.

Describe the physiological role of platelets.

  • Thrombus (blood clot) generation — NOTE: A thrombus can either be beneficial or harmful depending on the circumstances
  • Cytokine and growth factor release

What is meant by the term ‘monocyte-macrophage’?

A monocyte-macrophage is not a single type of cell but rather refers to a continuum of closely related immune cells, specifically monocytes and macrophages

Describe the difference between a monocyte and a macrophage.

  • Macrophages are tissue-resident cells derived from monocytes
  • Monocytes are a type of white blood cell (leukocyte) that circulates in the blood
  • They both however have similar functions of phagocytosis and cytokine production, but macrophages can also be involved in antigen-presentation and tissue repair

Describe the (patho)physiological role of monocyte-macrophages in atherosclerosis.

  • Foam cell formation
  • Cytokine and growth factor release
  • Major source of free radicals
  • Matrix Metalloproteinase release

What is a foam cell?

Foam cells are lipid-laden macrophages that play a critical role in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis

What are metalloproteinases?

  • Metalloproteinases are a family of enzymes that degrade various components of the extracellular matrix (ECM) and play critical roles in tissue remodelling, repair, and a wide range of physiological and pathological processes
  • They are characterised by their dependence on metal ions (primarily zinc) for catalytic activity

Describe the pathophysiological role of vascular smooth muscle cells in atherosclerosis.

  • Migration and proliferation within the plaque of the artery wall — This leads to the formation of the fibrous cap via VSMC movement from tunica media → tunica intima
  • Collagen synthesis — fibrous cap formation
  • Remodelling & fibrous cap formation — i.e when they become overactive, this can weaken the fibrous plaque

What is a fibrous cap?

  • A fibrous cap is a structural feature of an atherosclerotic plaque that forms within the walls of blood vessels, particularly in arteries
  • It plays a crucial role in determining the stability of the plaque and the risk of complications such as a heart attack or stroke

Describe the function of the fibrous cap.

  • The fibrous cap is used to protect the blood flow within the narrowed lumen of the artery from the necrotic, lipid core of a typical atherosclerotic plaque
  • This is good because the necrotic core of an atherosclerotic plaque typically contains thrombogenic materials such as lipids and cholesterol crystals
  • The fibrous cap hence increases the stability of the plaque, reducing the risk of these serious cardiovascular events

Describe how the size of the fibrous cap is important in atherosclerosis.

  • A thicker fibrous cap is generally better because it indicates a more stable atherosclerotic plaque
  • A thicker cap is also more resistant to damage from matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)

Stable Vs Vulnerable Plaque.

Describe the pathophysiological role of T lymphocytes in atherosclerosis.

  • Macrophage activation - CD4 Th1
  • Macrophage de-activation - CD4 Treg
  • Vascular smooth muscle cell death
  • B cell / antibody production - CD4 Th2

Blood Stem Cell Hierarchy.

What initially triggers plaque formation in atherosclerosis?

Plaque formation is often triggered by damage or dysfunction of the endothelium

Describe three ways that endothelial damage can arise in individuals with atherosclerosis.

  • High blood pressure (hypertension)
  • High cholesterol (LDL → oxLDL)
  • Smoking — Cigarette smoke has toxic chemicals that can harm the endothelial lining
  • Diabetes — High blood sugar can lead to endothelial dysfunction.
  • Inflammation — Inflammatory conditions (such as infections or autoimmune diseases) can also contribute to endothelial injury.

Recall another factor that can increase one’s risk of endothelial damage and hence atherosclerosis.

  • Areas of turbulent blood flow, such as at anatomical bends and branches of arteries, are more likely to develop plaques
  • These areas experience higher levels of stress, which can activate endothelial cells and promote a pro-inflammatory environment

which leads to plaque formation Describe how plaques generally form in atherosclerosis.

  • Damage to the endothelium occurs due to one of the above risk factors
  • This damage leads to the increased permeability of the endothelium to molecules such as LDL
  • LDL → oxLDL via oxidation, and these are highly toxic so they trigger an inflammatory response, recruiting white blood cells (macrophages)
  • Macrophages ingest the oxLDL → foam cells and this leads to the formation of atherosclerotic plaques
  • Vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) also migrate from the tunica media → tunica intima which leads to the formation of the fibrous cap
  • The plaque grows, and it consists of cholesterol, foam cells, smooth muscle cells, and extracellular matrix

Atherosclerotic Plaque.

What is meant by the term “plaque rupture” in atherosclerosis?

Plaque rupture is when the fibrous cap of an atherosclerotic plaque,

which covers a necrotic core containing lipids, foam cells, and debris, becomes thin or unstable and tears/bursts Describe the significance of plaque rupture in atherosclerosis.

(see lecture diagram / notes)

When a plaque ruptures, the body's clotting system is activated

  • This leads to the formation of a blood clot at the site of plaque rupture which can be dangerous in major arteries
  • For example, if this occurs in a coronary artery, it can lead to a myocardial infarction, however if this occurs in a cerebral artery, it

can lead to a stroke What is a fatberg?

‘Fatbergs’ are large, solid masses formed in sewer systems due to the congealing of fats, oils, and grease — They can grow to enormous sizes and clog pipes

Describe what is meant by the fatberg mechanism of atherosclerotic deposition.

  • The fatberg mechanism of atherosclerotic deposition refers to the idea that the increased presence of fats within the blood (hyperlipidaemia) leads to deposition of these fats within the walls of arteries, which causes vessel blockage and obstruction Is atherosclerosis actually a ‘fatberg’?
  • No.
  • The fatberg mechanism of atherosclerosis is inaccurate and outdated, and trials have shown us that atherosclerosis is actually a very active disease

Describe how the CANTOS trial shows that atherosclerosis has an inflammatory basis.

  • The CANTOS Trial showed us that atherosclerosis has an inflammatory basis
  • Patients at high risk of atherosclerosis complications injected with antibodies to Interleukin -1 (IL-1)
  • Fewer major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) (mostly stroke and heart attacks) were observed in treated patients

Describe the role of IL-1.

  • Interleukin-1 (IL-1) is a key pro-inflammatory cytokine
  • It acts on the hypothalamus to increase body temperature, causing fever and dysregulated IL-1 signalling is associated with several inflammatory and autoimmune diseases
  • Overall, IL-1 plays a role in orchestrating both protective responses against infection and contributing to pathological inflammation

How is IL-1 generated in atherosclerosis?

There are thought to be many mechanisms behind the generation of IL-1 in atherosclerosis, however cholesterol crystal formation seems to be the most likely one

Recall the main inflammatory cells in atherosclerosis.

  • Macrophages
  • NOTE: Macrophages are derived from monocytes

How are macrophage subtypes regulated?

Macrophage subtypes are regulated by combinations of transcription factors binding to regulatory sequences on DNA

Recall the two main subtypes of macrophages.

  • Inflammatory macrophages
  • Non-inflammatory and resident macrophages

Describe the function of inflammatory macrophages.

Inflammatory macrophages are adapted to kill microorganisms

Describe the function of non-inflammatory and resident macrophages.

Non-inflammatory and resident macrophages normally have homeostatic functions – they may be parenchymal

Recall two types of non-inflammatory/resident macrophage.

  • Alveolar resident macrophages
  • Splenic resident macrophages (spleen)

Describe the role of alveolar resident macrophages.

Surfactant lipid homeostasis — i.e surfactant produced by Type II alveolar pneumocytes

Describe the role of splenic resident macrophages.

Iron homeostasis

Recall the three types of lipoprotein.

  • LDL
  • HDL
  • Oxidised LDLs/Modified LDLs

What is low-density lipoprotein (LDL)?

  • Low-Density Lipoprotein (LDL) is a type of lipoprotein responsible for transporting cholesterol from the liver to various tissues throughout the body
  • It is often referred to as "bad cholesterol"
  • NOTE: LDL is a lipoprotein, it is not cholesterol itself

What is high-density lipoprotein (HDL)?

  • High-Density Lipoprotein (HDL) is a type of lipoprotein often referred to as "good cholesterol" because it helps remove excess cholesterol from the bloodstream and tissues
  • NOTE: HDL is a lipoprotein, it is not cholesterol itself

Where do HDL and LDL get their names from?

  • LDL particles are less dense because they have a lower protein content and a higher lipid content compared to HDL
  • HDL particles are more dense because they have a higher protein content and a lower lipid content compared to LDL
  • NOTE: Protein is more dense than lipids

What are oxidised LDLs/modified LDLs?

  • Oxidised LDL (oxLDL) and modified LDL are forms of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) that have undergone chemical or structural alterations, often by free radicals, enzymes and aggregation
  • These modified forms of LDL are highly toxic and pro-inflammatory and they play a critical role in the development of atherosclerosis

Describe how the J-curve can be related with LDL cholesterol.

  • The J-curve is a term used to describe the graphical representation of the relationship between LDL cholesterol levels and risk of cardiovascular complications (either myocardial infarction or stroke )
  • It explains how when you increase LDL cholesterol levels, the initial risk of cardiovascular complications decreases, before increasing substantially after a certain point (the bottom of the J-curve )

Ldl And Hdl Cholesterol.

Describe the structure of an LDL molecule.

  • An LDL molecule has a phospholipid monolayer with fats within the molecule
  • They also have apolipoproteins within the lipid monolayer which provide “molecular addresses for fat delivery” and some even interfere with clotting and clot lysis

Describe the implications of LDL and HDL on atherosclerosis.

  • LDL has pro-atherogenic effects seeing as it is involved in the transport of cholesterol from liver → peripheral tissues — High levels of LDL lead to increased risk of LDL oxidation → endothelial damage via oxLDL action → inflammation and plaque progression
  • HDL on the other hand has anti-atherogenic effects seeing as it is involved in the transport of cholesterol from peripheral tissues → liver — It inhibits the expression of adhesion molecules on endothelial cells, reducing monocyte recruitment and inflammation in arterial walls — It also promotes the efflux of cholesterol from foam cells, reducing the lipid content of plaques.

Describe how the modification of subendothelial trapped LDL can lead to atherosclerosis.

  • LDLs leak through the endothelial barrier — likely due to endothelial activation in areas of vortex
  • LDL is trapped by binding to sticky matrix carbohydrates (proteoglycans) in the sub-endothelial layer and becomes susceptible to modification
  • The best studied modification is oxidation via free radical attack from activated macrophages — LDL hence becomes oxidatively modified by free radicals
  • Oxidised LDL is phagocytosed by macrophages and stimulates chronic inflammation!

What is a vortex in the context of atherosclerosis?

  • In the context of atherosclerosis, a vortex refers to a specific pattern of blood flow characterised by swirling or turbulent motion
  • This type of flow can occur in areas of arterial branching or curvature, and it plays a significant role in the initiation and progression of atherosclerotic plaques

Endothelium In Atherosclerosis.

What are “foam cells”?

Foam cells are lipid-laden macrophages that play a critical role in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis

How are foam cells produced?

Foam cells arise when macrophages engulf oxidised low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL) within the arterial wall, triggering inflammation and a cascade of processes leading to the development of atherosclerotic plaques

Describe three ways foam cells are involved in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis.

  • Foam cells aggregate in the intima to form fatty streaks, the first visible sign of atherosclerosis
  • They secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines (i.e IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6)

which amplifies local inflammation as well as releasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) which can cause LDL → oxLDL

NOTE: Endothelial cells and vascular smooth muscle cells

Tunica Intima.

What is familial hyperlipidaemia?

  • Familial hyperlipidemia ( FH), is a genetic disorder characterised by elevated levels of lipids (cholesterol and/or triglycerides) in the blood, particularly low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol
  • Individuals with FH have massively elevated cholesterol (›~20 mmol/L) — (effective ‘normal’ range ~1-5 mmol/L)

What type of disease is familial hyperlipidaemia?

Autosomal dominant with gene dosage

What is meant if a disease has gene dosage?

It typically means that homozygotes are worse off than heterozygotes

Describe the main clinical feature of familial hyperlipidaemia.

  • Xanthomas and early atherosclerosis
  • If FH is untreated, it can cause fatal myocardial infarctions before age 20

Xanthomas.

What causes familial hypercholesterolemia?

One of the LDL-receptor molecules on cell surfaces of most liver cells

which is used to break down LDL, are faulty

  • This leads to LDL not being broken down appropriately, and the high levels accumulating in the serum
  • This will obviously lead to higher amounts of cholesterol being transported from the liver to the peripheral tissues

Where is the LDL-receptor found?

Liver cells

Describe the importance of the LDL-receptor.

  • LDL enters hepatocytes via LDL-R
  • This usually leads to the breakdown of LDL within these hepatocytes
  • NOTE: However, in LDL-R negative individuals, macrophages still accumulate cholesterol

Describe the significance of LDL-cholesterol accumulation by macrophages in LDL-R.

The fact that LDL-cholesterol is still accumulated by macrophages in individuals that lack the LDL-R is evidence that there is another receptor used to uptake LDL-C

Recall the other receptor used to uptake cholesterol in macrophages.

Scavenger receptor

Describe the difference between a scavenger receptor and a common LDL- receptor.

  • Scavenger receptors are not under feedback control
  • The common LDL-receptor is under feedback control and its expression is down-regulated as intracellular cholesterol levels increase

When are scavenger receptors expressed?

  • In atherosclerotic lesions, macrophages can express scavenger receptors and hoover up chemically modified LDL
  • They ‘accidentally’ bind to OxLDL

Evidence Of A Scavenger Receptor.

What are HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors?

HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors, commonly known as statins, are a class of drugs used to lower cholesterol levels in the blood

Describe the impact of HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors.

  • HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors lower cholesterol levels in the blood by inhibiting the rate-limiting step in cholesterol synthesis — HMG-CoA (6C) → mevalonate (6C)
  • This prevents the synthesis of cholesterol and hence reduces serum cholesterol

Describe how LDL-receptor expression can be altered in atherosclerosis.

  • The expression of the LDL-receptor is negatively regulated by intracellular cholesterol
  • EXPLANATION: This makes sense as if intracellular cholesterol is high, you don’t want much more coming in

Recall another thing that is inhibited by intracellular cholesterol levels.

  • Cholesterol synthesis is also negatively regulated by intracellular cholesterol — This led to the discovery of HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors (statins)
  • NOTE: Increasing the uptake of cholesterol can hence reduce serum cholesterol levels

What is PCSK9?

PCSK9 is a key regulator of LDL cholesterol levels by controlling the degradation of LDL receptors in the liver

Describe the physiological function of PCSK9.

PCSK9 binds to LDL receptors on the surface of liver cells

When PCSK9 is bound to an LDL receptor, the receptor is targeted for degradatio n in lysosomes instead of being recycled to the cell surface What are PCSK9 inhibitors?

  • These are monoclonal antibodies or other molecules designed to block PCSK9 from binding to LDL receptors, preventing receptor degradation
  • NOTE: PCSK9 inhibitors are now used to supplement statins

Describe the effect of PCSK9 inhibitors.

  • Since PCSK9 is used to degrade LDL-R, its inhibition leads to the upregulation of LDL-Rs
  • This means LDL-R expression goes up in the presence of a PCSK9 inhibitor → decreased serum LDL-cholesterol levels
  • NOTE: PCSK9-deficient humans are protected from cardiovascular disease

Describe how PCSK9 inhibitors are used clinically.

  • PCSK9 inhibitors are now in use for severe or statin-resistant hyperlipidaemia.
  • They are administered in the forms of: — Antibodies — Antisense — Si-RNA

Describe how macrophages can induce reverse cholesterol transport.

Macrophages can use their ABCA1 and ABCG1 pumps to induce reverse cholesterol transport

What are ABCA1/ABCG1 cholesterol pumps?

These are cholesterol pumps found on the surface of macrophages used to export cholesterol to HDL for reverse cholesterol transport

Describe how these ABCA1/ABCG1 pumps work.

  • ABCA1/ABCG1 pumps work by “scooping” cholesterol out of the macrophages and putting it into the HDL molecules for reverse cholesterol transport
  • These pumps are selective to Apolipoprotein A which is only found on HDL and they are good drug targets

What are macrophage scavenger receptors?

  • Macrophage scavenger receptors (SRs) are a group of cell surface receptors expressed on macrophages that are primarily involved in binding a wide variety of ligands, including modified forms of lipoproteins, pathogens, and cellular debris
  • They usually uptake these ligands too, hence the term “scavenger”

Recall the two main types of macrophage scavenger receptor.

  • Macrophage scavenger receptor A
  • Macrophage scavenger receptor B

Describe the function of macrophage scavenger receptor A.

  • Binds to oxidised LDL
  • Binds to Gram-positive bacteria like Staphylococci & Streptococci
  • Binds to dead cells

Describe the function of macrophage scavenger receptor B.

  • Binds to oxidised LDL
  • Binds to malaria parasites
  • Binds to dead cells

Describe the difference between macrophage scavenger receptors A and B.

  • Both bind to oxidised LDL and dead cells
  • However, receptor A is more involved in the binding of gram-positive bacteria and receptor B is more involved in the binding of gram- negative bacteria

Physiological Responses To Arterial Ox-Ldl Deposits.

Describe three ways macrophages can modify native LDL.

  • NADPH Oxidase
  • Myeloperoxidase
  • Generation of H2O2

Describe how macrophages can become enlarged foam cells.

  • Macrophages accumulate modified LDLs to become enlarged foam cells
  • They phagocytose these modified lipoproteins and become foam cells

Describe the prognosis of these foam cells.

Macrophages that have ingested modified LDL typically die from the fat overload and they release pro-coagulant tissue factor as well as lipid debris into the necrotic spaces of a plaque

Foam Cells & Macrophages.

What is CD68?

CD68 is a transmembrane glycoprotein primarily expressed on the surface of macrophages, monocytes, and other phagocytic cells

What is a cholesterol crystal?

A cholesterol crystal is a rigid, needle-shaped, or plate-like structure that forms when cholesterol in tissues or bodily fluids becomes insoluble and crystallises

Describe the effect of monocyte recruitment in atherosclerosis.

  • Monocyte recruitment leads to increased foam cell formation → monocyte death
  • This can lead to the release of toxic molecules such as MMPs, ROS and other cytokines/chemokines, leading to a vicious cycle of inflammation

Describe how cytokines are involved in the inflammatory response of atherosclerosis.

  • Cytokines usually activate endothelial cell adhesion molecules
  • NOTE: Endothelial cell adhesion molecules such as ICAM-1, VCAM-1

can facilitate foam cell infiltration and monocyte activation Recall the main cytokine involved in the inflammatory response of atherosclerosis.

Interleukin-1 (IL-1)

Describe how IL-1 is involved in the inflammatory response of atherosclerosis.

IL-1 is involved in the formation of intracellular cholesterol crystals and NFkB stimulation — It also coordinates multiple processes including cell death and cell proliferation; and elevated CRP

Describe how we understand the effects of interleukin-1 (IL-1) in the pathophysiology of atherosclerosis.

Atherosclerosis is reduced in mice without IL-1 and humans with anti-IL-1 antibodies

Describe how chemokines are involved in the inflammatory response of atherosclerosis.

Chemokines are small proteins that are chemoattractant to monocytes in atherosclerosis

Describe how MCP-1 induces chemotaxis of monocytes.

MCP-1 binds to a monocyte G-protein coupled receptor CCR2 to induce chemotaxis

Recall the main chemokine involved in the inflammatory response of atherosclerosis.

Monocyte chemotactic protein-1 (MCP-1)

Describe how we understand the effects of monocyte chemotactic protein-1 (MCP-1) in the pathophysiology of atherosclerosis.

Atherosclerosis is reduced in MCP-1 or CCR2-deficient mice

Describe how a positive feedback loop is involved in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis.

  • As monocytes (macrophages) take up oxidised/modified forms of LDL, this leads to activation of these macrophages
  • The macrophages then die as they are unable to withstand the toxicity of the oxLDL forms
  • They then secrete cytokines, chemokines, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and reactive oxygen species (ROS)

which can oxidise more macrophages, leading to a vicious cycle of inflammation

NOTE: The molecules released by macrophages also leads to breakdown of the fibrous cap. This breakdown of fibrous cap can lead to thrombogenesis if the blood flowing through the artery comes into contact with the necrotic core of the atherosclerotic plaque

What is a vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC)?

A vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) is a specialised type of smooth muscle cell found in the walls of blood vessels, primarily within the tunica media layer of arteries and veins

Describe how macrophages interact with vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) in atherosclerosis.

  • There is what is known as a “wound healing” role of the macrophage in atherosclerosis
  • Macrophages release growth factors that recruit VSMCs and stimulate them to migrate, survive, proliferate and deposit extracellular matrix — i.e structurally strong collagen

What is platelet derived growth factor?

  • PDGF is a growth factor with critical roles in cell proliferation, migration, and tissue repair
  • Unregulated PDGF signalling contributes to diseases like atherosclerosis and fibrosis
  • NOTE: PDGF can be secreted by macrophages

Describe the importance of platelet-derived growth factor in atherosclerosis.

  • Vascular smooth muscle cell chemotaxis
  • Vascular smooth muscle cell survival
  • Vascular smooth muscle cell division (mitosis)
  • Overall, it contributes to the strengthening of the fibrous cap as it helps VSMCs contribute to it

What is transforming growth factor beta?

Transforming Growth Factor Beta (TGF-β) is a multifunctional cytokine that plays critical roles in regulating cell growth, differentiation, immune responses, and tissue remodelling

Describe the importance of transforming growth factor beta in atherosclerosis.

  • Increased collagen synthesis
  • Matrix deposition

Describe the difference between platelet-derived growth factor and transforming growth factor beta.

  • Platelet-derived growth factor is more involved in VSMC chemotaxis and proliferation
  • Transforming growth factor beta is more involved in increasing ECM deposition by VSMCs

Describe the difference between vascular smooth muscle cells in normal and atherosclerotic individuals.

  • In normal individuals, the VSMCs have greater contractile function and exhibit lesser deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM)
  • In atherosclerotic individuals, the VSMCs have lower contractile function and exhibit greater deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM) to contribute to the fibrous cap

Vascular Smooth Muscle Cell Transformation.

What is a metalloproteinase?

A metalloproteinase is an enzyme that belongs to a group of proteases (enzymes that degrade proteins) requiring a metal ion (usually zinc, Zn) in their active site to function

Describe how metalloproteinases are implicated in atherosclerosis.

  • Macrophages can express metalloproteinases
  • Metalloproteinases usually degrade the fibrous cap of an atherosclerotic plaque → plaque rupture
  • Blood coagulation at the site of rupture may then lead to an occlusive thrombus and cessation of blood flow

Describe the effect of plaque erosion/rupture in atherosclerosis.

Blood coagulation at the site of rupture may lead to an occlusive thrombus and cessation of blood flow
