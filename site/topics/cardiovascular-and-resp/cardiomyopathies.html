Overview

What is dilated cardiomyopathy?

Dilated cardiomyopathy is characterised by dilated chambers and thin walls with reduced contractility

What is the characteristic feature of dilated cardiomyopathy?

Dilated cardiomyopathy is characterised by dilated chambers and thin walls with reduced contractility

Describe the effects of the reduced contractility seen in dilated cardiomyopathy.

Dilation of the chambers leads to reduced contractility

Describe what we would expect to see on an echocardiogram for dilated cardiomyopathy.

Echocardiogram shows a dilated left ventricle with reduced systolic function (ejection fraction) and typically global hypokinesis

Which parts of the heart are affected by dilated cardiomyopathy?

Dilated cardiomyopathy often affects left and right ventricle especially if aetiology is alcohol

Dilated Cardiomyopathy.

What are the commonest causes of dilated cardiomyopathy?

  • Idiopathic
  • Genetic
  • Toxins (alcohol, cardiotoxic chemotherapy)
  • Pregnancy (peripartum cardiomyopathy)
  • Viral infections (myocarditis)
  • Tachycardia-related cardiomyopathy
  • Thyroid disease
  • Muscular dystrophies

Describe the management of dilated cardiomyopathy.

  • A sodium-glucose co-transporter reuptake inhibitor (SGLT-2 inhibitor) - such as dapagliflozin and empagliflozin — (first line since 2023 before any other medication even ACE as long as no contraindications like infection)
  • Medical heart failure therapy - ACE inhibitors, beta-blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) antagonists
  • Diuretics for fluid overload
  • Anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation
  • Cardiac devices – cardiac resynchronisation therapy and/or implantable cardioverter defibrillator/ or LVAD left ventricular assisted device while waiting for transplant

What is the risk of untreated dilated cardiomyopathy?

  • Risk of heart failure hospitalisation
  • Cardiac arrhythmias
  • Sudden cardiac death due to ventricular arrhythmia
  • Reduced survival

What are the clinical signs and symptoms of heart failure and how does it differ between left and right heart failure?

  • Right heart failure – peripheral oedema — e.g. leg swelling, raised jugular venous pressure
  • Left heart failure – pulmonary oedema

What is hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and what causes it?

  • Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is a genetic disorder characterised by left ventricular hypertrophy that is not solely explained by abnormal loading conditions
  • It is caused by a missense mutation in 1 of at least 10 genes that encode the proteins of the cardiac sarcomere

What type of disease is hypertrophic cardiomyopathy?

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is a genetic disorder thought to affect 1 in 500 people in the UK. Most people inherit the disease from their parents

Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy.

Describe the difference between obstructive and non-obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

  • In obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, the interventricular septum is often involved and it often leads to disrupted outflow of blood
  • However in non-obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, the outflow is often unaffected

Cardiomyopathy.

Describe the symptoms of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

Although the majority of patients are asymptomatic throughout life, some present with severe limiting symptoms of dyspnoea, angina, and syncope; some may even die suddenly from cardiac causes

Describe the pathophysiology of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and how it can lead to sudden cardiac death.

  • Genetic, storage diseases, neuromuscular or mitochondrial disorders, malformation syndromes →
  • Thickening and disarray of left ventricular myocardium- can occur in any region of the left ventricle →
  • Frequently involves the interventricular septum, which results in an obstruction of flow through the left ventricular (LV) outflow tract
  • Disorganised myocytes disrupt signal conduction
  • Ventricular arrhythmias → Sudden cardiac death

Recall a common sequelae of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

Sudden cardiac death

Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy Pathophysiology.

Describe the management of obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Medical therapy

  • Beta-blocker
  • Verapamil or Dilitiazem
  • Disopyramide or Cibenzoline Nonpharmacotherapy
  • Surgical therapy
  • PTSMA
  • Pacing therapy

Describe the management of non-obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. LVEF ≥50%

  • Beta-blocker
  • Verapamil or Dilitiazem
  • Disopyramide or Diuretics (low dose) LVEF ‹50% (dilated phase)
  • Beta-blocker
  • ACE-inhibitor or ARB
  • MRA
  • Diuretics If therapy resistant
  • Cardiac resynchronisation therapy
  • Ventricular assist devices
  • Heart transplantation

Management Of Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy (Hcm).

What is restrictive cardiomyopathy?

  • It is a less well-defined cardiomyopathy as its diagnosis is based on establishing the presence of a restrictive ventricular filling pattern
  • NOTE: RCM accounts for approximately 5% of all cases of diagnosed cardiomyopathies

Describe the causes of restrictive cardiomyopathy.

It may be idiopathic, familial (has been related to troponin I or desmin mutations, the latter often in association with a skeletal myopathy), or associated with various systemic disorders, such as haemochromatosis, amyloidosis, sarcoidosis, Fabry's disease, carcinoid syndrome, scleroderma, anthracycline toxicity, or previous radiation
