Overview

What is an electrocardiogram (ECG)?

An ECG (Electrocardiogram) is a medical test that records the electrical activity of the heart over a period of time

Describe what an ECG actually does.

  • An ECG works by measuring the electrical activity throughout the heart at different stages of the cardiac cycle — i.e measures the increase in electric charge of the cardiac myocytes
  • Throughout the cardiac cycle, electrical activity within the heart is variable
  • The ECG measures these changes in electrical activity and records them — i.e high electrical activity during atrial/ventricular contraction etc. — This is because the myocytes are becoming depolarised, as there is an influx of Na+ and Ca2+ which causes their charge to increase

What unit does an ECG use to measure the electrical activity of the heart?

(milli)Volts (mV)

Recall three ways an ECG is clinically significant.

  • An ECG can detect:
  • Conduction abnormalities — i.e tract abnormalities
  • Structural abnormalities — i.e ventricular hypertrophy → increased QRS voltage
  • Perfusion abnormalities

Recall three reasons why an ECG is clinically convenient.

  • Relatively cheap and easy to undertake
  • Reproducible between people and centres
  • Quick turnaround on results/report

Clinical Relevance Of The Ecg.

What are electrodes in the context of an ECG?

  • Electrodes are the interface between the cable and the person
  • Sometimes they are plastic, sometimes they are sticky

Electrodes.

What are cables in the context of an ECG?

Cables are the instruments we use to connect the patient to the ECG analyser

What are leads in the context of an ECG?

  • Leads are a term used to describe the particular view of the heart that we have from the positioning we obtain from the electrode
  • NOTE: Leads are not a physical thing, they simply describe the view of the heart that we have

Electrodes, Cables/Wires And Leads.

What is a vector?

A vector is a quantity that has both magnitude and direction

Describe the significance of the arrows on a vector.

  • The direction of the arrow represents the net direction of movement
  • The size of the arrow represents the magnitude of the vector

Describe the cardiac interpretation of vectors on an ECG.

  • Each of the leads on an ECG represent the direction and the size that the electrical activity is going in, usually from 12 different views (i.e 12-lead ECG)
  • Because the leads represent the electrodes which are all in different positions, the lead gives us the information on what direction the force being produced is going in
  • The size/amplitude of the wave on the ECG gives us information on the magnitude of the force being produced

Describe the significance of having different leads which represent different positions on an ECG.

  • Having leads which represent different positions means that each lead would read differently depending on which lead you are looking at
  • For example, some leads may or may not show a P wave, depending on the location of the electrode attached to that lead

Describe the difference between atrial depolarisation and atrial contraction.

  • Atrial depolarisation is the act of the cells within the atria (atrial myocytes) altering in electrical charge due to the conduction of electrical signal which has reached them
  • Atrial contraction refers to the shortening in length of these myocytes which allows them to eject blood into their adjacent ventricles
  • However this contraction is only possible once the myocyte has been depolarised, leading to voltage-gated Na+ → Ca2+ channel opening
  • Atrial depolarisation causes atrial contraction
  • NOTE: It is just important to understand that the ECG is measuring the electrical events that occur within the heart. Electrical events (depolarisation) trigger the mechanical events (contraction)

Describe how Ca2+ interacts with actin to allow muscle contraction.

  • Ca2+ binds onto and moves the troponin complex present on actin
  • This binding of Ca2+ and movement of troponin causes the tropomyosin to move away from actin which is normally used to bind myosin
  • Myosin can then bind to actin and move it towards the M line, enabling contraction

What causes the P wave seen on ECGs?

The P wave on an ECG represents the depolarisation of the atria — Depolarisation of atria → Atrial systole

What causes the QRS complex normally seen on an ECG?

  • The QRS complex on an ECG represents the depolarisation of the ventricles — Depolarisation of ventricles → Ventricular systole
  • NOTE: This wave is large because there is a lot of electrical activity going on within the heart here

What causes the T wave normally seen on an ECG?

The T wave on an ECG represents the repolarisation of the ventricles

when they are resting after systole

NOTE: This wave is smaller because the electrical changes that occur during the T wave are much smaller than your typical QRS contraction. It produces a wave because there is still electrical activity occurring within the heart, albeit not of a contractile manner

Why is the repolarisation of the atria not represented by a distinct marker on an ECG?

  • Atrial repolarisation occurs almost simultaneously with ventricular depolarisation (represented by the QRS complex )
  • This means there isn’t a distinct wave that represents atrial repolarisation, similar to the T wave for ventricular repolarisation

Recall the three stages of the cardiac cycle as seen on an ECG.

  • Atrial systole
  • Ventricular systole
  • Diastole

Describe the significance of each stage of the ECG mapped with the cardiac cycle.

  • P wave - Atrial systole
  • QRS complex - Ventricular systole
  • T wave - Ventricular repolarisation/diastole

What is the sinoatrial (SA) node?

  • The sinoatrial node is the pacemaker of the heart, responsible for generating the signal and rhythm used to contract
  • It spontaneously depolarises at a given rate to determine heart rate

Describe step-by-step what is occurring as the cardiac cycle progresses onwards and map each stage onto a point on the ECG.

  • Sinoatrial node (SAN) spontaneously depolarises at a given rate, leading to conduction of the signal to the atria → atrial depolarisation — P wave
  • Atrioventricular node (AVN) then depolarises as the signal reaches it from the SAN. This slows down the conduction of signal from the atria to the ventricles, preventing ventricular contraction before they have even filled with blood — P to Q interval
  • The ventricular septum (Bundle branches) then depolarises and contracts after the Bundle of His. This provides a good “anchor” for the rest of the myocardium to contract around — Q section
  • The entire ventricle (Purkinje fibres) then depolarises and contracts. This obviously means there is a lot of electrical activity — R wave
  • The top of the heart then contracts and depolarises (after the bottom; heart contracts from bottom → top) — S wave
  • The ventricles then repolarise — T wave

Heart Structure.

Recall the most commonly used single lead for an ECG.

Lead II

In which direction does electrical activity move throughout the heart?

Left and inferior

Why is Lead II most commonly used?

  • In the majority of people, Lead II provides the biggest deflections on an ECG
  • This is due to its orientation, which allows the left and inferior nature of electrical movement throughout the heart to be reflected well
  • This is because the negative electrode of Lead II is at the Right Arm and the positive electrode of Lead II is at the Left Leg

What is Einthoven’s triangle?

Einthoven’s triangle is an anatomical triangle used to mark out and remember the locations of the negative and positive electrodes on Leads I, II and III on an ECG

Where is Einthoven’s triangle drawn from and to?

Right arm → Left arm → Left leg EINTHOVEN’S TRIANGLE.

What plane do Leads I, II and III measure electrical activity of the heart in?

Coronal plane — i.e from top to bottom, side-to-side

What type of leads are Leads I, II and III?

They are bipolar leads

Why are Leads I, II and III described as bipolar leads?

  • This is because these three leads require the placement of two electrodes at distinct positions on the body, and the reading is determined by whether the net movement of electrical activity is towards the negative or positive electrode
  • Other leads typically rely on the placement of one lead on the body

Describe the origin and insertion of Lead I.

Lead I - Right Arm → L eft Arm

Describe the origin and insertion of Lead II.

Lead II - Right Arm → L eft L eg

Describe the origin and insertion of Lead III.

Lead III - L eft Arm → L eft L eg

What is the rule of L’s in reference to electrode placement on an ECG?

  • The rule of L’s refers to a method we can use to remember which leads are positioned in which part of the body
  • Lead I - Right Arm → L eft Arm — One L - Lead One
  • Lead II - Right Arm → L eft L eg — Two Ls - Lead Two
  • Lead III - L eft Arm → L eft L eg — Three Ls - Lead Three

What is the rule of reading in reference to electrode placement on an ECG?

  • It helps us to understand the positioning of the negative and positive electrodes on an ECG
  • The negative electrodes are typically located on the left in relation to their positive electrodes which are typically located on the right
  • NOTE: We read words from left to right, same with charge

What plane do Leads V1, V2, V3, V4, V5 and V6 measure electrical activity of the heart in?

Transverse plane — i.e from front-to-back

Recall the order in which the V electrodes are placed in.

V1 → V2 → V4 → V3 → V5 → V6

Recall the placement of the V1 electrode.

Right sternal border in the 4th intercostal space

Recall the placement of the V2 electrode.

Left sternal border in the 4th intercostal space

Recall the placement of the V3 electrode.

Halfway between V2 and V4

Recall the placement of the V4 electrode.

Mid-clavicular line in the 5th intercostal space

Recall the placement of the V5 electrode.

Anterior axillary line at the level of V4

Recall the placement of the V6 electrode.

Mid-axillary line at the level of V4

Describe how the V electrodes work to measure electrical activity.

  • The V electrodes work by measuring electrical activity relative to Wilson’s Central Terminal (i.e centre of the chest) — Wilson’s Central Terminal = ⅓(RA+LA+LL)
  • Electrical activity that moves closer to the electrode than the central terminal produces a positive deflection and vice-versa — Activity moving toward a V electrode causes a positive (upward) deflection on the ECG — Activity moving away from a V electrode causes a negative (downward) deflection
  • The closer the electrical activity is to the V electrode, the larger the ECG deflection will be
  • This allows for measurement of heart activity in the three- dimensional plane

What is the formula for calculating Wilson’s Central Terminal?

Wilson’s Central Terminal = ⅓(RA+LA+LL)

Why do we need to measure heart electrical activity in the transverse plane?

  • The V leads help us to focus on specific regions of the heart based on their locations: — V1 and V2 : Provide information about the right ventricle and the interventricular septum (SEPTAL) — V3 and V4 : Focus on the anterior wall of the left ventricle (ANTERIOR) — V5 and V6 : Highlight the lateral wall of the left ventricle (LATERAL)
  • This specificity is critical for identifying localised abnormalities in different parts of the heart

Describe how the aVR, aVL and aVF leads work respectively.

  • These augmented leads work similarly to the bipolar leads, however one of the leads is not actually attached to the body, and is an average of two other leads
  • For example, a aV lead may insert at the left arm, but instead of originating from the left leg like Lead III, it could choose the halfway/“average” point between the right arm and left leg as its origin point (-ve electrode)

Describe how the aVR lead originates and inserts.

aVR - Halfway between left arm and left leg → Right arm — R = Right

Describe how the aVL lead originates and inserts.

aVL - Halfway between right arm and left leg → Left arm — L = Left

Describe how the aVF lead originates and inserts.

  • aVF - Halfway between right arm and left arm → Left leg — F = “Foot” ( NOTE: This is just to help remember) In the aV leads, is the negative electrode at the halfway point between two limbs, or the actual limb itself?
  • All aV leads have their negative electrodes at the halfway point between two limbs, and the actual left arm/left leg/ right arm is the positive electrode

The Twelve-Lead Electrocardiogram (Ecg).

What is a normal heart rate?

60-100 bpm

What is the cardiac axis?

  • The cardiac axis refers to the overall direction of the heart's electrical activity during depolarisation in the frontal plane on an ECG
  • It typically refers to the QRS axis, which is the overall direction of the heart’s electrical activity during ventricular depolarisation

What is a physiological QRS axis?

-30º to 90º

Describe the clinical significance of the cardiac axis.

  • The cardiac axis provides valuable information about the heart’s electrical conduction system and its anatomical orientation
  • Abnormal deviations in the axis can indicate underlying cardiac or systemic conditions, such as: — Hypertrophy — Conduction blocks — Myocardial infarction — Pulmonary disease

Describe how the cardiac axis can be calculated.

  • We have to obtain two leads that are perpendicular to each other — i.e Lead II and aVL, Lead I and aVF
  • We obtain the net vectors using the QRS complexes on both ECGs
  • Using trigonometry, we can create a triangle and using tan we can calculate the inner angle of the triangle, which gives us the cardiac axis
  • A normal QRS axis is -30º to 90º

Recall five components of an ECG that are clinically important.

  • Rate & rhythm
  • P-wave, P-R interval and P:QRS
  • QRS duration

What is the role of a defibrillator?

  • A defibrillator is a medical device designed to deliver a controlled electric shock to the heart
  • Its primary purpose is to restore a normal heart rhythm in cases of life-threatening arrhythmias, such as ventricular fibrillation (VF) or pulseless ventricular tachycardia (VT)
  • NOTE: A defibrillator removes irregular rhythm of the heart and stabilises it. It cannot solve problems such as asystole, but it can help in tachycardia

What is sinus rhythm?

Sinus rhythm is the rhythm of a healthy heart, which originates from the sinoatrial node (SAN)

Describe the findings we would expect to see on a physiological (sinus rhythm) ECG.

  • Each P-wave is followed by a QRS wave (1:1)
  • Rate is regular (even R-R intervals) and normal (83 bpm)
  • Otherwise unremarkable

Sinus Rhythm.

Describe the findings we would expect to see on a bradycardic ECG.

  • Each P-wave is followed by a QRS wave (1:1)
  • Rate is regular (even R-R intervals) and slow (56 bpm)

What can cause sinus bradycardia?

  • Medication or vagal stimulation
  • Drugs → Increased PNS activity
  • Disease → Decreased SNS activity

Sinus Bradycardia.

Describe the findings we would expect to see on a tachycardic ECG.

  • Each P-wave is followed by a QRS wave (1:1)
  • Rate is regular (even R-R intervals) and fast (107 bpm)

What often cause sinus tachycardia?

  • Tachycardia is often a physiological response (i.e. secondary) — i.e exercise
  • Drugs → Decreased PNS activity
  • Disease → Increased SNS activity

Sinus Tachycardia.

Describe the findings we would expect to see on a sinus arrhythmia ECG.

  • Each P-wave is followed by a QRS wave
  • Rate is irregular (variable R-R intervals) and normal-ish (65-100 bpm)
  • R-R interval varies with breathing cycle

What often cause sinus arrhythmia?

  • Sinus arrhythmia is often physiological and it represents variation within the breathing cycle
  • As we breathe in, there is deactivation of the PNS temporarily, which is removed once we exhale
  • This leads to the heart rate speeding up when we breathe in, but slowing down back to normal
  • The vagus nerve plays a role

Sinus Arrhythmia.

What is atrial fibrillation?

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is a common, irregular heart rhythm that causes the atria of the heart to quiver or twitch

Describe the findings we would expect to see on a atrial fibrillation ECG.

  • Oscillating baseline – atria contracting asynchronously
  • Rhythm can be irregular and rate may be slow
  • Turbulent flow pattern increases clot risk
  • Atria not essential for cardiac cycle

Which arrhythmia is characterised by the “saw-tooth pattern”?

Atrial flutter

Recall the systemic risk that atrial fibrillation poses.

Stroke

Atrial Fibrillation.

What is atrial flutter?

Atrial flutter is a type of abnormal heart rhythm characterised by a rapid and regular electrical activity in the atria

Describe the findings we would expect to see on a atrial flutter ECG.

  • Regular saw-tooth pattern in baseline (II, III, aVF)
  • Atrial to ventricular beats at a 2:1 ratio, 3:1 ratio or higher
  • Saw-tooth not always visible in all leads Is saw-tooth pattern always observed in atrial flutter?
  • Saw-tooth pattern is characteristic of atrial flutter, but is NOT

Atrial Flutter - Saw Tooth Pattern.

Describe the difference between atrial fibrillation and atrial flutter.

  • Atrial flutter is a more organised arrhythmia, with a predictable sawtooth ECG pattern and a regular rhythm — It is often easier to treat with cardioversion or ablation
  • Atrial fibrillation is a chaotic and irregular arrhythmia, associated with greater variability and a higher risk of complications like stroke

Recall one treatment option for patients with conduction blocks. What is first degree heart block?

First-degree heart block is a type of cardiac conduction abnormality

where there is a prolonged delay in the conduction of electrical impulses from the atria → ventricles

(see lecture diagram / notes)

Describe the findings we would expect to see on a first-degree conduction block ECG.

  • Persistent prolongation of PR segment/interval caused by slower AV conduction
  • Regular rhythm : 1:1 ratio of P-waves to QRS complexes

Describe what causes a first-degree conduction block.

First degree heart block is often a progressive disease of ageing — It is the most benign heart block

What is second degree heart block?

Second-degree heart block is a type of atrioventricular (AV) block

where some electrical impulses from the atria fail to conduct to the ventricles, leading to intermittent beats Recall the two types of second degree heart block.

  • Mobitz I
  • Mobitz II

Describe the findings we would expect to see on a second-degree conduction block (Mobitz I) ECG.

  • Progressive prolongation of the PR interval until beat skipped — i.e prolongation continually gets longer
  • Most P-waves followed by QRS; but some P-waves are not
  • Regularly irregular
  • Also called Wenckebach

Describe what causes a second-degree conduction block.

Second degree heart block is often caused by a diseased AV node

Second-Degree Heart Block (Mobitz I).

Describe the difference between a Mobitz I and a Mobitz II conduction block.

  • A Mobitz I conduction block is characterised by the progressive prolongation of the P-R interval before the lack of QRS complex
  • A Mobitz II conduction block is characterised by the lack of QRS complex in a lot of heartbeats, however there is no progressive prolongation of P-R interval

Describe the findings we would expect to see on a second-degree conduction block (Mobitz II) ECG.

  • P-waves are regular, but only some are followed by QRS — i.e non-conducted P waves
  • No P-R prolongation
  • Regularly irregular : successes to failures (e.g. 2:1) or random

Second-Degree Heart Block (Mobitz Ii).

What is third degree heart block?

Third-degree heart block, also known as complete heart block, is the most severe form of atrioventricular (AV) block that occurs when there is complete failure of conduction of electrical impulses from the atria to the ventricles — No communication from atria → ventricles

Describe the findings we would expect to see on a third-degree conduction block ECG.

  • P-waves are regular, QRS are regular, but no relationship
  • P waves can be hidden within bigger vectors
  • A truly non-sinus rhythm – back-up pacemaker in action — i.e there is no coordination of activity between atria and ventricles

What is ventricular tachycardia?

This is when the ventricles beat too fast

Describe the findings we would expect to see on a ventricular tachycardia ECG.

  • P-waves hidden – dissociated atrial rhythm
  • Rate is regular and fast (100-200 bpm)

Describe the great risk of ventricular tachycardia.

There is a high risk of deteriorating into fibrillation (cardiac arrest)

How can ventricular tachycardia be resolved?

Ventricular tachycardia is a shockable rhythm – defibrillators are widely available to treat it
