Overview

What is haemostasis?

Haemostasis is the cellular and biochemical processes that enables both the specific and regulated cessation of bleeding in response to vascular insult

Recall two reasons why haemostasis is important.

  • To prevent blood loss from normal blood vessels
  • To prevent blood leaking from injured blood vessels
  • To enable tissue repair

Describe the vague process that occurs in a blood vessel in response to endothelial cell injury.

  • Vasoconstriction occurs
  • Formation of unstable platelet plug in blood vessel to prevent blood loss — assisted by von-Willebrand factor
  • Stabilisation of the plug with fibrin occurs - coagulation
  • Dissolution of blood clot occurs alongside vessel repair in fibrolysis

Mechanism Of Haemostasis.

Recall three reasons why we need to understand haemostatic mechanisms.

  • Diagnose and treat bleeding disorders
  • Control bleeding in individuals who do not have an underlying bleeding disorder
  • Identify risk factors for thrombosis
  • Treat thrombotic disorders
  • Monitor the drugs that are used to treat bleeding and thrombotic disorders

How is equilibrium an important aspect of haemostasis?

  • There needs to be a balance between coagulation factors/platelets and fibrinolytic factors/anticoagulant proteins
  • This is because they both need to be in the right abundance to prevent excessive bleeding or thrombosis

Equilibrium In Haemostasis.

Recall two ways the haemostatic balance can be tipped towards bleeding.

  • Lack of a specific factor — Failure of production (congenital + acquired) — Increased consumption/clearance
  • Defective function of a specific factor — Genetic — Acquired: drugs, synthetic defect, inhibition

Loss Of Equilibrium In Haemostasis.

What is primary haemostasis?

This is the process through which platelets aggregate together at the site of injury — “Unstable platelet plug”

What is the purpose of an unstable platelet plug?

The unstable platelet plug forms quickly at the site of vascular injury to provide an immediate but temporary barrier against blood loss

What is secondary haemostasis?

This is when stabilisation of the plug with fibrin occurs -

Coagulation

What component of platelets allows them to bind to collagen in blood vessels and where is it located?

  • Glycoproteins
  • They are located in the plasma membrane

What are the two types of platelet glycoproteins used to bind collagen?

  • Glp1a - alpha
  • Glp1b - beta

How does the action of these two glycoproteins differ?

  • Glp1a allows the platelet to bind directly to collagen
  • Glp1b requires the use of a von Willebrand factor to bind to collagen

What is von-Willebrand factor?

von-Willebrand factor (vWF) is a large glycoprotein that plays a huge role in the adhesion of platelets to exposed collagen within the damaged endothelial wall

Describe how von-Willebrand factor performs its function.

von-Willebrand factor (vWF) performs its function by adhering onto the Glp1b receptor on the platelet surface, and attaching itself to exposed collagen on the damaged endothelial wall

Where is von-Willebrand factor found and produced?

  • It is found and produced in endothelial cells
  • Hence, when there is damage to the endothelial layer, von- Willebrand factor uncoils and allows binding of the GlpIb receptor to it

Platelet Adhesion Using Von-Willebrand Factor.

How does the binding of platelets to collagen encourage platelet- platelet interaction?

  • It allows the platelet to conform to a more rounded shape
  • This allows it to interact with other platelets better.

What does the adhesion of platelets to collagen lead to?

Activation of platelets which results from the release of their storage granule content.

Platelet Response To Adhesion.

What are the two types of granule released by platelets after adhesion?

  • Alpha granules
  • Dense granules
  • There are important molecules stored within the granules of these platelets

What is the open canalicular system?

This is the system used by platelets to release the contents of the granules they need to release after adhesion — The platelet membrane is invaginated to form this system

Recall the most important dense granule that is released by platelets following adhesion to collagen.

ADP

Which other molecule is produced following platelet adhesion to the endothelial membrane?

Prostaglandins - specifically thromboxane A2

How is thromboxane A2 produced?

  • It is produced by platelets using arachidonic acid
  • NOTE: Arachidonic acid is a component of cell membranes

What effect do these molecules have on platelets?

  • They have positive feedback effects which allows them to increase platelet recruitment, aggregation and activation
  • This forms the platelet plug

How do ADP and thromboxane have this effect?

  • ADP binds to the p2y12 receptor
  • Thromboxane A2 binds to the thromboxane A2 receptor

What occurs as a result of this platelet activation by ADP and thromboxane A2?

Platelet activation leads to a change in the conformation of the GlpIIb/IIIa (2b/3a) receptor on platelets from its inactive state → active state — This process is known as “inside out” or “flip-flopping”

What is GlpIIb/IIIa?

GpIIb/IIIa (Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa) is an integrin receptor found on the surface of platelets that is integral to the process of platelet aggregation and clot formation during haemostasis

What does the GlpIIb/IIIa receptor do?

The GlpIIb/IIIa receptor provides binding sites for ligands such as fibrinogen and von-Willebrand factor (vWF)

How does fibrinogen then act to progress primary haemostasis?

It binds to the GlpIIb/IIIb receptor and this leads to “outside-in” signalling which allows platelets to link together and stabilise the platelet plug

What is another role of thromboxane A2, other than activating platelets?

It acts as a vasoconstrictor, and this promotes the formation of platelets in the initial response to blood vessel injury.

Describe the process of primary haemostasis.

  • Platelets arrive at the site of vascular injury
  • Platelets adhere to the endothelial wall either directly or via vWF
  • This platelet adhesion → platelet aggregation using a positive feedback mechanism
  • An unstable platelet plug has now formed and this provides the platform for its stabilisation and hence coagulation

Describe the importance of platelets in haemostasis.

  • Platelets are integral to the formation of the initial unstable platelet plug in response to vascular insult — This plug provides an immediate but temporary barrier against blood loss
  • They hence also provide the platform upon which the stable clot can be built

What is thrombocytopenia?

Thrombocytopenia is the medical term for a significantly low number of platelets within the blood

Describe the three main mechanisms behind thrombocytopenia.

  • Bone marrow failure — i.e insufficient production — NOTE: Platelets are produced within the bone marrow
  • Accelerated clearance
  • Pooling and destruction in an enlarged spleen

Recall two disorders of bone marrow failure that lead to thrombocytopenia.

  • Leukaemia — i.e via “overcrowding”, increased load on the bone marrow
  • Vitamin B12 deficiency — B12 is essential for DNA synthesis. Deficiency leads to reduced megakaryocyte production and hence reduced thrombocyte production

Blood Cell Hierarchy.

Recall two disorders of accelerated platelet clearance that lead to thrombocytopenia.

  • Auto-immune thrombocytopenic purpura (auto-ITP) — i.e destruction of platelets
  • Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)

What is auto-immune thrombocytopenic purpura (auto-ITP)?

  • Auto-immune thrombocytopenic purpura (auto-ITP) is an autoimmune disorder in which the immune system mistakenly targets platelets
  • NOTE: ITP is a very common cause of thrombocytopenia

Describe what happens in auto-immune thrombocytopenic purpura (auto-ITP).

Antiplatelet autoantibodies are produced by the body in response to the production or presence of platelets within the blood

When a autoantibody binds to a platelet, the platelet becomes sensitised and the macrophages come in and clear the platelet

Over time, this depletes the presence of platelets within the blood → thrombocytopenia

Describe the effects of immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP).

  • Bleeding and bruising — i.e purpura
  • Splenomegaly
  • Fatigue and weakness

What are purpura?

Purpura refers to purple or red spots and patches that appear on the skin or mucous membranes due to small blood vessels under the skin breaking and leaking blood

What is Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)?

A process in which there is generalised and uncontrolled activation of coagulation followed by marked activation of the fibrinolytic system.

Describe the pathophysiology of Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC).

This activation of coagulation results from the expression of TF within the circulation

How does Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) lead to thrombocytopenia?

Because platelets are required to form blood clots, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) reduces the number of available platelets within the blood → thrombocytopenia

What is prostacyclin?

It is a prostaglandin that is used as a vasodilator in response to the dissolution of blood clots, inhibiting platelet aggregation — PGI2 is used in endothelial cells

How is prostacyclin produced?

  • By endothelial cells from arachidonic acid
  • This makes sense as endothelial cells will be present when the blood vessel isn’t damaged and conditions need to return back to normal.

What is the difference between a prostacyclin and a prostaglandin?

A prostacyclin is a type of prostaglandin that has vasodilatory effects — i.e thromboxane A2 is also a type of prostaglandin, but this has vasoconstrictory effects

Recall the two steps involved in thromboxane A2 synthesis from arachadonic acid.

  • Arachadonic acid → Cyclic endoperoxides — Catalysed by cyclo-oxygenase
  • Cyclic endoperoxides → Thromboxane A2 — Catalysed by thromboxane synthetase

Recall the two steps involved in prostacyclin synthesis from arachadonic acid.

  • Arachadonic acid → Cyclic endoperoxides — Catalysed by cyclo-oxygenase
  • Cyclic endoperoxides → Prostacyclin — Catalysed by prostacyclin synthetase

What are aspirin and clopidogrel?

They are anti-platelet drugs that are used to reduce the risk of heart attack and strokes for those with cardiovascular disease.

How does aspirin work?

It irreversibly blocks the action of Cyclo-Oxygenase (COx)

which is a vital component in the production of thromboxane A2

This means that thromoboxane A2 cannot bind to the thromboxane A2 receptor and cannot activate platelets as it normally does, reducing the formation of blood clots

Why does aspirin not inhibit the action of prostacyclin, since it also needs COx for its synthesis?

  • Endothelial cells are able to produce COx on their own (due to them having nuclei), meaning that they are not as affected by the action of aspirin
  • Overall there is hence a net decrease in platelet aggregation and the formation of blood clots.
  • NOTE: Platelet aggregation is inhibited directly via downregulation of thromboxane pathway and also inhibited via “non-inhibition” of prostacyclin pathway

How does clopidogrel work?

  • It works by preventing ADP from binding to its p2y12 receptor — NOTE: p2y12 is on platelet membranes
  • Similarly, this prevents the activation of platelets and the positive feedback loop is not achieved to recruit more and more platelets to the site of vessel damage

Antiplatelet Therapy.

What is von Willebrand disease?

  • This is a condition in which von Willebrand factor (vWF) is either reduced or defective
  • It leads to dysfunctional primary haemostasis

Recall two causes of von Willebrand disease.

  • Hereditary decrease of quantity/function (common)
  • Acquired due to antibody (rare)

Recall the two functions of von Willebrand factor.

  • To anchor platelets to exposed collagen within the endothelial wall
  • Stabilising Factor VIII (Eight), protecting it from damage — hence if vWf is low, Factor VIII may also be low

Describe the inheritance pattern of von Willebrand disease (VWD).

Von Willebrand disease (VWD) has an autosomal inheritance pattern

Recall the three types of von Willebrand disease.

  • Type 1 von Willebrand disease
  • Type 2 von Willebrand disease
  • Type 3 von Willebrand disease

Describe the two broad categories of von Willebrand disease and which Type falls into each.

  • Deficiency of von Willebrand factor - Type 1 and Type 3
  • Abnormal function of von Willebrand factor - Type 2

What is Type 1 von Willebrand disease?

This is the most common and mildest form of VWD where individuals have low levels of vWF but still retain some normal function

What is Type 2 von Willebrand disease?

In type 2 VWD, the vWF is present but defective, meaning it does not function properly

What is Type 3 von Willebrand disease?

This is the most severe form of VWD and involves virtually no detectable vWF in the bloodstream

Recall two hereditary disorders of the vessel wall.

  • Hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia
  • Ehlers-Danlos syndrome and other connective tissue disorders — i.e affecting collagen

What is hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia?

Hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia is a rare genetic disorder that affects the blood vessels, leading to the development of abnormal blood vessels that are prone to bleeding

What is Ehlers-Danlos syndrome?

Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS) is a group of genetic connective tissue disorders characterised by hyperflexibility of the joints, skin that is easily bruised and hyper-elastic, and abnormally fragile blood vessels and tissues

Recall two acquired causes of vessel wall disorder.

  • Steroid therapy — i.e via atrophy of collagen
  • Ageing (‘senile’ purpura)
  • Vasculitis
  • Scurvy (Vitamin C deficiency)

Describe four clinical features of primary haemostasis disorders.

  • Immediate bleeding
  • Prolonged bleeding from cuts
  • Nose bleeds (epistaxis):prolonged › 20 mins
  • Gum bleeding: prolonged
  • Heavy menstrual bleeding (menorrhagia)
  • Bruising (ecchymosis), may be spontaneous/easy
  • Prolonged bleeding after trauma or surgery

What are petechiae?

  • Petechiae are small, round, pinpoint spots that appear on the skin or mucous membranes due to bleeding underneath the skin
  • The spots are often red, purple, or brown in colour and are caused by broken blood vessels (capillaries) near the skin's surface
  • NOTE: Petechiae and purpura have similar causes, but different physical manifestations

In which disorders are petechiae observed?

Thrombocytopenia

In which disorders are purpura observed?

  • Platelet (thrombocytopenic purpura)
  • Vascular disorders

Purpura Vs Petechiae.

What is severe von Willebrand disease like?

  • Severe von-Willebrand disease leads to haemophilia-like bleeding due to low Factor VIII levels
  • NOTE: vWF is used to protect and stabilise Factor VIII

Ecchymosis.

Describe three tests we can do to test for primary haemostasis.

  • Platelet count, platelet morphology
  • Bleeding time (PFA100 in lab)
  • Assays of von Willebrand Factor (vWF)
  • Clinical observation
  • NOTE: Coagulation screen (PT, APTT) is normal (except more severe VWD cases where FVIII is low)

Platelet Count And Severity Of Disorder.

Describe how we can treat primary haemostasis that stems from failure of production/function.

  • Replace missing factor/platelets (i.e VWF containing concentrates) — Prophylactic OR — Therapeutic
  • Stop drugs — i.e aspirin/NSAIDs

Describe how we can treat primary haemostasis that stems from immune destruction.

  • Immunosuppression — i.e prednisolone
  • Splenectomy for ITP

Describe how we can treat primary haemostasis that stems from increased consumption.

  • Treat cause
  • Replace as necessary

Recall three other treatments for primary haemostasis.

  • Desmopressin — vasopressin analogue
  • Tranexamic acid — antifibrinolytic
  • Fibrin glue/spray
  • Other approaches — i.e hormonal

Describe how desmopressin works as a treatment for haemostatic disorders.

  • Desmopressin is a vasopressin analogue
  • There is a 2-5x increase in von-Willebrand factor (vWF) levels

when desmopressin is given however it works by releasing endogenous stores

This means it can only be effective in patients with mild haemostatic disorders

What is tranexamic acid?

  • An antifibrinolytic drug used to prevent fibrinolysis
  • It is a synthetic derivative of lysine

How does tranexamic acid work?

It works by binding to plasminogen in competitive inhibition

which prevents fibrin from binding to it and plasmin being generated

  • This works because it is a derivative of lysine and so it “tricks” the plasminogen into binding onto it instead of fibrin which has lysine on it
  • No generation of plasmin occurs leading to no fibrinolysis

Recall another term for secondary haemostasis.

Coagulation

Describe the role of secondary haemostasis.

  • The role of secondary haemostasis is the generation of thrombin (Factor IIa), which will convert fibrinogen → fibrin
  • NOTE: Deficiency of any coagulation factor results in a failure of thrombin generation and hence fibrin formation

Recall the three phases of coagulation.

  • Initiation
  • Amplification
  • Propagation

Phases Of Coagulation.

Describe how the balance can be tipped towards bleeding in secondary haemostasis.

The balance can be tipped towards bleeding via a reduction in coagulant factors or an increase in fibrinolytic/anticoagulant proteins

Recall three ways the balance can be tipped towards bleeding in coagulation.

  • Coagulation factor deficiency
  • Dilution
  • Increased consumption

Recall a hereditary deficiency of coagulation factor production.

Haemophilia A/B

What is Haemophilia A?

Haemophilia A is a genetic bleeding disorder caused by a deficiency or dysfunction of Factor VIII

What is Haemophilia B?

Haemophilia A is a genetic bleeding disorder caused by a deficiency or dysfunction of Factor IX

Recall two acquired deficiencies of coagulation factor production.

  • Liver disease — liver is involved in synthesis of some coagulant factors
  • Anticoagulant drugs — Warfarin — Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs)

Recall an acquired cause of coagulation disorder via dilution.

Blood transfusion — i.e insufficient plasma given in relation to red cells transfused

Recall an acquired cause of coagulation disorder via increased consumption.

  • Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) (common)
  • Immune – autoantibodies (rare)

Recall two hereditary coagulation disorders.

  • Haemophilia A (Factor VIII deficiency)
  • Haemophilia B (Factor IX deficiency) — sex linked
  • NOTE: Other coagulation disorders are very rare

Describe what generally occurs in coagulation.

  • In coagulation, the unstable platelet plug is stabilised by fibrin via a cascade of reactions
  • This leads to the protection against excessive bleeding going from temporary to long-term

Describe the effects of haemophilia on coagulation.

  • Due to the lack of Factor VIII/IX in Haemophilia A/B, there is a failure to generate fibrin which means that the platelet plug generated in primary haemostasis is not stabilised
  • This leads to excessive bleeding which is a hallmark of haemophilia

Platelet Plug In Haemophilia.

Recall a hallmark of haemophilia.

  • Haemarthrosis — Bleeding in haemophilia occurs largely in the joints
  • NOTE: In developed countries where there is access to prophylactic factor replacement therapy, joints are not implicated

What is haemarthrosis?

  • Haemarthrosis is bleeding into a joint, causing pain and swelling
  • It can occur due to an injury or as a complication of haemophilia

Describe the sequelae of haemarthrosis.

  • As blood leaks from damaged arteries into the synovial space, components from the blood such as iron can cause inflammation of the synovial membrane — This can lead to chronic synovitis and joint destruction
  • Chronic muscle wasting is also a common complication of haemarthrosis as the muscles are used less frequently

Blood Supply Of The Tibiofemoral Joint.

What should be avoided in patients with haemophilia and why?

  • Intramuscular (IM) injections
  • This is because the blood vessels supplying the muscle may be damaged, which could lead to the formation of extensive haematomas

Describe the effects of Factor VIII and IX deficiency.

  • In Factor VIII and IX deficiency, the bleeding is severe but compatible with life
  • There is also spontaneous joint and muscle bleeding

Describe the effects of Factor II deficiency.

Factor II (prothrombin) deficiency is lethal and incompatible with life

Describe the effects of Factor XI deficiency.

Factor XI deficiency generally leads to bleeding after trauma, however it is not as severe as Factor VIII/IX deficiency and it is not spontaneous

Describe the effects of Factor XII deficiency.

In Factor XII deficiency, there is no bleeding at all

Describe three acquired causes of coagulation disorders.

  • Liver failure – decreased production — Most coagulation factors are synthesised in the liver
  • Anticoagulant drugs
  • Dilution — Red cell transfusions no longer contain plasma — Major haemorrhage requires transfusion of plasma as well as red cells and platelets to avoid dilution effect (i.e too much blood, not enough clotting factors)

Describe three characteristics of coagulation disorders.

  • Generalised activation of coagulation (via Tissue factor) — i.e Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation
  • Associated with sepsis, major tissue damage, inflammation
  • Consumes and depletes coagulation factors
  • Platelets consumed - thrombocytopenia
  • Activation of fibrinolysis depletes fibrinogen – raised D-dimer — (a breakdown product of fibrin)
  • Deposition of fibrin in vessels causes organ failure

What is D-dimer?

  • D-dimer is a fibrin degradation product released during fibrinolysis
  • Elevated D-dimer levels indicate increased clot formation and breakdown but are non-specific and can occur in various conditions

Describe three clinical features of coagulation disorders.

  • Superficial cuts do not bleed — platelets are fine, unstable plug will suffice
  • Bruising is common, nosebleeds are rare
  • Spontaneous bleeding is deep, into muscles and joints
  • Bleeding after trauma may be delayed and is prolonged
  • Bleeding frequently restarts after stopping

Describe the clinical distinction between bleeding due to platelet (primary) and coagulation defects.

  • In platelet defects, there is superficial bleeding into skin, mucosal membranes but in coagulation defects there is bleeding into deep tissues, muscles, joints
  • In platelet defects, bleeding is immediate after injury but in coagulation defects bleeding is delayed, but there is severe bleeding after injury — Bleeding is delayed in 2º haemostasis because the unstable plug still forms
  • Bleeding is often also prolonged in coagulation disorders

Describe three tests for coagulation disorders.

  • Screening tests (‘clotting screen’) — Prothrombin time (PT) — Activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) — Full blood count (platelets)
  • Coagulation factor assays (for Factor VIII etc)
  • Tests for inhibitors — i.e antithrombin, Protein C, Protein S, tPA

How is the prothrombin time used to measure the activity of factors?

  • It measures the speed of the extrinsic pathway
  • It is a reaction that occurs in a tube to determine how fast these factors can form a blood clot and hence their levels of activity.

Describe the steps involved in the use of prothrombin time to measure factor activity.

  • Set up a test tube with your sample of blood and add sodium citrate
  • Spin the test tube so that sodium citrate and plasma mix to form platelet-poor-plasma
  • Add tissue factor and calcium to start the reaction. Start a timer
  • The time taken for the first fibrin clot to form is the prothrombin time

How is the activated partial thromboplastin time used to measure the activity of factors?

It measures the speed of the intrinsic pathway.

Which factors are only involved in the extrinsic pathway?

Factors VIIa

Which factors are only involved in the intrinsic pathway?

Factors XII, XI, VIII, IX

Which factors are involved in both the intrinsic and extrinsic pathways (common pathway)?

Factors II, V and X.

Which pathway is measured by the PT?

Extrinsic pathway

Which pathway is measured by the APTT?

Intrinsic pathway

Describe the steps involved in the use of activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) to measure factor activity.

  • Set up a test tube with your sample of blood
  • Add calcium and phospholipid
  • Add a contact activator of Factor XII and start a timer
  • The time taken for the first fibrin clot to form is the activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT)

Describe three ways we can replace missing coagulation factors.

  • Plasma (fresh frozen plasma FFP) — Contains all coagulation factors
  • Cryoprecipitate — Rich in Fibrinogen, FVIII, VWF, Factor XIII
  • Factor concentrates — Concentrates available for all factors except factor V. — Prothrombin complex concentrates (PCCs) Factors II, VII, IX, X
  • Recombinant forms of FVIII and FIX are available

Recall the components of cryoprecipitate.

  • Fibrinogen
  • FVIII
  • von Willebrand factor (vWF)
  • Factor XIII

Deep Vein Thrombosis (Dvt).

What is post-thrombotic syndrome?

  • Post-thrombotic syndrome refers to the long-term damage caused by the complications of deep vein thrombosis
  • This includes pain, swelling, skin changes, and venous ulcers

Recall two examples of arterial thrombosis.

  • Stroke
  • Myocardial infarction

What is meant by the haemostatic ‘end point’?

  • The haemostatic end point is the critical point in disease progression or trauma where the body’s coagulation system is exhausted or overwhelmed
  • It is often the cause of death in those who die from coagulative disorders

Describe three characteristics of thrombosis.

  • Intravascular coagulation
  • Inappropriate coagulation
  • Venous (or arterial)
  • Obstructs flow
  • May embolise to lungs

What is Virchow’s triad?

Virchow’s triad is a medical concept describing the three primary factors that contribute to the formation of a thrombus (blood clot) within the vasculature

Recall the three components of Virchow’s triad.

  • Abnormal vessel wall — i.e ruptured plaque → thrombosis
  • Abnormal blood — i.e increased clotting factor presence
  • Abnormal blood flow — i.e stenosed arteries, long haul flight (stasis), pregnancy (compression by the fetus)
  • “BBV”

Describe which component is dominant in venous thrombosis.

Abnormal blood

Describe which component is dominant in arterial thrombosis.

Abnormal vessel walls

What is thrombophilia?

Thrombophilia refers to a group of conditions in which the blood has an increased tendency to form clots

Describe three clinical features of thrombophilia.

  • Thrombosis at a young age — Physiologically, this should not really occur
  • Spontaneous thrombosis
  • Multiple thromboses
  • Thrombosis while anticoagulated

Describe how the homeostatic balance of coagulation can be tipped towards venous thrombosis.

  • A reduction in anticoagulant proteins
  • An increase in coagulant factors
  • Myeloproliferative disorders → increased platelet count

Recall two anticoagulant proteins.

  • Antithrombin
  • Protein C
  • Protein S

How does antithrombin work?

Antithrombin is a direct inhibitor of thrombin (IIa) and Factor Xa

Which molecule is used to potentiate the action of antithrombin?

  • Heparin
  • Antithrombin binds to endothelial cell associated heparins to cause potentiation of its effects

What are proteins C and S?

Indirect inhibitors of thrombin generation

Describe how proteins C and S work together as anticoagulants.

Protein C acts directly and protein S is its cofactor

Explain how the protein C pathway works to inhibit coagulation.

  • Thrombin binds to thrombomodulin (endothelial cell receptor that helps anticoagulation)
  • This activates Protein C
  • Protein C inactivates Factors Va and VIIIa in the presence of Protein S
  • This means Factor II cannot be converted to Factor IIa any longer and thrombin is no longer produced from prothrombin — This is because Factor Va and VIIIa help Factor Xa activate Factor II to Factor IIa
  • Clotting hence reduces as thrombin cannot do fibrinogen → fibrin

What is the direct role of protein C in the inhibition of coagulation?

To inactivate Factors Va and VIIIa which prevents the activation of Factor II (prothrombin) to Factor IIa (thrombin)

Recall the two mechanisms behind inherited thrombophilic traits.

  • Anticoagulant deficiency
  • Procoagulant excess

Which is generally more common, procoagulant excess or anticoagulant deficiency?

Procoagulant excess

Recall two disorders of anticoagulant deficiency.

  • Antithrombin deficiency
  • Protein C deficiency
  • Protein S deficiency

Recall two disorders of procoagulant excess.

  • Factor V Leiden
  • Factor VIII excess — ›1.5 iu/ml, NR 0.5 -1.5
  • Prothrombin (factor II) excess

What is Factor V Leiden?

  • Factor V Leiden is an inherited disorder that results in a mutation in the gene coding for the Factor V coagulation factor
  • This mutation leads to Factor V changing shape

Describe the pathophysiology of Factor V Leiden.

  • In Factor V Leiden, a point mutation in the Factor V gene causes a change in its structure
  • This mutation makes Factor V resistant to inactivation by activated Protein C (APC)
  • As a result, Factor V remains active for longer, promoting the overproduction of thrombin and increasing the risk of blood clots

As age increases, the risk of venous thrombosis development increases significantly

Venous thrombosis is multi-causal and it arises from the interaction between genetic and acquired risk factors
