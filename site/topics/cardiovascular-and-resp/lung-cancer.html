Overview

What is lung cancer?

Lung cancer is a type of cancer that originates in the cells of the lungs, which are essential organs for breathing and gas exchange

How common is lung cancer amongst other cancers in the UK?

Lung cancer is the 3rd most common cancer in UK

How many diagnoses of lung cancer are there in the UK annually?

~48 000 diagnoses/year

How many deaths from lung cancer are there in the UK annually?

  • ~35,000 deaths/year
  • It is the leading cause of death from cancer

What is the common age to get lung cancer?

  • Peak age = 75-90 Is lung cancer more common in males or females?
  • Males

What proportion of lung cancer patients have never smoked?

10-15% of patients with lung cancer never smoked

What is the major risk factor for lung cancer?

Smoking

  • Duration
  • Intensity — i.e how many per week?

Recall three causes of lung cancer other than smoking.

  • Asbestos — exposure (plumbers, ship-builders, carriage workers, carpenters, etc) – risk up to x2
  • Radon — e.g. silver miners in Germany late 19th century; 1950s uranium mining in Colorado
  • Indoor cooking fumes — wood smoke, frying fats
  • Chronic lung diseases — COPD, fibrosis
  • Air pollution
  • Familial/ genetic — several loci identified

What is asbestos?

  • Asbestos is a group of naturally occurring silicate minerals composed of thin, fibrous crystals
  • It was widely used in the past for its durability, heat resistance, and insulating properties but is now recognised as a significant health hazard

Asbestos.

What is radon?

Radon is a colourless, odourless, and tasteless radioactive gas that occurs naturally as a result of the decay of uranium in soil, rock, and water

Recall three types of cell present within the lung.

  • Epithelial cells — alveolar, ciliated, goblet, basal (stem) cells
  • Immune cells
  • Endothelial cells
  • Mesenchymal cells
  • Neuroendocrine cells — release neurotransmitters
  • Stem/Progenitor cells

Describe the pathogenesis of lung cancer.

  • Lung cancer can develop in all differentiated and undifferentiated cells
  • The interaction between inhaled carcinogens and the epithelium of upper and lower airways leads to the formation of DNA adducts
  • Persisting DNA adducts/misrepaired adducts result in a mutation and can cause genomic alterations → cancer

What are DNA adducts?

DNA adducts are pieces of DNA that are covalently bound to a cancer-causing chemical

Sugar-Phosphate Backbone.

Which part of the DNA molecule is usually affected by DNA adducts?

DNA adducts typically form on the nitrogenous bases of the DNA molecule, as these are the most chemically reactive component

Describe the role that DNA adducts in the pathogenesis of lung cancer.

  • Adducts can interfere with DNA replication by causing errors in base pairing. This is because the cancer-causing chemicals bind to the nitrogenous bases seen within the sugar-phosphate backbone of the DNA double-helix and alter their structure
  • For example, this could lead to a guanine adduct mispairing with thymine instead of cytosine → cancer-causing mutations

Bronchial Tree.

What are Clara cells?

  • Clara cells (club cells) are non-ciliated secretory epithelial cells found in the small airways of the lungs, specifically the bronchioles
  • They perform secretory, detoxification and regenerative functions

Recall the four types of lung cancer.

  • Squamous cell carcinoma
  • Adenocarcinoma
  • Large cell lung cancer
  • Small cell lung cancer

Describe how these lung cancers are usually grouped.

  • Non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) = Squamous cell carcinoma, Adenocarcinoma, Large cell lung cancer
  • Small cell lung cancer = Small cell lung cancer

Describe what a squamous cell carcinoma is and where in the lungs they originate.

  • A squamous cell carcinoma is a malignant tumour of squamous epithelial cells
  • It usually originates from the bronchial epithelium, usually in the centre of the lungs

What percentage of all lung cancers do squamous cell carcinomas account for?

Squamous cell carcinomas account for ~30% of all lung cancers and they are the second-most common type of lung cancer

Squamous Cell Carcinoma.

Describe what an adenocarcinoma is and where in the lungs they originate.

  • An adenocarcinoma is a malignant tumour of glandular epithelial cells
  • They usually originate from mucus-producing glandular tissue and are more peripherally-located rather than central

What percentage of all lung cancers do adenocarcinomas account for?

Adenocarcinomas account for ~40% of all lung cancers and they are the most common type of lung cancer

Describe what a large cell lung cancer is.

Large cell lung cancers are malignant tumours that originate within the large cells of the lung — This primarily includes alveolar macrophages and Type II pneumocytes (alveolar cells) which are used to protect and support lung structure respectively

What percentage of all lung cancers do large cell lung cancers account for?

Large cell lung cancers account for ~15% of all lung cancers NON-SMALL CELL LUNG CANCERS (NSCLCs).

Describe what a small cell lung cancer is and where in the lungs they originate.

Small cell lung cancers are highly malignant tumours that originate within the pulmonary neuroendocrine cells

What percentage of all lung cancers do small cell lung cancers account for?

It accounts for ~15% of all lung cancers

Describe the difference between Type 1 and Type 2 pneumocytes.

  • Type I pneumocytes are thin, squamous (flat) epithelial cells that cover about 95% of the alveolar surface area. — They are extremely thin to allow for efficient gas exchange between the air in the alveoli and the blood in the capillaries
  • Type II pneumocytes are cuboidal cells that are larger than Type I cells and cover about 5% of the alveolar surface area. — They produce and secrete pulmonary surfactant, a substance that reduces surface tension in the alveoli, preventing the alveolar walls from sticking together and collapsing during exhalation
  • NOTE: Pneumocytes are the same as alveolar cells

What is the most common lung cancer type?

Adenocarcinoma

What is the most malignant lung cancer type?

Small cell lung cancer

Recall four important oncogenes implicated in lung cancer.

  • epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) tyrosine kinase
  • anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) tyrosine kinase
  • c-ROS oncogene 1 (ROS1) receptor tyrosine kinase
  • BRAF (downstream cell-cycle signalling mediator)

What is the physiological role of epidermal growth factor

receptor ( EGFR ) tyrosine kinase? • The Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR) is a key tyrosine kinase receptor that regulates important cellular processes like growth, proliferation, survival, and differentiation

Describe in which cancers epidermal growth factor receptor ( EGFR ) tyrosine kinase is implicated.

  • 15-30% of adenocarcinomas
  • It is implicated more so in women, those with Asian ethnicity, and never-smokers

What is the physiological role of anaplastic lymphoma kinase

( ALK ) tyrosine kinase? • The physiological role of Anaplastic Lymphoma Kinase (ALK) is crucial for normal development, particularly in the nervous system, muscle development, and immune function • It regulates cell survival, differentiation, and migration through its tyrosine kinase activity

Describe in which cancers anaplastic lymphoma kinase ( ALK ) tyrosine kinase is implicated.

  • 2-7% of non-small cell lung cancers
  • It is implicated especially in younger patients and never-smokers

What is the physiological role of c-ROS oncogene 1 ( ROS1 )

receptor tyrosine kinase? • ROS1 is also involved in normal cell signalling pathways that control growth, migration, and survival

Describe in which cancers c-ROS oncogene 1 ( ROS1 ) receptor tyrosine kinase is implicated.

  • 1-2% of non-small cell lung cancers (NSCLCs)
  • It is implicated especially in younger patients and never-smokers

What is the physiological role of BRAF (downstream cell-cycle

signalling mediator)? • BRAF is a downstream cell-cycle signalling mediator in the MAPK/ERK pathway, which regulates key cellular processes such as growth, proliferation, differentiation, and survival

Describe in which cancers BRAF (downstream cell-cycle signalling mediator) is implicated.

  • 1-3% of non-small cell lung cancers
  • It is implicated especially in smokers

Recall three key symptoms of lung cancer.

  • Cough
  • Weight loss — high metabolic demands due to requirements of tumour development
  • Breathlessness
  • Fatigue
  • Chest pain
  • Haemoptysis
  • NOTE: Lung cancer is frequently asymptomatic, people often present late

Lung Cancer - Symptoms.

Recall three neurological features of advanced/metastatic lung disease.

  • Focal weakness
  • Seizures
  • Spinal cord compression

Recall another feature of advanced/metastatic lung disease.

Bone pain

What is meant by a paraneoplastic syndrome?

Paraneoplastic means a group of symptoms that develop when cancer cells release substances that disrupt the normal function of surrounding cells and tissue

Recall two paraneoplastic syndromes of advanced/metastatic lung disease.

  • Clubbing
  • Hypercalcaemia — PTHrP (parathyroid hormone–related peptide)
  • Hyponatraemia — ectopic ADH
  • Cushing’s — elevated corticosteroids due to treatment

Lung Cancer Metastases.

Describe three clinical signs of lung cancer.

  • Cachexia (muscle wasting)
  • Clubbing
  • Superior vena cava obstruction (Pemberton’s sign)
  • Horner’s syndrome

What is clubbing?

Clubbing, also known as digital clubbing or hypertrophic osteoarthropathy, is a clinical sign characterised by abnormal swelling and rounding of the fingertips and toes

What causes clubbing?

  • Clubbing is typically associated with several types of chronic diseases that cause prolonged oxygen deprivation to tissues or persistent inflammation
  • It is thought to involve vascular changes to soft tissue at the nail beds and increased blood flow to the distal parts of the fingernails and toes — Inflammatory or fibrotic changes in the connective tissue beneath the nails may also contribute to the shape change.

Clubbing.

What is Pemberton’s sign?

  • Pemberton's sign is a clinical test used to identify the presence of superior vena cava (SVC) syndrome, which can occur when there is obstruction or compression of the superior vena cava
  • Pemberton's sign is performed by having the patient raise both of their arms above their head and hold them there for a short period of time — (typically around 30 seconds to 1 minute)
  • Positive Pemberton's sign occurs if this manoeuvre leads to facial flushing, distended neck veins, and/or cyanosis (a bluish discolouration of the face or neck), indicating a blockage of venous return to the heart due to compression of the superior vena cava — This is because raising the arms increases venous pressure in the upper body and intrathoracic pressure
  • This is often a sign of an SVC obstruction, which may be caused by a tumour (such as lung cancer ), lymphadenopathy, or goitre (enlarged thyroid gland) PEMBERTON’S SIGN.

Lung Cancer & Superior Vena Cava.

What is Horner’s syndrome?

  • Horner's syndrome is a rare neurological condition caused by disruption of the sympathetic nerve pathway that controls the muscles of the eye and surrounding structures
  • It commonly leads to ptosis, miosis (pupil constriction), anhidrosis (reduced sweating in one side of face) and enopthalmos (opposite of exopthalmos) HORNER’S SYNDROME.

Describe the steps involved in the diagnostic strategy of lung cancer.

  • Establish most likely diagnosis
  • Establish fitness for investigation and treatment
  • Confirm diagnosis and histological type — genomic testing key if considering systemic treatment in NSCLC
  • Confirm staging

Recall five potential members of the lung cancer disciplinary team.

  • Respiratory doctor
  • Radiologist
  • Thoracic surgeon
  • Pathologist — looks at tissues
  • Oncologist
  • Palliative care doctor
  • Patient — most important stakeholder in the MDT

Describe how lung cancer screening works.

  • Current or ex-smokers aged between 55 and 74 are invited via their GP to attend a lung health check
  • They are then offered an appointment for a low dose CT scan

What proportion of patients who come in for lung cancer screening have lung cancer detected in them?

  • Lung cancer was detected in 29/1145 (2.5%) participants scanned
  • Of these, 58.6% had stage 1 lung cancer which shows it is being detected early

Recall three imaging techniques used in the diagnosis of lung cancer.

  • X-Ray
  • CT scan
  • PET-CT scan

Describe the overall role of imaging in the diagnosis of lung cancer.

  • Imaging is used to detect tumours and where applicable, stage the tumours present
  • We can assess the size of the tumour, node involvement and metastasis using imaging

Describe what we would see on an X-ray of a patient with lung cancer.

  • In the black space within the lung that usually represents air, there would be a zone of opacity, in what is usually referred to as consolidation — This represents the tumour within the lungs
  • NOTE: X-rays are the most basic form of imaging for lung cancer

Describe a limitation of X-rays in lung cancer diagnosis.

  • It is possible for lung cancer to not be picked up on an X-ray
  • The tumour is usually only picked up when it is very advanced. Hence in urgent situations, we go straight to CT scans for suspected lung cancer

Cxr - Lung Cancer.

Describe how a CT scan can be useful to us in the diagnosis of lung cancer.

  • CT scans can be used to locate the tumour and identify metastases that potentially may have spread from the lungs to elsewhere in the body
  • For example, the liver, lymph nodes or other organs

Ct Scan - Lung Cancer.

Describe how a PET-CT scan can be useful to us in the diagnosis of lung cancer.

  • A PET scan can be used to detect and exclude occult metastases — It uses a dye to highlight areas of high metabolic activity
  • NOTE: The fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) dye contains glucose. Cells with high metabolic activity (i.e cancer cells) absorb more FDG because they consume glucose at higher rate.

What is an occult metastasis?

Occult metastases are tumours that are not detected during an initial examination but are found through further evaluation

When is a PET-CT scan used?

  • A PET-CT scan is used after a CT scan has been used and there is no occult metastasis detected — The PET-CT scan can then be used to rule out occult metastasis
  • NOTE: It would be useless to use a PET-CT scan if you have already detected occult metastasis upon CT scan, as you are no longer ruling anything out

Pet-Ct Scans - Lung Cancer.

What is a biopsy?

A biopsy, put simply, is a small section of tissue that has been taken from a patient

Describe the overall role of a biopsy in the diagnosis of lung cancer.

  • A biopsy is the gold standard for confirming lung cancer after imaging studies — i.e CT or PET-CT suggest a suspicious lesion
  • It helps us to identify the type of lung cancer (i.e NSCLC, SCLC)

which is helpful in directing our treatment

(see lecture diagram / notes)

Describe how we decide which biopsy to take in the diagnosis of lung cancer.

We choose our method of biopsy based on accessibility, availability and impact on staging (i.e how pivotal it could be)

Describe four ways we can obtain a biopsy in the diagnosis of lung cancer.

  • Bronchoscopy
  • Endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS)
  • Navigational bronchoscopy + robotic bronchoscopy
  • CT-guided lung biopsy

When is a simple bronchoscopy biopsy used?

  • For tumours of central and segmental airways
  • Bronchoscopy involves the insertion of a bronchoscope into the airways and a biopsy sample being obtained

Airways.

When is a endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS) biopsy used?

  • It is used in combination with transbronchial needle aspiration of mediastinal lymph nodes (EBUS [TBNA]) and so its purpose is to sample the mediastinal lymph nodes — i.e to stage mediastinum +/- achieve tissue diagnosis
  • NOTE: This imaging technique is very similar to bronchoscopy, but the bronchoscope is much wider (fatter)

When is a navigational bronchoscopy biopsy used?

  • Navigational bronchoscopy/robotic bronchoscopy is used for peripheral lesions/nodules that are not amenable to conventional bronchoscopy or CT guided biopsy
  • NOTE: It is a new and advanced technique

When is a CT-guided biopsy used?

  • CT-guided lung biopsies are used to access peripheral lung tumours
  • NOTE: CT-guided biopsies are not without risk. There is risk of pneumothorax and risk of significant bleeds

What is the next step after imaging and biopsy?

Staging

Describe how TNM staging can be used in the staging on lung cancer.

  • T1-4: tumour size and location
  • N0-3: lymph node involvement – mediastinum + beyond
  • M0-1c: metastases + number

Recall the tumour size range for T0.

  • No primary tumour
  • Carcinoma in situ

Recall the tumour size range for T1.

  • ‹3cm
  • T1a = 0-1 cm
  • T1b = 1-2 cm
  • T1c = 2-3 cm

Recall the tumour size range for T2.

  • 3-5 cm
  • T2a = 3-4 cm
  • T2b = 4-5 cm

Recall the tumour size range for T3.

5-7 cm

Recall the tumour size range for T4.

›7 cm

Recall the criteria for N0.

No regional node metastasis

Recall the criteria for N1.

Metastasis in ipsilateral pulmonary or hilar nodes

Recall the criteria for N2.

Metastasis in ipsilateral mediastinal/subcarinal nodes

Recall the criteria for N3.

Metastasis in contralateral mediastinal/hilar, or supraclavicular nodes

Recall the criteria for M0.

No distant metastasis

Recall the criteria for M1a.

Malignant pleural/pericardial effusion or pleural/pericardial nodules or separate tumor nodule(s) in a contralateral lobe

Recall the criteria for M1b.

Single extrathoracic metastasis

Recall the criteria for M1c.

Multiple extrathoracic metastases (1 or ›1 organ)

Lung Cancer Stage Classification.

Describe three factors that we can use to determine and direct treatment of lung cancer.

  • Patient fitness
  • Cancer histology
  • Cancer stage
  • Patient preference
  • Health service factors

What is the WHO performance status?

  • The WHO performance status is used to assess a patient’s fitness
  • It can be helpful in determining whether a patient is eligible for surgery etc.

Describe the groups within the WHO performance status.

  • 0 – Asymptomatic — Fully active, able to carry on all predisease activities without restriction
  • 1 – Symptomatic but completely ambulatory — Restricted in physically strenuous activity but ambulatory and able to carry out work of a light or sedentary nature. For example, light housework, office work
  • 2 – Symptomatic, up and about ›50% of waking hours — Ambulatory and capable of all self care but unable to carry out any work activities
  • 3 – Symptomatic, confined to bed or chair ›50% of waking hours — Capable of only limited self-care
  • 4 – Completely disabled (Completely disabled. Cannot carry on any self-care. Totally confined to bed or chair)
  • 5 – Death

What does ambulatory mean?

This means the patient is able to walk around

Describe how the WHO performance status is useful to us in directing treatment for lung cancer.

  • Radical treatment (i.e surgery) is usually restricted to between groups 0 and 2 — This is between asymptomatic and symptomatic, up and about ›50% of waking hours
  • Comorbidities and current lung function are also very important factors that must be considered when directing treatment

What is the standard of care for early stage disease in lung cancer?

  • Surgical resection
  • Lobectomy + lymphadenectomy is the usual approach — i.e local resection

Describe the main alternative type of surgical resection other than lobectomy.

  • Sublobar resection is used if the tumour is stage 1 (≤3 cm)
  • This includes wedge resection and segmental resection

Describe how surgery is used in the treatment of lung cancer.

  • Lobectomy + lymphadenectomy is the usual approach
  • If the tumour is stage 1 (≤3 cm), wedge resection and segmental resection can be conducted

Surgery In Lung Cancer.

Recall an alternative treatment to surgery for treatment of lung cancer.

Radical radiotherapy

What is radical radiotherapy?

  • Radical radiotherapy is a type of radiation therapy administered with the intent to cure cancer or achieve long-term disease control, rather than to simply relieve symptoms (i.e in palliative radiotherapy)
  • It involves delivering high doses of radiation to precisely target and destroy cancer cells while sparing surrounding healthy tissues as much as possible

Describe when radical radiotherapy can be used in the treatment of lung cancer.

  • Radical radiotherapy is an alternative to surgery for early stage disease
  • NOTE: The outcomes are not so good. The mediastinum isn’t targeted

What is stereotactic ablative body radiotherapy (SABR)?

  • Stereotactic ablative body radiotherapy (SABR) is considered a form of radical radiotherapy
  • It is a form which includes a lower dose

Recall three systemic treatments for lung cancer.

  • Oncogene-directed therapy
  • Immunotherapy
  • Cytotoxic chemotherapy

What is oncogene-directed therapy?

Oncogene-directed therapies are treatments that are used to target the oncogenes commonly implicated in the pathogenesis of lung cancer

Recall two oncogene-directed therapies that target epidermal growth factor receptor ( EGFR ) tyrosine kinase.

  • Erlotinib
  • Gefitinib
  • Afatinib
  • Dacomitinib
  • Osimertinib

Recall two oncogene-directed therapies that target anaplastic lymphoma kinase ( ALK ) tyrosine kinase.

  • Crizotinib
  • Ceritinib
  • Alectinib
  • Brigatinib
