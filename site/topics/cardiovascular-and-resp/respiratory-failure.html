Overview

What is respiratory failure?

  • This is a syndrome of inadequate gas exchange due to dysfunction of one or more components of the respiratory system
  • ALTERNATIVE: Respiratory failure is a serious medical condition that occurs when the respiratory system cannot effectively exchange oxygen and carbon dioxide in the blood.

Recall three groups we can use to classify the signs and symptoms of respiratory failure.

  • Nervous
  • Respiratory muscle
  • Pulmonary

Describe the nervous signs and symptoms of respiratory failure.

  • CNS/Brainstem dysfunction — i.e stroke
  • Peripheral nervous system dysfunction — i.e weakness
  • Neuro-muscular junction dysfunction

Describe the respiratory muscle signs and symptoms of respiratory failure.

  • Diaphragm & thoracic muscles weakness
  • Extra-thoracic muscles weakness

Extrathoracic Muscles.

Describe the pulmonary signs and symptoms of respiratory failure.

  • Airway disease
  • Alveolar-capillary problems
  • Circulation issues

Describe the epidemiology of respiratory failure.

Women around the world are disproportionately affected by respiratory failure when compared to men

Describe how badly respiratory failure is leading to death.

Chronic respiratory disease is the 3rd leading cause of death (2017) — 39.8% rise from 1990

Describe the social and financial costs of respiratory failure.

EU 380m Euro’s annually (2019) care for chronic respiratory disorders — This accounts for: Inpatient care, lost productivity

Describe how the prevalence of respiratory failure has changed from 1990 to 2017.

  • From 1990 to 2017, the prevalence, mortality, and DALY rates per 100k dropped by 14.3%, 42.6% and 38.2% respectively
  • NOTE: Smoking was banned in public places in 2007

What is a DALY?

  • DALY stands for Disability-Adjusted Life Year, a metric used to measure the overall burden of disease
  • It combines both the years of life lost due to premature death and the years lived with disability or illness
  • This helps quantify the impact of diseases or health conditions on populations in a way that includes both fatal and non-fatal outcomes.

Describe the hierarchy of the respiratory tree from trachea to alveoli.

  • Trachea
  • Bronchi
  • Bronchioles
  • Terminal Bronchioles
  • Respiratory Bronchioles
  • Alveolar Ducts
  • Alveolar Sacs

Describe how oxygen transport within the lungs works.

  • Oxygen transport from the alveoli to the capillaries works using pressure gradients
  • The pressure of O2 in the alveoli is greater than that of the capillaries,

which means O2 flows down the pressure gradient across the thin permeable membrane provided by the capillary endothelium (one cell thick)

(see lecture diagram / notes)

Oxygen Transport.

What is CdO2?

CdO₂ refers to the content of dissolved oxygen in the blood — It is a part of the total oxygen content in the blood and specifically measures the oxygen that is physically dissolved in the plasma, as opposed to oxygen bound to haemoglobin.

What is CaO2?

  • CaO₂ refers to the arterial oxygen content, which is a measure of the total amount of oxygen carried in arterial blood
  • It includes both the oxygen bound to haemoglobin (HbO2) and the oxygen dissolved in plasma (CdO2)

What is meant by pulmonary transit time?

  • Pulmonary transit time refers to the amount of time it takes for blood to travel from the pulmonary artery (through the lungs) to the pulmonary veins, where it returns to the heart
  • NOTE: The pulmonary circulation begins when blood enters the pulmonary arteries from the right side of the heart, and it ends when the oxygenated blood returns to the heart via the pulmonary veins to be pumped into the systemic circulation

Recall the time that blood in the pulmonary circulation spends in direct gas exchange with the lungs.

0.75 seconds

What is meant by gas exchange time?

Gas exchange time refers to the duration it takes for gases (primarily oxygen (O₂) and carbon dioxide (CO₂) ) to diffuse across the alveolar- capillary membrane in the lungs

Recall the normal physiological gas exchange time.

0.25 seconds

Pulmonary Circulation.

What is pleural pressure?

Pleural pressure refers to the pressure within the pleural cavity, which is the space between the parietal pleura (lining the chest wall) and the visceral pleura (covering the lungs).

Describe the role of pleural pressure within the lungs.

  • Pleural pressure's main role is to create a negative pressure that helps maintain lung expansion
  • If pleural pressure becomes too positive (i.e pneumothorax), the lung cannot expand fully, which can hinder ventilation and reduce gas exchange capacity — i.e because the pleural cavity will apply pressure against the lungs

which may cause it to collapse

(see lecture diagram / notes)

When pleural pressure is negative, it ensures that the lungs expand during inhalation

This expansion lowers the alveolar pressure (relative to atmospheric pressure), allowing air to flow into the lungs.

Explain how pleural pressure varies between the apex and bottom of the lungs.

  • Higher up the lung, there is more negative (lower) pleural pressure because the bottom of the lungs is pulling down the top by means of gravity
  • This means that the tissue at the top is stretched and there is also a greater compression of the pleural cavity in lower regions, leading to a less negative (higher) pleural pressure in the lower regions of the lung

Pleural Cavity.

What is transmural pressure?

Transmural pressure refers to the difference in pressure across the wall of a structure, such as a blood vessel or the lung. — i.e transmural pressure gradient of lung measures pressure difference between inside and outside lungs

What is meant by compliance with regards to lung tissue?

Lung compliance refers to how easily the lung tissue can stretch and expand in response to changes in pressure.

Explain the difference in compliance between the apex and bottom of the lungs.

  • At the top (apex) of the lungs, the alveoli are already partially expanded because of the more negative pleural pressure — i.e the pleural cavity is not applying as much pressure on the lungs at the top, so the alveoli are more widely opened
  • This means that the alveoli at the apex are stiffer, and they have less ability to expand further during inhalation
  • In contrast, the alveoli at the bottom (base) of the lungs are less inflated at the start of the breath due to the higher pleural pressure — i.e the pleural cavity is applying more pressure on the lungs at the bottom, so the alveoli are less widely opened
  • This means they have more room to expand during inspiration, and they are therefore more compliant (they can expand more easily)

What is ventilation?

  • Ventilation refers to the process of moving air in and out of the lungs to facilitate gas exchange
  • It involves two main phases: inhalation (inspiration) and exhalation (expiration)

Describe how ventilation varies between the apex and bottom of the lungs.

  • Higher up the lung, there is more negative (lower) pleural pressure because the bottom of the lungs is pulling down the top by means of gravity, meaning that the tissue at the top is stretched, and also creating greater compression of the pleural cavity in lower regions — As a result, the alveoli at the apex are more open at rest. This means that they have less capacity for further expansion when compared to the bottom, meaning they are less compliant — Less ventilation
  • Lower down the lung, there is less negative pleural pressure because there is a greater compression of the pleural cavity in lower regions due to gravity, leading to a less negative (higher) pleural pressure in the lower regions of the lung — As a result, alveoli are less open during rest. This means that they have more capacity for further expansion when compared to the apex, meaning they are more compliant — More ventilation

Describe the relationship between compliance and ventilation.

  • High compliance means the lungs expand and relax easily with less effort, allowing for more efficient movement of air →better ventilation — the bottom
  • Low compliance means the lungs are stiff and resist expansion and relaxation, requiring more effort to move air in and out →reduced ventilation — the apex

What is perfusion?

Perfusion refers to the process of blood flow through the blood vessels of the body, specifically to various organs and tissues — It is crucial for delivering oxygen, nutrients, and hormones to cells and removing waste products like carbon dioxide and urea

What is meant by recruitment in regards to perfusion?

In the context of perfusion, recruitment refers to the process by

which previously underperfused or non-functioning blood vessels (often capillaries) are opened or activated to increase blood flow to specific areas, particularly in the lungs or other organs.

(see lecture diagram / notes)

Explain how perfusion varies between the apex and bottom of the lungs.

  • The blood flow (perfusion) at the bottom of the lungs is also better because gravity causes greater intravascular pressure in the bottom of the lung — Pressure = Flow x resistance
  • There is also greater recruitment, flow rate and less resistance at the bottom of the lungs
  • NOTE: This is in an individual standing upright. Perfusion varies based on the orientation of an individual

Describe the significance of ventilation-perfusion ratio.

  • The V/Q ratio is the ratio of ventilation (V) to perfusion (Q) in the lungs. Ideally, ventilation and perfusion should be well matched for efficient gas exchange.
  • Ventilation (V) : The volume of air that reaches the alveoli per minute. This is the flow of fresh air into the lungs, where oxygen is taken up, and carbon dioxide is released.
  • Perfusion (Q) : The volume of blood flowing through the pulmonary capillaries per minute, carrying carbon dioxide to be exhaled and oxygen to be absorbed.

What is the ideal ventilation-perfusion ratio?

  • 0.8 — i.e perfusion › ventilation
  • This balance ensures that oxygen is being exchanged efficiently to the abundant blood flow
  • If ventilation were greater than perfusion (V/Q › 1), it would lead to inefficient gas exchange, with oxygen not being properly absorbed into the blood, which is not optimal for respiratory function

Describe how ventilation-perfusion matching differs between the apex and bottom of the lung.

  • V/Q ratio is much lower at the bottom of the lung, allowing for more efficient gas exchange than the apex
  • This is because there is much better perfusion at the bottom of the lung
  • The base of the lung hence performs most of the work during respiration
  • NOTE: If you lie down on your front, you could potentially achieve a more equal V/Q ratio across the entire lung because the effects of gravity are negated

Ventilation-Perfusion Matching.

What is the tidal volume?

The amount of air that moves in or out of the lungs with each respiratory cycle

What is the inspiratory reserve volume?

The remaining volume of air that can still be inspired after one respiratory cycle to reach the maximum inspiratory effort

What is the expiratory reserve volume?

The remaining volume of air that can still be expired after one respiratory cycle to reach the maximum expiratory effort

What is the residual volume?

  • The remaining volume of air within the lungs that cannot be breathed out
  • This is to do with the structure and texture of the lungs

What is inspiratory capacity?

The volume of air that can potentially be breathed in after the last respiratory cycle to reach the vital capacity of the lungs — i.e inspiratory reserve volume + tidal volume

What is vital capacity?

The maximum volume of air that can be breathed into the lungs from the residual volume — i.e inspiratory reserve volume + tidal volume + expiratory reserve volume

What is the difference between total lung capacity and vital capacity?

  • Total lung capacity is the maximum volume of air that the lungs can contain
  • Vital capacity is the maximum volume of air that can be inspired by the lungs
  • i.e vital capacity = total lung capacity (~6L) - residual volume

What is functional residual capacity?

  • The volume of air which the lung assumes when inspiratory and expiratory forces are balanced (~2.5L)
  • This is the volume of the lungs in a dead person — expiratory reserve volume + residual volume

Lung Mechanics Volumes & Capacities.

What is minute ventilation?

Volume of gas entering and leaving the lungs per minute

What is the formula for minute ventilation?

Minute ventilation (L/min) = Tidal volume (L) x Breathing frequency (breaths/min)

What is alveolar ventilation?

Volume of gas entering and leaving the alveoli of the lungs per minute

What is the formula for alveolar ventilation?

Alveolar ventilation = (tidal volume - dead space) x breathing frequency

Why is alveolar ventilation used?

  • Dead space accounts for all of the air that is lost in the airways
  • Alveolar ventilation informs us how much of the inspired air actually reaches the alveoli of the lungs and can be used in gas exchange
  • NOTE: Although, there is alveolar dead space that still needs to be accounted for

What is ‘dead space’ in the lungs?

  • Total dead space of the lungs is the volume of air in the respiratory system that does not take part in gas exchange.
  • In humans, this is equal to anatomical plus alveolar dead space — i.e nasal passages (or oral cavity, if breathing through the mouth), pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi (primary, secondary, and tertiary bronchi), bronchioles (up to the terminal bronchioles)

What is alveolar dead space?

Alveolar dead space refers to the volume of the alveoli (the tiny air sacs in the lungs where gas exchange occurs) that are ventilated but not perfused with blood — Hence they cannot participate in gas exchange

What is the formula for compliance?

Compliance = Change in Lung Volume / Change in Transmural Pressure — i.e if the lung volume increases a lot at a constant transmural pressure, then the lung is more compliant — i.e as pleural pressure increases, compliance decreases

Compliance.

What is meant by elastance with regards to lung tissue?

  • The tendency to recoil to its original volume
  • It is the inverse of compliance

Describe the differences in elastance between the apex and bottom of the lungs.

  • The apex of the lungs have a higher elastance than the bottom
  • This is because they have a lower compliance, and hence a higher tendency to return to their original structure

What is the formula for elastance?

Elastance = Change in Transmural Pressure / Change in Lung Volume — i.e if a set increase in transmural pressure causes a lower increase in lung volume (as it wants to conform to its original conformation), elastance is said to be higher — i.e as the pleural pressure increases, elastance increases

Elastance.

  • This means that muscles cannot contract or relax, and so it leads to fatiguable weakness

Describe how the physiological classification of respiratory failure works.

Physiological classification of respiratory failure works in four groups: — Type I — Type II — Type III — Type IV

What is Type 1 respiratory failure?

Type 1 respiratory failure is a condition characterised by low oxygen levels in the blood (hypoxemia) with normal or low carbon dioxide levels (normocapnia or hypocapnia)

Recall another name for Type 1 respiratory failure.

Hypoxemic respiratory failure — (PaO2 ‹60 at sea level)

Recall the value for hypoxaemia.

PaO2 ‹60 mmHg

What causes Type 1 respiratory failure?

  • Type 1 respiratory failure is caused by impaired gas exchange in the lungs
  • Oxygen uptake is impaired but carbon dioxide (CO2) removal remains relatively intact, at least initially
  • NOTE: The usual cause is ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) mismatch or shunting, where blood bypasses oxygenated alveoli or is inadequately oxygenated in the lungs.

Recall three characteristics of Type 1 respiratory failure.

  • Hypoxemia
  • Hypocapnia or Normocapnia — Hypercapnia is rare
  • Increased shunt fraction (Qs/Qt)
  • Alveolar flooding → crackles on auscultation
  • Hypoxemia refractory to supplemental oxygen — i.e do not improve even when given oxygen, this is because gas exchange is impaired

Why is CO2 normal or low in Type 1 respiratory failure?

  • Because CO₂ diffuses 20x better than O₂, so even if oxygenation is impaired by ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) mismatch, CO₂ can still be removed (NORMAL)
  • Often, patients hyperventilate in response to hypoxia → blowing off CO₂ (LOW)

What is shunt fraction?

  • Shunt fraction (Qs/Qt) is the percentage of cardiac output that does not get fully oxygenated at the lungs, due to the blood bypassing the pulmonary capillaries
  • It is calculated by Qs (shunt (unoxygenated flow)) over Qt (total cardiac output) — It usually occurs when blood flows through shunts, such as areas where pulmonary ventilation is inadequate, leading to incomplete oxygenation

Shunts.

What is Type 2 respiratory failure?

Type 2 respiratory failure is a condition where the body fails to effectively remove carbon dioxide (CO₂) from the blood, leading to elevated CO₂ levels (hypercapnia) and often low oxygen levels (hypoxemia)

Recall another name for Type 2 respiratory failure.

Hypercapnic respiratory failure

Recall the value for hypercapnia.

PaCO2 ›45 mmHg

What characterises Type 2 respiratory failure?

  • Failure to exchange or remove carbon dioxide
  • The central problem is the failure to move sufficient air into and out of the alveoli (poor ventilation), leading to hypercapnia and hypoxemia (PaO2 ‹60 mmHg)

Recall three characteristics of Type 2 respiratory failure.

  • Hypercapnia
  • Hypoxemia (often present) — though not always as severe as in Type 1 respiratory failure
  • Decreased alveolar minute ventilation (V A )
  • Dead space ventilation — i.e wasting of gas exchange

Describe the difference between Type 1 and Type 2 respiratory failure.

  • In Type 1 respiratory failure, the primary problem is oxygenation of blood that passes through the alveolar capillaries — This is due to either the blood bypassing oxygenated alveoli, inefficient oxygen uptake (i.e shunting) or ventilation/perfusion mismatch — This means hypoxemia is present in Type 1 failure, with hypercapnia less likely to be present as the movement of air into and out of the lungs is still intact, but uptake of oxygen from lungs → blood is impaired
  • In Type 2 respiratory failure, the primary problem is ventilation — This means that O2 and CO2 struggle to go both ways in the lungs which is why the hypercapnia is often accompanied with hypoxemia
  • NOTE: Generally, because CO2 diffuses across the alveolar-capillary membrane much more efficiently, it takes significant ventilation dysfunction for hypercapnia to be present

What is shunting?

Shunting in the context of the respiratory system refers to a situation

where blood flows through the lungs without participating in gas exchange.

This can cause hypoxemia

What is Type 3 respiratory failure?

Type 3 respiratory failure occurs when respiratory failure develops in the context of surgery, particularly following abdominal or thoracic procedures

Recall another name for Type 3 respiratory failure.

Perioperative respiratory failure

Describe the characteristics of Type 3 respiratory failure.

  • Hypoxemia or hypercapnoea
  • Increased atelectasis (lung/alveolar collapse) due to low functional residual capacity (FRC) with abnormal abdominal wall mechanics

What is atelectasis?

Atelectasis is the collapse of part or all of a lung/alveoli, leading to reduced or absent ventilation in the affected area

How can Type 3 respiratory failure be prevented?

  • Anaesthetic or operative technique
  • Posture — i.e sitting up
  • Incentive spirometry
  • Analgesia
  • Attempts to lower intra- abdominal pressure

What is Type 4 respiratory failure?

  • Type 4 respiratory failure refers to respiratory failure associated with circulatory shock, rather than a primary issue with the lungs or ventilation
  • Type IV describes patients who are intubated and ventilated. During shock (Septic/cardiogenic/neurologic) — This is because they have really high oxygen demands in shock and the breathing is unable to keep up

Recall another name for Type 4 respiratory failure.

Shock

What causes Type 4 respiratory failure?

Inadequate perfusion — As a result, there is reduced oxygen delivery to tissues

How can Type 4 respiratory failure be treated?

  • The aim is to restore the state of normoxemia from hypoxemia. This is achieved by:
  • Optimising ventilation
  • Improving gas exchange and unloading the respiratory muscles, lowering their oxygen consumption

Which organ can be affected by optimised ventilation? (EXTRA-POSTGRAD)

Heart

Describe the secondary impacts of optimised ventilation on the heart. (EXTRA - POSTGRAD)

  • Reduced afterload (good for LV)
  • Increased pre-load (bad for RV)

What is pneumonia?

  • Pneumonia is a lung infection that inflames the air sacs (alveoli) in one or both lungs.
  • The alveoli may fill with fluid or pus, leading to symptoms such as coughing, difficulty breathing, and fever

Describe the causes of pneumonia.

  • Pneumonia is classified based on the type of microorganism causing the infection or how it was acquired. These include:
  • Bacterial — i.e Streptococcus pneumoniae
  • Viral — i.e influenza, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), or coronaviruses (e.g., SARS-CoV-2)
  • Fungal
  • Aspiration — Occurs when food, drink, or stomach contents are inhaled into the lungs, leading to infection.
  • Hospital-acquired
  • Community-acquired

Describe the symptoms of pneumonia.

  • Cough (may produce green, yellow, or bloody mucus)
  • Shortness of breath or difficulty breathing
  • Chest pain, especially when breathing deeply or coughing
  • Fever, chills, or sweating
  • Fatigue or weakness
  • Confusion or changes in mental status (especially in older adults)
  • Loss of appetite
  • Bluish lips or fingertips (due to lack of oxygen in severe cases)

Describe the general prognosis of pneumonia.

  • Most healthy individuals recover with appropriate treatment
  • Severe pneumonia can lead to complications such as respiratory failure, sepsis, or lung abscesses, particularly in high-risk groups
  • Early diagnosis and treatment improve outcomes significantly

Pneumonia.

Acute Respiratory Failure.

What is acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)?

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a type of respiratory failure characterised by rapid onset of widespread inflammation in the lungs

Describe the epidemiology of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).

The prevalence of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is ~6-7 per 100,000 = 600-700 people/yr in UK

Recall the mortality rate of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).

30-40%

Recall three components in the Berlin definition of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).

  • Timing — Within 1 week of a known clinical insult or new or worsening respiratory symptoms
  • Chest imaging — Bilateral opacities - not fully explained by pleural effusions, lobar/lung collapse, or nodules — opacities = increased density (compared to air)
  • Origin of oedema — Respiratory failure not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload — Need objective assessment (e.g., echocardiography) to exclude hydrostatic oedema if no risk factor present — i.e oedema due to hydrostatic pressure
  • Oxygenation Severity PaO₂/FiO₂ Ratio Interpretation Mild 200–300 mmHg (with PEEP ≥5) Moderate 100–200 mmHg (with PEEP ≥5) Severe ≤100 mmHg (with PEEP ≥5)

Berlin Definition Of Ards.

What is meant by the heterogenous disease presentation acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)?

  • This refers to the fact that ARDS can manifest with a wide range of clinical features, severities, and responses to treatment
  • This variability stems from differences in the underlying causes, the extent and distribution of lung injury, and individual patient factors

Recall three pulmonary causes of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).

  • Aspiration — Aspiration induces a strong inflammatory response in the lungs
  • Trauma
  • Burns: Inhalation
  • Surgery
  • Drug Toxicity

Recall three extra-pulmonary causes of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).

  • Trauma
  • Pancreatitis
  • Burns
  • Transfusion

  • BM transplant
  • Drug Toxicity

Describe how burns can lead to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).

  • Severe burns cause widespread tissue damage, releasing pro- inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6) and chemokines into the bloodstream
  • This systemic inflammatory response increases capillary permeability throughout the body, including the lungs
  • The resulting fluid leakage into alveoli and interstitial spaces disrupts gas exchange, a hallmark of ARDS

Describe how surgery can lead to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).

  • Tissue trauma during surgery triggers the release of pro- inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1) and chemokines
  • This inflammatory response can lead to systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), increasing capillary permeability, including in the lungs, and contributing to pulmonary oedema

Describe how drug toxicity can lead to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).

Drug toxicity can lead to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)

through direct and indirect mechanisms that damage the lungs and disrupt the alveolar-capillary barrier

Certain drugs may induce the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-alpha, IL-6 and IL-1 and can also trigger eosinophilic activation

Describe how pancreatitis can lead to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).

  • Pancreatitis causes activation of inflammatory cascades with the release of: — Cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6). — Proteases and reactive oxygen species (ROS).
  • These inflammatory mediators spread through the bloodstream, causing widespread endothelial activation and increased capillary permeability, including in the lungs.
  • The result is non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, a hallmark of ARDS.

Describe how transfusion can lead to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).

  • Transfusion-Related Acute Lung Injury (TRALI) is a common cause of ARDS
  • Transfusion introduces bioactive substances (i.e antibodies, cytokines) that activate neutrophils
  • These activated neutrophils release reactive oxygen species (ROS) and proteolytic enzymes
  • They can then cause pulmonary endothelial and alveolar epithelial damage, resulting in ARDS via the increased pulmonary capillary permeability

Describe how bone marrow transplants can lead to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).

  • Generally the same process is behind it all:
  • Inflammatory mediators spread through the bloodstream, causing widespread endothelial activation and increased capillary permeability, including in the lungs → ARDS
  • In patients with bone marrow transplants, the inflammation can be triggered by factors related to the transplant process such as graft- versus-host disease, infections, idiopathic pneumonia syndrome etc.

What is acute lung injury?

  • Acute lung injury is a clinical condition characterised by acute inflammation of the lungs, leading to increased pulmonary vascular permeability, alveolar damage, and impaired gas exchange
  • ARDS is a severe form of acute lung injury

Acute Lung Injury.

Recall four in-vivo pieces of evidence of immune involvement in acute lung injury.

  • TNF signalling implicated — i.e. in a mouse without TNFR-1, there was reduced lung injury
  • Leukocyte activation and migration — alveolar macrophages → used to clean up damage — neutrophil lung migration
  • DAMP release — HMGB-1 and RAGE
  • Cytokine release — IL-6, IL-8, IL-1B, IFN-gamma
  • Cell death — necrosis in lung biopsies — apoptotic mediators - FAS, FAS-l, BCl-2

What is TNFR-1?

  • TNFR-1 is one of the two main receptors for tumour necrosis factor- alpha (TNF-α)
  • TNF-α is a key cytokine involved in inflammation, immune regulation, and apoptosis

Recall five tried therapies for acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).

  • Steroids
  • Salbutamol — short-acting β2-adrenergic receptor agonist (SABA)
  • Surfactant
  • N-Acetylcysteine — upregulates glutathione → antioxidant effects
  • Neutrophil esterase (elastase) inhibitor
  • GM-CSF — promoting activation of alveolar macrophages (only over- activation is bad!)
  • Statins

Recall three trialling therapies for acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).

  • Mesenchymal stem cells — evidence of ex-vivo benefit
  • Keratinocyte growth factor
  • Microvesicles
  • Steroids
  • ECCO2R — Extracorporeal carbon dioxide removal (ECCO2R)

Describe the optimal position to be in for patients with acute lung injury.

  • Proning is a common technique used to optimise ventilation in patients with acute lung injury — lying on the stomach
  • It means that the effects of gravity are rendered null and the lung is equally ventilated and perfused across the entire lung

Describe two underlying distinct biological processes present in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).

  • Pulmonary vascular endothelialitis, thrombosis and angiogenesis
  • Radiological evidence of poor perfusion

What is pro-inflammatory ARDS?

This subtype of ARDS typically exhibits higher levels of systemic inflammation, with release of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-alpha being seen more commonly

Describe the difference between hyper and hypo inflammatory endotypes of ARDS.

Hyper-inflammatory endotypes of ARDS typically exhibit higher levels of systemic inflammation, worsened lung injury, and often correlates with more severe outcomes in contrast with comparatively hypoinflammatory ARDS — i.e patients that release lower amounts of IL-6 and TNF-a typically exhibit more favourable phenotypes

Recall the three principles of intervention for ARDS.

  • Treat underlying disease
  • Provide respiratory support
  • Multiple organ support

Describe three ways we can treat the underlying disease in respiratory failure.

  • Inhaled therapies — Bronchodilators — Pulmonary vasodilators
  • Steroids
  • Antibiotics
  • Anti-virals
  • Drugs — Pyridostigmine — acetylcholinesterase inhibitor — Plasma exchange — IViG — Rituximab — autoimmune suppressant

Describe three ways we can provide respiratory support for respiratory failure.

  • Physiotherapy — position them in a way they can cough
  • Oxygen
  • Nebulisers
  • High flow oxygen — up to 60L oxygen
  • Non-invasive ventilation — i.e PEEP
  • Mechanical ventilation
  • Extra-corporeal support — takes over function of heart and lungs

What is a nebuliser?

  • A nebuliser is a medical device used to deliver medication directly into the lungs in the form of a fine mist or aerosol
  • It is useful for patients who have trouble using their inhalers for one reason or another

Nebuliser.

What is extra-corporeal support?

Extracorporeal support refers to medical procedures or devices that provide temporary assistance to vital organs by functioning outside the body — i.e "extra-corporeal," = "outside the body"

Describe three ways we can offer multiple organ support for respiratory failure.

  • Cardiovascular support — Fluids — Vasopressors — Inotropes — Pulmonary vasodilators
  • Renal support — Haemofiltration — Haemodialysis
  • Immune therapies — Plasma exchange — Convalescent plasma

What is a vasopressor?

  • A vasopressor is a type of medication that causes vasoconstriction and increases blood pressure
  • They work by stimulating alpha-1 adrenoceptors in the smooth muscle of blood vessels and beta-1 adrenoceptors in the heart

What is an inotrope?

An inotrope is a type of medication that alters (increase or decrease) the contractility of the heart muscle ( myocardium ) to affect the strength or force of its contractions

What is convalescent plasma?

  • Convalescent plasma is plasma collected from individuals who have recovered from an infection and have developed antibodies against the pathogen that caused the disease
  • This plasma is used to help others fight the same infection, particularly those who are severely ill or at high risk of complications

What is PEEP?

  • PEEP stands for Positive End-Expiratory Pressure, which is a mode of mechanical ventilation used to help keep the lungs inflated at the end of exhalation
  • It helps to keep the lungs inflated, which prevents alveolar collapse, commonly observed in conditions such as ARDS, pneumonia and pulmonary oedema
  • This recruitment of collapsed alveoli increases the surface area available for gas exchange, leading to improved oxygenation

What is extracorporeal membrane oxygenation?

  • Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (ECMO) is a life-saving technique used to provide prolonged cardiac and respiratory support to patients whose heart and lungs are severely damaged and cannot function adequately on their own
  • It works by temporarily taking over the function of the heart and/or lungs, allowing time for healing or recovery

Describe how respiratory support evolves with the severity of ARDS.

  • At the lower end of the spectrum, treatment is centred more around conservative fluid management and low volume ventilation, with a slight focus on PEEP
  • Then, prone positioning becomes a factor
  • In more severe cases, inhaled pulmonary vasodilators and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation can be used to treat ARDS
  • As severity of ARDS increases, the magnitude of PEEP also increases

Describe three sequelae of ARDS.

  • Poor gas exchange — Inadequate oxygenation — Poor perfusion — Hypercapnoea
  • Increased susceptibility to infection — Sepsis
  • Inflammation — Inflammatory response
  • Systemic effects
