Overview

What is a respiratory infection?

A respiratory infection is an illness caused by an infectious agent, such as a virus, bacteria, or other pathogens, that affects the respiratory system

Describe the frequency of respiratory infection in adults.

The average adult has 4-6 respiratory infections per year — ‹1% result in more severe disease, i.e most of them are mild

Describe the severity of respiratory infection in adults.

50% of these respiratory infections usually result in a GP visit — There is also a ‹0.005% infection-fatality rate

Describe the distinction between the upper and lower respiratory tracts.

  • The upper respiratory tract refers to the areas above the trachea, such as the nasal cavity, pharynx and larynx
  • The lower respiratory tract refers to the areas below the trachea, such as the bronchi, bronchioles, alveoli and the lungs

Upper Vs Lower Respiratory Tract.

Recall three symptoms of upper respiratory infection.

  • A cough
  • Sneezing
  • A runny or stuffy nose
  • A sore throat
  • Headache

Recall three symptoms of lower respiratory infection.

  • A “productive” cough - phlegm
  • Muscle aches
  • Wheezing
  • Breathlessness
  • Fever
  • Fatigue

What is wheezing?

  • Wheezing is a high-pitched whistling sound that occurs when air flows through narrowed or obstructed airways during breathing
  • It is most commonly heard during exhalation

What is pneumonia?

  • Pneumonia is a lung infection that causes inflammation in the air sacs (alveoli) of one or both lungs
  • These air sacs may fill with fluid or pus, leading to symptoms such as difficulty breathing, chest pain, fever, and coughing

Recall three symptoms of pneumonia.

  • Chest pain — infection of the pleura → pain
  • Blue tinting of the lips (cyanosis) — lack of sufficient oxygen in the blood
  • Severe fatigue
  • High fever

Describe the geographical impact of respiratory infection.

  • Respiratory infection resulted in an estimated 5 million deaths annually between 1990 and 2015
  • ~3 million deaths annually from acute lower respiratory infections
  • Excluding pandemics, acute respiratory infections are one of the leading causes of death in the world

What are Disability Adjusted Life Years (DALYs)?

  • Disability Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) are a sum of Years of Life Lost (YLL) and Years Lost to Disability (YLP) — They are used to quantify the overall burden of disease — It represents the total number of years lost due to illness, disability, or premature death within a population
  • It is good because it is important for us to understand that illness is not only severe when it causes mortality, but certain illnesses can really worsen one’s quality of life GLOBAL BURDEN OF RESPIRATORY DISEASE (DALYs).

Why do acute lung infections only rank 4th in global deaths but 1st in global DALYs?

  • Ranking 4th in global deaths but 1st in global DALYs is indicative of the fact that acute lung infections disproportionately cause mortality in elderly and infant populations
  • In others between these ages, acute lung infections are less likely to lead to death hence why they do not rank so highly on the global deaths scale, but they still cause disability causing them to rank highly on the DALY side of things — i.e Years Lost to Disability = high
  • This distinction also highlights the significant non-fatal health burden associated with acute lung infections

Describe how the mortality burden of respiratory infections changes with age.

As age increases, the mortality burden of respiratory infections also increases

Describe the global mortality burden of respiratory infections in adults over 70.

In adults over 70, there is a significantly higher risk of mortality from respiratory infection

Describe how pneumonia rates change with age.

  • Pneumonia rates increase with age
  • The risk of getting pneumonia dramatically increases as you pass the age of 70

Respiratory Infections In Children Under Five.

Recall the two main populations that are highly vulnerable to respiratory infection.

  • Children under 5
  • Adults over 70

Recall the five main classes of risk factor for pneumonia.

  • Demographic and lifestyle-related risk factors
  • Social risk factors
  • Medication-related risk factors
  • Medical history-related risk factors
  • Specific risk factors for certain pathogens

Recall three demographic and lifestyle-related risk factors for pneumonia.

  • Age — ‹2 years and ›65 years
  • Cigarette smoking
  • Excessive alcohol consumption

Recall three social risk factors for pneumonia.

  • Contact with children aged ‹15 years — they are exposed to more respiratory infections
  • Poverty
  • Overcrowding

Recall three medication-related risk factors for pneumonia.

  • Inhaled corticosteroids — immunosuppression
  • Immunosuppresants (e.g steroids)
  • Proton pump inhibitors

Describe how proton-pump inhibitors (PPIs) are a risk factor for pneumonia.

  • Stomach acid serves as a natural barrier against pathogens ingested with food or saliva
  • Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) reduce stomach acidity, allowing harmful bacteria to survive and potentially migrate to the respiratory tract through aspiration
  • This increases the risk of aspiration pneumonia — i.e foreign material such as food, liquid, saliva, or vomit, is inhaled (aspirated) into the lungs

Recall three medical history-related risk factors for pneumonia.

  • COPD, Asthma
  • Heart disease
  • Liver disease
  • Diabetes mellitus
  • HIV, Malignancy, Hyposplenism
  • Complement or Ig (antibody) deficiencies
  • Risk factors for aspiration
  • Previous pneumonia
  • NOTE: Comorbidities in general are a risk factor for developing pneumonia

Recall three specific risk factors for certain pathogens causing pneumonia.

  • Geographical variations
  • Animal contact — zoonotic origins
  • Healthcare contacts

What is asthma?

  • Asthma is a chronic respiratory condition that affects the airways in the lungs, making them inflamed, swollen, and more sensitive to certain triggers
  • This inflammation causes the airways to narrow, leading to difficulty in breathing

What is the major cause of asthma hospitalisation?

  • Asthma exacerbations
  • NOTE: Hospitalisation due to exacerbation is a major risk factor (predictor) of asthma mortality.

What is the major cause of asthma exacerbations?

Respiratory infection — Mainly viral

What is the difference between a pandemic and endemic?

  • A pandemic is a global outbreak of a disease that spreads across multiple countries or continents, typically affecting a large number of people. — It typically causes high levels of mortality and morbidity
  • A disease is considered endemic when it is consistently present in a specific population, geographic area, or region at predictable levels

Pneumonia.

What is the CRB/CURB-65 scoring system in pneumonia?

  • The CRB-65 and CURB-65 scoring systems are clinical tools used to assess the severity of (potential) bacterial pneumonia
  • It is used in both primary care and hospital settings

Recall the components of the CRB/CURB-65 scoring system is used in a clinical setting.

  • Confusion
  • Respiratory rate – ›30 breaths/min
  • Blood pressure - ‹90 systolic and/or 60 mmHg diastolic
  • 65 - 65 years old or older
  • In hospital add
  • Urea - ›7 mmol/L — i.e secondary to increased protein breakdown, hypoperfusion of kidneys

Describe how the CRB/CURB-65 scoring system is used in a clinical setting.

  • 0 = Low severity — likely suitable for home treatment + antibiotics
  • 1-2 = Moderate severity — consider hospital referral
  • 3-4 = High severity — urgent hospital admission, empirical antibiotics

What are empirical antibiotics?

Empirical antibiotics are antimicrobial drugs prescribed to treat an infection before the exact causative microorganism and its antibiotic susceptibility are identified

Crb/Curb-65 Scoring System.

Recall the two main ways pneumonia can be acquired.

  • Community acquired pneumonia (CAP)
  • Hospital acquired pneumonia (HAP) — Includes ventilator associated pneumonia

What is community acquired pneumonia (CAP)?

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is a lung infection that develops in individuals who have not been recently hospitalised or had significant exposure to healthcare settings

Recall three agents that cause community acquired pneumonia (CAP).

  • Streptococcus pneumoniae (40-50%)
  • Mycoplasma pneumoniae
  • Staphylococcus aureus
  • Chlamydia pneumoniae
  • Haemophilus Influenzae

What is hospital acquired pneumonia (HAP)?

Hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP) is a lung infection that occurs 48 hours or more after hospital admission, and was not present or incubating (developing) at the time of admission

Recall three agents that cause hospital acquired pneumonia.

  • Staphylococcus aureus
  • Psuedomonas aeruginosa
  • Klebsiella species
  • E. coli
  • Acinetobacter spp.
  • Enterobacter spp.

What is ventilator acquired pneumonia?

Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) is a type of hospital- acquired pneumonia (HAP) that develops 48 hours or more after endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation

Recall three agents that cause ventilator acquired pneumonia.

  • Psuedomonas aeruginosa (25%)
  • Staphylococcus aureus (20%)
  • Enterobacter

Streptococcus Pneumoniae In Action.

Describe the difference between typical and atypical pneumonia.

  • Typical and atypical bacterial pneumonias are distinguished between on the basis of their causative agents
  • Typical pneumonias are caused by more common forms of bacteria, atypical pneumonias are caused by more distinct forms of bacteria
  • Typical pneumonia is also the most common type of pneumonia whereas atypical pneumonia has a slower onset of symptoms and is milder

Recall two causative agents of typical pneumonia.

  • Streptococcus pneumoniae
  • Haemophilus influenzae
  • Moraxella catarrhalis

Recall two causative agents of atypical pneumonia.

  • Mycoplasma pneumoniae
  • Chlamydia pneumoniae
  • Legionella pneumophilia

Recall the two main types of treatment for bacterial pneumonia.

  • Supportive therapy
  • Antibiotics

Describe how supportive therapy is used in pneumonia.

  • Oxygen
  • Fluids
  • Analgesia
  • Nebulised saline — to reduce airway irritation
  • Chest physiotherapy
  • NOTE: These are more short-term things, used to alleviate and improve symptoms in the moment

Describe the different antibiotics that are used in pneumonia.

  • Penicillins — i.e amoxicillin — beta lactams that bind proteins in the bacterial cell wall to prevent transpeptidation (formation of peptide bonds within cell wall)
  • Macrolides — clarithromycin – bind to the bacterial ribosome to prevent protein synthesis

What is the structural role of peptidoglycan in bacterial cells?

Peptidoglycan allows bacterial cells to take up water via osmosis and not burst by being able to withstand the pressure

What is the function of D-alanyl-D-alanine transpeptidase (penicillin- binding-protein)?

It is used to allow the monomers of the peptidoglycan wall to stick together and maintain its strength.

Describe the mechanism used by amoxicillin on bacteria.

  • Amoxicillin (like penicillin) is a beta-lactam
  • It has a beta-lactam ring which is used to bind to the serine residue in the active site of penicillin binding protein, and inhibit its action.
  • This means the bacteria cannot produce its peptidoglycan cell wall as the enzyme has been inactivated and so it bursts under high osmotic pressure

Describe how macrolides work.

  • Macrolides are a class of antibiotics that primarily work by inhibiting bacterial protein synthesis
  • They do this by binding to the 50S ribosomal subunit, blocking peptide elongation
  • This inhibition of protein synthesis means important enzymes and structural proteins are not produced which prevents replication and survival of bacteria
  • NOTE: Human ribosomes (80S) are structurally distinct from bacterial ribosomes (70S), so macrolides selectively target bacterial protein synthesis without affecting human cells.

Recall an example of a macrolide antibiotic.

Clarithromycin

Describe how we know which antibiotic to use to treat pneumonia.

  • Antibiotics are administered to individuals with pneumonia based on their CURB-65 score
  • Local guidelines are usually followed, however generally the higher the CURB score, the more likely it is we revert to combination therapy

Bacterial Pneumonia Treatment Guidelines.

What is the key to increasing the success of antibiotics?

  • Time to administration — for every hour in septic shock, survival rate is reduced by 7.9%
  • Choosing the right antibiotic — Penicillins for typical CAPs, Macrolides for atypical CAPs — Both can be used when unsure to ensure coverage

Can bacterial pneumonia be caught?

Possible Origins Of Bacterial Pneumonia.

What is the human microbiome?

This describes the ~100 trillion microbial cells that populate our bodies at every barrier surface

What is a microbiota?

Microbiota describes the ecological communities of microbes found inside multi-cellular organisms

What is meant by the term “commensal”?

  • This describes microbes that live in a “symbiotic” relationship with their host
  • They provide vital nutrients to the host in the presence of a suitable ecological niche

What is an opportunistic pathogen?

A microbe that takes advantage of a change in conditions (often immunosuppression)

What is a pathobiont?

A microbe that is normally commensal, but if found in the wrong environment (e.g. anatomical site) can cause pathology.

Why does the outcome of viral infections vary between individuals?

  • This is due to the interaction between a virus and a host
  • A virus can have different effects on a host depending on how the host deals with it
  • If the host response is good and deals with the infection well, then the symptoms will be far less severe and prognosis would be better
  • However, if the host response is too excessive, it could trigger cellular inflammation which causes the same effects as a viral pathogen — Likewise, an inadequate host response would lead to a bad prognosis

What is the host-pathogen balance?

The host-pathogen balance describes how the potency of the host response as well as the antagonising pathogen plays a part in determining the outcome of infection — i.e there is a dynamic interaction between a pathogen and its host

Describe how a poor host-pathogen balance can lead to bad prognosis of respiratory infection.

An extreme host response can lead to cellular inflammation and epithelial damage which can result in: — Airway narrowing — Fluid and mucus build-up in the airways and parenchyma — Damage to the gas exchange surfaces

Describe the difference between a viral and bacterial respiratory infection.

The viruses that cause respiratory infection are not pathobionts — They all come from external sources, unlike bacteria

Influenza Mechanism.

Describe where H1N1 Influenza A has its effects to cause infection.

Haemagglutinin binds 𝛂2,6 sialic acids

Describe where H5N1 avian flu has its effects to cause infection.

Haemagglutinin binds 𝛂2,3 sialic acids

Describe where the common cold (rhinovirus) has its effects to cause infection.

  • Different rhinoviruses use different receptors to enter cells and replicate
  • Major group – ICAM-1
  • Minor group – LDLR

Describe where SARS-CoV-2 has its effects to cause infection.

The spike (S) protein of SARS-CoV-2 binds Angiotensin converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) in the nasal epithelium or Type II pneumocytes — ACE2 is important for maintaining lung function (expressed on pneumocytes)

Describe how virus binding is selective throughout the respiratory tract.

Most respiratory viruses can infect cells throughout the respiratory tract, but tend to preferentially adapt to bind cells of the upper respiratory tract if they have existed in humans for a prolonged time

What is RSV?

RSV stands for Respiratory Syncytial Virus, a highly contagious virus that primarily affects the respiratory system

Describe the global health burden of RSV.

  • RSV is the leading cause of infant hospitalisation in the developed world
  • 50% of children infected have it in year 1 of life — All children infected have it by year 3.

Recall three clinical features of RSV.

  • Nasal flaring
  • Chest wall retractions
  • Hypoxemia and cyanosis
  • Croupy cough — barking cough, like a dog’s bark
  • Expiratory wheezing, prolonged expiration, rales and rhonchi
  • Tachypnoea with apnoeic episodes
  • NOTE: 1% of patients develop severe bronchiolitis

What are rales and rhonchi?

  • Rales and rhonchi are terms used to describe different types of abnormal lung sounds heard through a stethoscope
  • Rales are short, popping or crackling sounds heard during inhalation
  • Rhonchi are low-pitched, continuous sounds that resemble a snoring or gurgling noise

Recall two risk factors for RSV.

  • Premature birth
  • Congenital heart and lung disease RSV.

Recall a physical barrier of the upper airway that helps protect against infection.

Hairs and cilia – particulate capture

Recall two physical/chemical barriers of the epithelial lumen that help protect against infection.

  • Fluid lining the lumen — filled with antimicrobial peptides and proteases
  • Mucociliary escalator — cilia moves mucus up and out of the lungs
  • Epithelial barrier

Describe how immune fortification is used to protect against infection.

  • Innate immune responses
  • Resident immune cells
  • Recruited immune cells

Describe two ways the respiratory epithelium provides a physical barrier to infection.

  • Tight junctions – prevents systemic infection
  • Mucous lining and cilial clearance – prevents attachment, clears particulates
  • Antimicrobials – recognise, neutralise and/or degrade microbes and their products
  • Pathogen recognition receptors – recognise pathogens either outside or inside a cell
  • Interferon pathways – activated by viral infection. Promotes upregulation of anti-viral proteins and apoptosis
