Overview

What is a restrictive lung disease?

Restrictive lung disease is a category of respiratory disorders characterised by a reduced ability of the lungs to expand and fill with air

Recall the two ways the volume of the lungs can be restricted.

  • Intrinsic lung disease
  • Extrinsic lung disease

Describe how intrinsic lung diseases restrict lung disease.

Intrinsic lung diseases describe conditions that affect the lung parenchyma directly, leading to impaired lung function — These diseases typically involve inflammation, scarring (fibrosis), or damage to the alveoli

Recall an intrinsic lung disease.

Interstitial lung disease (ILD)

Describe how extrinsic lung diseases restrict lung disease.

Extrinsic lung diseases describe conditions that affect structures separate from the lungs, however the ability of the lungs to fill and expand is still impaired

Recall an extrinsic lung disease.

  • Pleural dysfunction
  • Chest wall dysfunction
  • Neuromuscular dysfunction — decreased ability of respiratory muscles to inflate / deflate the lungs

Recall four important cellular components of the lung parenchyma.

  • Alveolar type 1 epithelial cells (Type I pneumocytes)
  • Alveolar type 2 epithelial cells (Type II pneumocytes)
  • Fibroblasts
  • Alveolar macrophages

Describe the role of alveolar type 1 epithelial cells.

  • The primary role of Type 1 cells is to facilitate the diffusion of oxygen (O₂) into the blood and carbon dioxide (CO₂) out of the blood
  • They provide a gas exchange surface of ~70m2

Describe the role of alveolar type 2 epithelial cells.

  • Type 2 cells produce surfactant, a substance that reduces surface tension and prevents alveolar collapse
  • Type 2 cells are also stem cells and can differentiate into Type 1 cells to replace damaged ones

Describe the role of fibroblasts.

  • Fibroblasts are the cells that produce extracellular matrix (ECM), such as Type 1 collagen
  • This extracellular matrix (ECM) is important for tissue development, providing structural support as well as growth and adhesion of cells

Describe the role of alveolar macrophages.

Alveolar macrophages are used to phagocytose foreign material found within the lungs — They also phagocytose surfactant produced by Type II pneumocytes, helping to ensure there is a healthy balance

What is the interstitial space in the lungs?

The interstitial space of the lungs describes the space between the alveolar epithelium and capillary endothelium

Interstitial Space With Fibrosis.

Describe what is found in the interstitial space of the lungs.

The interstitial space contains lymphatic vessels, occasional fibroblasts and ECM

Describe the role of the interstitial space in the lungs.

  • It provides structural support to the lung and it is very thin (few micrometers thick) to facilitate gas exchange
  • The lymphatic vessels also help to drain excess fluid/cells from the lung

Describe how macrophages are linked with the lung epithelium.

Alveolar macrophages are closely linked with the alveolar epithelium, located in and around the outer layers of the lungs

Macrophages And Pulmonary Epithelium.

What is an interstitial lung disease (ILD)?

  • Interstitial lung disease (ILD) refers to a diverse group of lung disorders characterised by inflammation and/or scarring (fibrosis) of the pulmonary interstitium — i.e the space between the alveolar epithelium and capillary endothelium
  • There are over 200 recognised interstitial lung diseases (ILDs)

Recall five types of interstitial lung disease (ILD).

  • Idiopathic — IPF, NSIP, DIP (Desquamative interstitial pneumonia)
  • Autoimmune-related — RA-ILD, SSc-ILD — RA = Rheumatoid arthritis
  • Exposure-related — Hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP)
  • With cysts or airspace filling
  • Sarcoidosis
  • Others — eosinophilic pneumonia

What is sarcoidosis?

  • Sarcoidosis is a chronic, systemic inflammatory disease characterised by the formation of non-caseating (non-cheesy) granulomas (clumps of immune cells) in multiple organs, most commonly the lungs and lymph nodes
  • NOTE: Granulomas are usually physiologically useful in protecting against infection, however they are pathologically significant when they disrupt normal tissue function

Which interstitial lung disease (ILD) patterns are associated with high and low rates of survival?

  • Usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) are associated with the lowest rates of survival — i.e idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)
  • Fibrotic NSIPs (non-specific interstitial pneumonias) and similar patterns are associated with better prognosis
  • These patterns are observed on CT scans

Describe three important features of a clinical history for interstitial lung disease (ILD).

  • Progressive breathlessness
  • Non-productive cough — usually
  • Limitation in exercise tolerance
  • Symptoms of connective tissue disease? — i.e pain in joints, swelling
  • Occupational and exposure history
  • Medication history — drug induced ILD → http://www.pneumotox.com
  • Family history — up to 20% of idiopathic ILDs are familial

Describe three features seen upon clinical examination of interstitial lung disease (ILD).

  • Low oxygen saturations (resting or exertion)
  • Fine bilateral inspiratory crackles
  • Digital clubbing — common in IPF
  • (+/- features of connective tissue disease – skin, joints, muscles)

Digital Clubbing.

Describe three investigations done for interstitial lung disease (ILD).

  • Blood tests — i.e anti-nuclear antibody (ANA), rheumatoid factor (RhF), anti- citrullinated peptide (CCP) - AUTOIMMUNE
  • Pulmonary function tests
  • 6-minute walk test (6MWT) —

how far they can walk in 6 minutes

  • NOTE: SpO2 ≤ 88% associated with increased risk of death
  • High-resolution CT scan (HRCT)
  • Invasive testing — Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) — Surgical lung biopsy (2-4% mortality)

Describe how the physiology of the lungs changes in restrictive lung disease.

  • Compliance decreases in restrictive lung diseases (stiffer) — This means the lung is less willing to succumb to forces that want to make it expand
  • This results in reduced DLCO, reduced FVC and reduced arterial PO2

What is diffusing capacity of lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO)?

  • This is a pulmonary test which measures how well carbon monoxide (and hence oxygen) can pass from the lungs into the bloodstream
  • The patient inhales a small amount of CO mixed with other gases

After holding the breath for a brief period (about 10 seconds), the patient exhales, and the amount of CO remaining in the exhaled air is measured

(see lecture diagram / notes)

Describe how DLCO is impacted in restrictive lung disease.

DLCO is reduced in restrictive lung diseases

Describe how the FEV1/FVC ratio changes in restrictive lung disease.

  • FVC reduces in all restrictive lung diseases
  • Hence, FEV1/FVC ratio is usually normal or elevated

Fev1/Fevc Changes In Restrictive Lung Disease.

What is high-resolution CT scanning (HRCT)?

  • High-resolution CT (HRCT) scanning is a specialised type of computed tomography (CT) imaging that provides detailed, high- quality images of the lungs and chest structures
  • It is useful because changes to the lung in interstitial lung disease occur at a very microscopic level, so HRCT allows visualisation of these changes
  • NOTE: HRCT, like CT, also uses X-rays

Describe how high-resolution CT scanning is used in the diagnosis of interstitial lung disease (ILD).

  • High - density substances (e.g. bone) absorb more x-rays and appear whiter
  • Low - density substances (e.g. air) absorb few x-rays and appear darker
  • HRCT also helps us to produce images in the axial, sagittal and coronal planes

Recall three HRCT patterns in restrictive lung disease.

  • Usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP)
  • Non-specific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP)
  • Organising pneumonia

Describe how these HRCT patterns vary in restrictive lung disease.

  • Usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) — changes are at peripheries of the lung and sub-pleural areas, lung bases — honeycombing of lung, scarring
  • Non-specific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP) — a more inflammatory pattern, no honeycombing
  • Organising pneumonia — another inflammatory pattern

Recall five members of the multidisciplinary team (MDT) involved in the management of interstitial lung disease (ILD).

  • Physiotherapist
  • Occupational therapist
  • Radiologist
  • Pulmonologist
  • Respiratory physiologist
  • Pathologist
  • Rheumatologist

Describe the roles of each of these multidisciplinary team (MDT) members.

  • Physiotherapist/Occupational therapist — supportive care, pulmonary rehabilitation
  • Clinical nurse specialist (CNS) — supportive care, symptom management, palliative care
  • Radiologist — interprets and finds patterns on HRCT
  • Pulmonologist — interprets medical history, diagnosis and treatment of ILDs
  • Respiratory physiologist — interprets lung volume and gas exchange analysis on PFTs, assesses disease progression
  • Pathologist — interprets histopathological features of lung biopsy
  • Rheumatologist — reviews serological tests, diagnosis and treatment of CTD-ILDs

Multidisciplinary Diagnosis Of Ild.

Describe the general principles of early stage interstitial lung disease (ILD) management.

  • Pharmacological therapy (for inflammatory forms of ILD, i.e IPF) — immunosuppressive drugs, antifibrotics
  • Clinical trials
  • Patient education
  • Vaccination
  • Smoking cessation
  • Treatment of co-morbidities — i.e gastroesophageal reflux, obstructive sleep apnoea, pulmonary hypertension
  • Pulmonary rehabilitation

Describe the general principles of late stage interstitial lung disease (ILD) management.

  • Supplemental oxygen
  • Lung transplantation
  • Palliative care — symptom management, end-of-life care

What is idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)?

  • Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF) is a chronic, progressive, and ultimately fatal interstitial lung disease (ILD) characterised by scarring (fibrosis) of the lung parenchyma
  • It is a disease of unknown cause

Describe the prevalence of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) in the UK.

6,000 new cases are diagnosed each year

Which gender is affected more by idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)?

  • Men
  • Its incidence also increases significantly with age, as most sufferers are ›60 years old

Describe how idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) affects FVC.

  • Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), like any restrictive lung disease, decreases FVC
  • The average decline in forced vital capacity (FVC) is 150 – 200mls / year

Describe how exacerbations can affect one’s experience of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF).

  • Acute exacerbations occur in 5-15% of IPF patients
  • The median survival for an individual with an exacerbation is 3-4 months and in-hospital mortality is ~50%

Describe the general prognosis of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF).

  • On average without treatment, the average individual lives for 3-5 years post-IPF diagnosis — This is a fairly poor prognosis
  • However, there is a lot of variability as individuals can have worse prognoses or even better especially with treatments such as antifibrotics

Recall three predisposing factors of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF).

  • Genetic susceptibility — MUC5B (mucin production), DSP
  • Environmental triggers — smoke, viruses, pollutants, dusts
  • Cellular ageing — telomere attrition, senescence

Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (Ipf).

What initiates idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)?

IPF is initiated by alveolar epithelial injury

Describe the pathophysiology of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF).

  • Environmental triggers, cellular ageing and genetic susceptibility contribute to Type I and Type II alveolar cell damage
  • This damage leads to increased collagen deposition which leads to the scarring commonly seen in IPF

Describe the visible changes that occur in relation to histopathology in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF).

  • The interstitium would proliferate and thicken to cause areas within the lung called honeycomb cysts — Proliferating fibroblasts contribute to this thickening
  • Spatial heterogeneity also describes the concept of these abnormal areas of lung being located near and around the normal areas of lung

Ipf On A Ct Scan.

Describe the impact of immunosuppression on the progression of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF).

  • Immunosuppression is actually harmful in IPF
  • This was discovered via a randomised, placebo-controlled trial which saw anti-inflammatory drugs increase the risk of death and hospitalisation in IPF

What is an antifibrotic?

Antifibrotic drugs are medications designed to slow down or prevent the excessive formation of fibrous (scar) tissue in organs, particularly in the lungs

Recall two antifibrotics used to treat IPF.

  • Nintedanib - tyrosine kinase inhibitor — reduces fibroblast activation and collagen deposition
  • Pirfenidone – a pyridine compound

Describe the impact of antifibrotics on the progression of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF).

Antifibrotics slow disease progression in IPF but do not cure the disease

Antifibrotics.

What is hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP)?

Hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) is an interstitial lung disease (ILD) caused by an immune-mediated response in susceptible and sensitised individuals to inhaled environmental antigens

What is pneumonia?

  • Pneumonia is a lung infection that causes inflammation in the air sacs (alveoli) of one or both lungs
  • These air sacs may fill with fluid or pus, leading to symptoms such as difficulty breathing, chest pain, fever, and coughing

What causes hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP)?

  • Inhaled environmental antigens
  • The disease affects the small airways and parenchyma — Small airways = bronchi, bronchioles and alveoli

Describe the classification of hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP).

  • Acute hypersensitivity pneumonitis
  • Chronic hypersensitivity pneumonitis

Describe the difference between acute and chronic hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP).

  • Acute HP is intermittent and requires high-level exposure — It has an abrupt symptom onset and presents as a flu-like syndrome 4-12 hrs after exposure
  • Chronic HP is long-term and only requires low-level exposure — It can either be non-fibrotic (purely inflammatory) or fibrotic – associated with higher mortality

Describe the epidemiology of hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP).

  • Hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) is rare — It occurs in 1 in 100,000 individuals
  • The mean age onset is 50 – 60 yrs and it is less frequent in smokers
  • NOTE: Those with HP have a 3x higher mortality risk compared to the general population

Describe the pathophysiology of hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP).

  • Hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) is driven by immunological dysregulation
  • Environmental antigen exposure triggers the inflammatory response
  • The inflammatory response is mediated by T-helper cells and antigen-specific immunoglobulin (Ig) G antibodies
  • The accumulation of lymphocytes and formation of granulomas is a driver of hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP)

Pathophysiology Of Hypersensitivity Pneumonitis (Hp).

What is an environmental antigen?

An environmental antigen is any substance present in the surrounding environment that can trigger an immune response when inhaled, ingested, or otherwise encountered.

Describe the usual steps taken in the diagnosis of hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP).

  • Detailed exposure history – antigen not identified in ~50% — i.e when have you been away from environmental antigens? did symptoms improve?
  • Inspiratory ‘squeaks’ on auscultation - caused by the coexisting bronchiolitis
  • Specific circulating IgG antibodies (serum precipitins) to potential antigens
  • HRCT
  • Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) — lymphocyte count ›30%

Describe the treatment of hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP).

  • Complete antigen removal / avoidance is crucial
  • Corticosteroids often used
  • Immunosuppressants — i.e mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) and azathioprine used but poor evidence base
  • Progressive, fibrotic HP → Nintedanib (antifibrotic)

Describe the long-term effects of corticosteroids.

  • Corticosteroids can increase one’s risk of developing conditions such as diabetes, osteoporosis and hypertension
  • Corticosteroids are also immunosuppressants, so they can increase one’s risk of serious infection

What is systemic sclerosis (SSc)?

Systemic sclerosis (SSc) is an autoimmune connective tissue disease characterised by immune dysregulation and progressive fibrosis that affects skin, with variable internal organ involvement

Describe the prevalence of systemic sclerosis (SSc).

Systemic sclerosis (SSc) is rare - there are 10 – 50 cases per 1,000,000 per year

Recall the demographic group systemic sclerosis (SSc) is most common in.

Young, middle-aged women

Describe how systemic sclerosis is related to interstitial lung disease (ILD).

  • Interstitial lung disease (ILD) develops in 30-40% and is the most common cause of death from systemic sclerosis (SSc)
  • It has a 10-year mortality of 40%

Recall three risk factors for a worse survival rate from systemic sclerosis (SSc).

  • Male
  • Older age
  • Smoker
  • ›20% extent on HRCT
  • FVC ‹70%

Recall the two types of systemic sclerosis (SSc).

  • Limited cutaneous SSc — Pulmonary hypertension is more common
  • Diffuse cutaneous SSc (more extensive) — ILD is more common
  • NOTE: Systemic sclerosis (SSc) is classified on the basis of the amount of skin involved

Describe three clinical features of systemic sclerosis (SSc).

  • Sclerodactyly
  • Raynaud’s
  • Telengiactasias
  • Digital ulcer
  • Abnormal nailfold capillaroscopy

What is sclerodactyly?

Sclerodactyly is a condition characterised by thickening and tightening of the skin on the fingers (and sometimes the toes), which leads to a loss of flexibility and restricted movement

What is Raynaud’s?

  • Changes in the colour of the fingertips and pain associated with cold weather or temperature change
  • ALTERNATIVE: Raynaud's phenomenon is a condition in which blood flow to the fingers, toes, and sometimes other extremities is temporarily reduced or blocked due to vasospasm (narrowing) of small blood vessels in response to cold or stress

What is telengiactasias?

Telangiectasias are small, dilated blood vessels near the surface of the skin or mucous membranes, often appearing as red, purple, or blue spider-like lesions

What is abnormal nailfold capillaroscopy?

Abnormal nailfold capillaroscopy refers to changes in the tiny blood vessels (capillaries) at the base of the nails (nailfolds) that are visible under a microscope, which can indicate an underlying health condition.
