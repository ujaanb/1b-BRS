Overview

What is the function of the auricles of the heart?

  • To act as a supporting vessel for the atria of the heart
  • It allows extra blood to flow into it in cases of atrial overflow, helping to relieve atrial pressure — i.e during exercise when there is increased venous return

What is the ligamentum arteriosum?

The ligamentum arteriosum is an embryological remnant that connects the aortic arch to the pulmonary trunk at its point of bifurcation. — It is a remnant of the ductus arteriosum

What is the septum of the heart?

The wall of tissue within the heart that separates the left side from the right side

Describe the structure of the pericardial sac.

  • It has two layers, the serous pericardium (INNER) and fibrous pericardium (OUTER)
  • The serous pericardium can be further split into two layers — Visceral pericardium (INNER) — Parietal pericardium (OUTER)
  • It also has a pericardial cavity in between it

Describe the passage of blood through the heart.

  • Blood enters the heart at the right atrium
  • Blood passes from right atrium → right ventricle
  • Blood passes from right ventricle →pulmonary artery to lungs
  • Blood passes from lungs to pulmonary vein → left atrium
  • Blood passes from left atrium → left ventricle
  • Blood passes from left ventricle → aorta → rest of the body

What is a cusp in the heart?

A cusp in the heart refers to a flap of tissue within a heart valve that opens and closes to regulate blood flow

How are the cusps connected to ventricular walls?

  • The cusps are connected to chordae tendineae which then connect to the papillary muscles within the wall
  • These papillary muscles contract, and since the cusps are connected to them via chordae tendinae, the valve is pulled downwards, meaning that blood cannot flow into the atria by mistake

Cusps Of The Heart Valves.

Recall when the papillary muscles of the heart contract.

During ventricular systole

Describe how the papillary muscles interact with the valves of the heart.

  • When papillary muscles contract, since the cusps are connected to them via chordae tendinae, the valve is pulled downwards
  • However, the primary factor that drives the opening and closing of the heart valves is the pressure changes that occur between the ventricles and atria

When the papillary muscles contract, intraventricular pressure increases simultaneously and so the AV valves naturally close as a result of this pressure change

NOTE: AV valve closing means it goes in the superior

What is the function of the cusps of the heart valves?

To allow the valves to open and close during the flow of blood

through the various chambers of the heart

The cusps form the actual bodies of the heart valves, and blood flows through the middle of them — i.e tricuspid, bicuspid valves

What are pectinate muscles?

The pectinate muscles are muscular ridges located in the atria of the heart, specifically in the right atrium and auricle

What is the function of the pectinate muscles?

The pectinate muscles help in increasing the surface area in the right atrium

Pectinate Muscles.

What are the chordae tendinae?

Chordae tendineae are cord-like tendons that connect the papillary muscles to the tricuspid valve and mitral valve in the heart

What is the function of the chordae tendinae?

They help prevent the valve from prolapsing back into the atria during the ventricular systole —

When the ventricle contracts, the AV valve closes and the chordae tendinae help to prevent prolapse of the valve

(see lecture diagram / notes)

Chordae Tendinae.

What are trabeculae carneae?

Trabeculae carnae are irregular muscular columns that project from the inner surface of the right and left ventricles of the heart

What is the function of trabeculae carnae?

Trabeculae carnae provide additional support to ventricular valves and help in maintaining the stroke volume and cardiac output

Trabeculae Carnae.

What are papillary muscles?

Papillary muscles are small, cone-shaped muscles located in the ventricles of the heart

Describe the function of the papillary muscles.

They attach to the cusps of the atrioventricular valves via chordae tendineae and contract to prevent inversion or prolapse of these valves on systole (or ventricular contraction)

Papillary Muscles.

What is the interventricular septum?

The interventricular septum is a muscular wall that separates the right and left ventricles of the heart

What is the function of the interventricular septum?

It is a triangular wall of cardiac tissue that comprises muscular and membranous tissue and it separates the right and left ventricles

Interventricular Septum.

Describe the difference between the pectinate muscles

and the trabeculae carneae. Key Differences Feature Pectinate Muscles Trabeculae Carneae Location Atria (mainly right Ventricles atrium) Appearance Parallel ridges Irregular, interwoven projections Function Aid in atrial contraction Prevent suction, coordinate ventricular contraction Significance Visible especially in the Cover inner ventricular right atrium walls • In summary, while both structures are muscular ridges, pectinate muscles are unique to the atria and help with atrial contraction, whereas trabeculae carneae are unique to the ventricles, preventing suction and ensuring efficient ventricular contraction

Heart Anatomy.

Recall the four phases of systole.

  • Atrial systole
  • Isovolumetric contraction
  • Rapid ejection
  • Slow ejection

Recall the three phases of diastole.

  • Isovolumetric relaxation
  • Rapid passive filling
  • Slow passive filling

What is atrial systole?

  • The first stage of systole
  • During this stage, the atria contract slightly, and more blood is added to the ventricle to ‘top up’ its volume — Sometimes referred to as “atrial kick”

What causes the P-wave normally seen on an ECG?

  • It signifies the start of atrial systole
  • It is a slight increase in pressure of the atria caused by slight contractions as a result of increased Ca2+ delivery

What is meant by ‘isovolumetric contraction’?

A contraction of something, however the volume of the contracting vessel does not change

Describe the events that occur during isovolumetric contraction.

  • The ventricles contract, increasing their pressure above that of the atria — This causes the tricuspid and bicuspid valves to close, hence we can hear the sound when using a stethoscope
  • However, there is no change in volume when the ventricles contract as the pulmonary and aortic valves are still closed
  • NOTE: Isovolumetric contraction is characterised by an increase in ventricular pressure leading to the AV (tricuspid) valve closing

What causes the QRS complex normally seen on an ECG?

Contraction of the ventricles as a result of ventricular depolarisation

What occurs to initiate rapid ejection?

Opening of the pulmonary and aortic valves caused by contraction of the ventricles, increasing their pressure above that of the aorta and pulmonary arteries

Describe the events that occur during rapid ejection.

Blood is ejected rapidly into the aorta and pulmonary arteries as a result of this ventricular contraction

What occurs to initiate reduced ejection?

Ventricles begin to relax due to ventricular repolarisation

Describe the events that occur during reduced ejection.

  • The blood flow out of the ventricles decreases
  • The pulmonary and aortic valves (semilunar valves) begin to close
  • Ventricular volume decreases more slowly

What causes the T wave normally seen on an ECG?

Ventricular repolarisation after they have finished contracting during systole

What occurs to initiate isovolumetric relaxation?

Semilunar valves close — i.e aortic valve, pulmonary valve

Describe the events that occur during isovolumetric relaxation.

  • All valves are closed
  • The ventricles are recovering from systole, they are passively relaxing and their pressure decreases passively — due to repolarisation
  • No blood is going anywhere — We hear the second heart sound hear on a stethoscope,

when the semilunar valves close

NOTE: Isovolumetric relaxation is characterised by the end of systole, when the ventricular pressure falls below the pulmonary artery/aortic pressure

What is the dichrotic notch, and what causes it?

  • This is the small increase in aortic pressure caused by the aortic valve shutting after ventricular systole
  • It marks the end of ventricular systole

Aortic Pressure - Dichrotic Notch.

Describe the events that occur during rapid passive filling.

  • Ventricular pressure decreases below atrial pressure
  • This causes the AV valves to open again and the ventricles fill with blood rapidly

Describe the events that occur during reduced passive filling.

  • Ventricular volume increases a lot more slowly
  • This phase is also known as diastasis

Why are reduced and rapid passive filling referred to as “passive filling”?

Because the ventricles are filling up without the need for any atrial contractions, unlike in atrial systole

What is the longest phase of the cardiac cycle?

Reduced passive filling

What is left ventricular end-diastolic volume?

This is the volume of blood remaining in the ventricles at the end of diastole

What is left ventricular end-systolic volume?

This is the volume of blood remaining in the ventricles at the end of systole

The Cardiac Cycle.

Recall the formula for cardiac output.

  • Cardiac output = heart rate x stroke volume
  • This will usually give you the volume of blood pumped out of the heart per minute

How can we calculate stroke volume?

Stroke volume (SV) = end diastolic volume (EDV) - end systolic volume (ESV)

Recall what is meant by ‘pulse pressure’ and how it is calculated.

  • Pulse pressure = systolic pressure - diastolic pressure
  • This is because pulse pressure is the pressure generated during every pulse in order to raise diastolic pressure to systolic pressure

Recall how mean arterial pressure can be calculated using pulse pressure.

Mean arterial pressure (MAP) = diastolic pressure + 1/3 of pulse pressure

Recall the other formula for mean arterial pressure (MAP).

  • Mean arterial pressure (MAP) = (cardiac output(CO) x Systemic vascular resistance (SVR))+ central venous pressure (CVP))
  • This is derived from the formula Pressure = Flow x Resistance using Ohm’s Law — Flow = Cardiac output, Resistance = Systemic vascular resistance — This equation highlights that the pressure required to maintain a certain flow is directly proportional to the resistance encountered

What is a healthy mean arterial pressure required to perfuse organs?

In health, a MAP ›65 mmHg represents the pressure necessary to adequately perfuse the body organs

What causes heart sounds?

Heart sounds occur due to heart valves opening and closing as the heart pumps blood

How many heart sounds are there physiologically in an adult?

  • Physiologically, there are two heart sounds
  • NOTE: In a healthy adult, S1 and S2 are typically the only heart sounds audible. S3 is considered physiological in certain populations (like younger people or athletes), but its presence in older adults often points to underlying heart conditions

What causes the first heart sound (S1)?

The first sound S1 is caused by the closure of the mitral and tricuspid (atrioventricular, AV) valves, which occurs when the ventricles contract to pump blood into the pulmonary artery and aorta

What causes the second heart sound (S2)?

The second sound (S2) is caused by the closure of the aortic and pulmonary (semilunar) valves, which occurs when the ventricles relax to receive blood from the atria after pumping blood

What causes the third heart sound (S3)?

The third sound (S3) is a low-frequency sound that occurs in early diastole, produced by rapid filling and expansion of ventricles — CHATGPT: it is caused by sudden tension of the ventricles, enough to produce sound vibrations within the myocardial wall

What causes a pathological S3?

The most common cause of pathological S3 is congestive cardiac failure

What is congestive cardiac failure?

  • Congestive cardiac failure is a condition where the heart cannot pump effectively, leading to fluid congestion in the lungs and other parts of the body
  • It is commonly the sequelae of valvular defects, coronary artery disease, hypertension and other cardiomyopathies
  • NOTE: The term “congestive” refers to the build-up of fluid that often occurs in this condition, as a failing heart can lead to congestion in the lungs and other parts of the body

What causes the fourth heart sound (S4)?

  • The fourth sound (S4) is a low-frequency sound that occurs in late diastole, produced by forceful atrial contractions forcing blood into stiff ventricles
  • Unlike S3, S4 is always pathological.

What causes the pathological S4?

The S4 sound (always pathological) usually indicates atrial hypertrophy (seen in Aortic Stenosis) or stiff ventricles (systemic hypertension, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and ischaemia)

What causes a heart murmur?

A heart murmur is caused by the turbulent blood flow through the heart valves and is generally blowing, whooshing, or rasping sound heard during a heartbeat

Recall the three types of heart murmur.

  • Systolic murmur
  • Diastolic murmur
  • Continuous murmur

What is a systolic murmur?

This type of murmur occurs when the heart is pumping blood to the rest of the body. — i.e ventricular systole

What is a diastolic murmur?

This type of murmur occurs when the heart relaxes between beats to fill up with blood.

What is a continuous murmur?

This type of murmur occurs throughout the heartbeat

Murmurs In Various Structural Heart Diseases.

What is aortic stenosis?

Aortic stenosis occurs when the aortic valve narrows and blood cannot flow normally from left ventricle → aorta

What is aortic regurgitation?

Aortic regurgitation is the leaking of the aortic valve of the heart that causes blood to flow in the reverse direction during ventricular diastole, from the aorta into the left ventricle

What is patent ductus arteriosus?

  • Patent ductus arteriosus is a medical condition in which the ductus arteriosus fails to close after birth
  • This allows oxygenated blood from the aorta to flow back to the lungs t hrough the via the pulmonary artery
  • Blood flows from the high-pressure aorta into the lower-pressure pulmonary artery, creating a left-to-right shunt. This increases blood flow to the lungs and can lead to pulmonary congestion and overloading of the left heart chambers
