Overview

What is a structural heart disease?

Structural heart diseases cover a number of defects which affect the valves/chambers of the heart and the aorta

Recall the four commonest congenital heart diseases.

  • Ventral septal defect
  • Tetralogy of Fallot (ToF)
  • Atrial septal defect
  • Coarctation of aorta

Why is the left side of the heart more prone to damage than the right?

  • This is due to the higher pressure that exists within the left side of the heart — The right side of the heart is a low-pressure system
  • This increases the likelihood of wear-and-tear on the left side of the heart

What is a cardiomyopathy?

Cardiomyopathy is a general term for diseases of the heart muscle,

where the walls of the heart chambers have become stretched, thickened or stiff What type of heart disease is aortic stenosis/regurgitation?

Valvular disease — i.e of the aortic valve

Describe and compare the epidemiology of aortic and mitral valve disease.

  • Both of these valvular diseases tend to present later in life — i.e age 64+
  • Mitral valve disease is generally more common than aortic valve disease

Epidemiology Of Aortic And Mitral Valve Disease.

What is aortic stenosis?

Aortic stenosis is a condition in which the aortic valve, the heart valve that opens to allow blood to flow from left ventricle → aorta and then to the rest of the body, becomes narrowed and stiff

Describe the epidemiology of aortic stenosis.

  • Aortic Stenosis (AS) is the most common valvular disease in the US and Europe requiring treatment
  • It is largely a disease of older people - the seventh or eighth decade of life — i.e ›70 years
  • It is the second most frequent cause for cardiac surgery

What usually precedes aortic stenosis?

Aortic stenosis is usually preceded by aortic sclerosis (aortic valve thickening without flow limitation )

What is the difference between a risk factor and a cause?

  • Risk factors may or may not always lead to the expression of a certain condition — They simply increase one’s risk of developing the condition
  • Causes are almost always directly linked to the expression of a condition

Recall four risk factors for aortic stenosis.

  • Hypertension
  • High LDL cholesterol levels
  • Smoking — increased risk of calcification and inflammation, increased oxidative stress
  • Elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) — high levels of systemic inflammation → progression of calcification and AS
  • Congenital bicuspid valves — i.e instead of tricuspid, this means it is more prone to damage
  • Chronic kidney disease (CKD)
  • Radiotherapy
  • Older age

Recall two causes of aortic stenosis.

  • Rheumatic heart disease
  • Congenital heart disease
  • Calcium build-up

Aortic Stenosis.

What is rheumatic heart disease?

Rheumatic heart disease (RHD) is a condition in which permanent damage to the heart valves occurs as a result of rheumatic fever, an inflammatory disease that can develop after a strep throat infection caused by Streptococcus pyogenes

What is congenital heart disease?

Congenital heart disease (CHD) refers to a group of structural abnormalities in the heart or blood vessels that are present from birth

Describe why high LDL cholesterol levels are a risk factor for aortic stenosis.

High LDL (low-density lipoprotein) cholesterol is a significant risk factor for aortic stenosis because it contributes to the buildup of plaque and calcification on the aortic valve, leading to its narrowing and stiffening

Describe why chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a risk factor for aortic stenosis.

  • CKD disrupts the body’s ability to activate vitamin D, which is necessary for calcium absorption and balance
  • This deficiency can lead to an overproduction of parathyroid hormone (PTH), which reabsorbs calcium from the bone → blood
  • This can lead to hypercalcaemia which can increase risk of valvular calcification

What is concentric ventricular hypertrophy?

Concentric ventricular hypertrophy (CVH) refers to the thickening (hypertrophy) of the heart's ventricular walls, particularly the left ventricle, which occurs in response to increased pressure overload — In this condition, the heart muscle becomes thicker

Describe two initial causes of fibrosis/calcification of the aortic valve.

  • Anti-Streptococcal antibodies wrongly attack valves leading to inflammation of valve endocardium — This is secondary to untreated Group A streptococcus infections
  • Wear & tear of the valve or disruption of valve endothelium

Describe the pathophysiology of aortic stenosis.

  • Anti-Streptococcal antibodies wrongly attack valves leading to inflammation of valve endocardium OR Wear & tear of the valve or disruption of valve endothelium
  • This leads to the fibrosis and calcification of aortic valve
  • Left ventricle (LV) has to contract harder to pump blood through the stenotic valve- overtime leads to concentric LV myocardial hypertrophy
  • Hypertrophic LV becomes stiff overtime and harder to fill leading to decreased cardiac output (diastolic dysfunction)
  • Pressure overload in LV leads to a backlog of blood in the left atrium

which causes it to dilate

This ultimately leads to an increase in the pressure in the lungs causing pulmonary congestion

Describe how aortic stenosis can have adverse effects on the lungs.

  • The resulting hypertrophic LV becomes stiff overtime and harder to fill leading to decreased cardiac output (diastolic dysfunction)
  • This pressure overload in LV causes a backlog of blood in the left atrium which causes it to dilate and ultimately leading to increase in pressure in the pulmonary veins which bring blood from the lungs to the heart
  • This increased pressure in the pulmonary veins forces fluid out of the blood vessels and into the lung tissues and alveoli, causing pulmonary congestion. — This can lead to impaired gas exchange, causing respiratory distress

Describe the difference between concentric and eccentric hypertrophy.

  • Concentric hypertrophy is caused by pressure overload → hypertrophy of ventricular wall → walls of the ventricles get larger inwards — aortic stenosis
  • Eccentric hypertrophy is caused by volume overload → dilation of ventricles → walls of the ventricles get larger outwards — aortic regurgitation

Concentric Vs. Eccentric Hypertrophy.

Recall the type of heart murmur heard in aortic stenosis.

Ejection-systolic murmur — “Crescendo-decrescendo murmur”

What is an ejection-systolic murmur?

An ejection systolic murmur is a type of heart murmur that occurs

when blood flows turbulently through a narrowed vessel or an irregular valve during systole

(see lecture diagram / notes)

Explain why this heart murmur is heard in patients with aortic stenosis.

This is because when the left ventricle contracts, the flow of blood is impaired through the stenotic valve — This occurs during systole, so it is called an ejection-systolic murmur

Describe the nature of the ejection-systolic murmur.

  • The ejection-systolic murmur can be described to have a “crescendo-decrescendo” sound
  • This is because it gets louder during the flow of blood through the stenotic valve before quietening down again

Recall three clinical symptoms/signs of aortic stenosis.

  • Ejection-systolic murmur
  • Syncope on exertion — poor peripheral perfusion
  • Angina on exertion — poor coronary artery perfusion secondary to aortic stenosis
  • Diffuse crackles on auscultation of lungs and dyspnoea — i.e fluid retention, scarring or inflammation of lungs

What is the investigation of choice for aortic stenosis?

  • Doppler echo scan
  • NOTE: The doppler echo scan also helps us to identify the stenosis or calcification of the aortic valve very clearly

Why is a doppler echo scan so useful for aortic stenosis?

  • Doppler echocardiography allows calculation of the pressure gradient (difference in pressure) between the left ventricle and the aorta during systole
  • There is a higher pressure gradient in aortic stenosis due to the stenosed valve which forces the LV to contract harder

Describe the treatment of aortic stenosis. For severe aortic stenosis

  • Transcatheter valve replacement
  • Surgical valve prosthesis

Describe the difference between transcatheter valve replacement and surgical valve prosthesis.

  • In transcatheter valve replacement/implantation (TAVI), a catheter is inserted usually through the femoral artery in the groin (the transfemoral approach) and threaded up to the heart. — The new valve is then carefully guided to the aortic valve location, using real-time imaging to ensure precise positioning
  • A surgical valve prosthesis is an artificial heart valve that is implanted to replace a damaged or diseased natural heart valve, often in cases of severe valve stenosis (narrowing) or regurgitation (leakage) — This is done using either open-heart surgery or minimally invasive surgical techniques.

Link To Understand Tavi (Tavr).

Describe the choice of management for aortic stenosis.

The choice of intervention should be a shared decision-making process, taking account of lifetime risks and benefits associated with the type of approach (transcatheter versus surgical) and type of valve (mechanical vs. bioprosthetic)

Recall the two types of valve used to treat aortic stenosis.

  • Bioprosthetic valve
  • Mechanical valve

Describe the difference between a bioprosthetic and a mechanical valve.

  • A bioprosthetic valve is made from animal tissue and has a limited lifespan (i.e 10-20 years) meaning it may require replacement
  • A mechanical valve is made from durable materials like titanium, carbon, or other synthetic materials and is typically a lifelong solution

Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement.

What is aortic regurgitation?

Aortic regurgitation (AR) is the diastolic leakage of blood from the aorta into the left ventricle. — i.e the reverse of aortic stenosis

Why does aortic regurgitation typically occur?

It occurs due to incompetence of valve leaflets resulting from either intrinsic valve disease or dilation of the aortic root

What is aortic root dilatation?

Aortic root dilatation is a condition where the aortic root —the section of the aorta closest to the heart that connects to the aortic valve— becomes abnormally enlarged or widened

Why does aortic root dilatation lead to aortic regurgitation?

  • Aortic root dilatation can lead to aortic regurgitation (AR) because the enlargement of the aortic root affects the structure and positioning of the aortic valve leaflets
  • This can cause them to not close properly which allows blood to leak back into the left ventricle

Describe the acute clinical presentation of aortic regurgitation.

It can be acute → medical emergency, presenting with sudden onset of pulmonary oedema and hypotension or cardiogenic shock

Describe the chronic clinical presentation of aortic regurgitation.

It can be chronic → culminate into congestive cardiac failure

What is cardiogenic shock?

Cardiogenic shock refers to the tissue hypoperfusion caused by an inability or failure of the heart to pump blood efficiently

What is congestive cardiac failure?

  • Congestive cardiac failure is a condition where the heart cannot pump effectively, leading to fluid congestion in the lungs and other parts of the body
  • It is commonly the sequelae of valvular defects, coronary artery disease, hypertension and other cardiomyopathies
  • NOTE: The term “congestive” refers to the build-up of fluid that often occurs in this condition, as a failing heart can lead to congestion in the lungs and other parts of the body

Recall the two groups of causes for aortic regurgitation.

  • Congenital/acquired — i.e affecting the valves
  • Aortic root dilatation

Recall three congenital/acquired causes of aortic regurgitation.

  • Rheumatic heart disease
  • Infective endocarditis (IE) — bacteria attaching to valves
  • Aortic valve stenosis
  • Congenital heart defects
  • Congenital bicuspid valves — aortic valve is physiologically tricuspid

Describe how aortic stenosis can lead to aortic regurgitation.

  • As the leaflets of the valves degenerate and become more rigid, they may not fully meet when they close, resulting in leakage or regurgitation of blood back into the left ventricle during diastole
  • Chronic aortic stenosis can also → aortic root dilatation as the left ventricular pressure increases over time

Recall three aortic root dilation-related causes of aortic regurgitation.

  • Marfan’s syndrome
  • Connective tissue disease/collagen vascular diseases
  • Ankylosing spondylitis — a chronic inflammatory disease
  • Idiopathic
  • Traumatic
  • NOTE: Pretty much anything that affects the connective tissue of the aorta Is the aortic valve tricuspid or bicuspid?
  • Normal aortic valve : Tricuspid (three cusps)
  • Congenital anomaly : Bicuspid (two cusps), seen in about 1-2% of people

Describe the pathophysiology of aortic regurgitation.

  • The fundamental problem behind aortic regurgitation is that the aortic valve is unable to close properly, leading to the leakage of blood from the aorta back into the left ventricle. This is caused either by: — Aortic root dilatation (via Marfan’s syndrome, ankylosing spondylitis or syphilis) OR — Inflammation of valvular endocardium (via untreated Group A streptococcus → rheumatic fever, infective endocarditis)
  • This fault in the aortic valve leads to acute dilatation in the short term

where the left ventricle, by means of the Frank-Starling Law, increases stroke volume

In the long term, the left ventricle eccentrically hypertrophies to accommodate the increase in volume and this can lead to damage of the myocardium and eventually → systolic heart failure

What is the Frank-Starling relationship?

A law that describes how as filling of the heart increases, so too does the force of contraction generated

Recall three clinical signs/symptoms seen in aortic regurgitation.

  • Diastolic murmur
  • The (S3) ventricular gallop sound that occurs in early diastole, produced by rapid filling and expansion of ventricles.
  • Angina on exertion
  • Fatigue
  • Increased back pressure in lungs leading to pulmonary congestion

Recall three clinical findings in the acute presentation of aortic regurgitation.

  • Cardiogenic shock
  • Tachycardia — compensation for reduced cardiac output
  • Cyanosis
  • Pulmonary oedema
  • Diastolic murmur
  • NOTE: Signs of hypoperfusion

Describe three clinical findings in the chronic presentation of aortic regurgitation.

  • Wide pulse pressure — abnormally large difference between systolic and diastolic blood pressure, caused by increased ventricular contraction due to increased filling volume
  • Corrigan (wate hammer pulse)

What is a bounding/Corrigan/collapsing pulse?

  • A bounding pulse (also known as Corrigan pulse or collapsing pulse ) is a type of pulse that has a strong, forceful beat that rises quickly to a high intensity and then falls away just as rapidly
  • It results from the large stroke volume followed by the exaggerated collapse of the large vessels on the diastolic return of blood to the left ventricle
  • In aortic regurgitation, it is present due to the increased systolic pressure resulting from increased blood flow back into the ventricles (Frank-Starling relationship), and decreased diastolic pressure caused by blood leakage via the aortic valve

Collapsing Pulse.

Explain the ventricular S3 gallop heard in aortic regurgitation.

A pathological S3 is often referred to as a "ventricular gallop" and indicates that the ventricle is stiff or dilated and cannot properly handle the incoming blood, leading to a more pronounced sound during rapid filling

Recall the type of heart murmur heard in aortic regurgitation.

Diastolic murmur

Explain why this heart murmur is heard in patients with aortic regurgitation.

  • This is because the leaking of blood from aorta → left ventricle (LV) occurs whenever the aortic valve is closed
  • Because the aortic valve is open during systole, the murmur is only heard during diastole as this is the period where the aortic valve is closed

What is the investigation of choice for aortic regurgitation?

Echocardiography (ECG) is the best non-invasive test to diagnose and grade the severity of aortic regurgitation (AR)

Describe the treatment of aortic regurgitation.

  • Aortic Valve Replacement (AVR) — in acute situations/medical emergencies
  • Vasodilator therapy — This can delay the need for aortic valve replacement in chronic, asymptomatic patients
  • Prevention is key: It is important to treat underlying causes such as rheumatic fever and infective endocarditis before focusing on aortic valve replacement and other interventional techniques

Describe how vasodilator therapy works to treat aortic regurgitation.

  • Vasodilator therapy works by reducing the afterload (the pressure the heart must overcome to pump blood out) and preload (the volume of blood the heart has to handle)
  • This overall helps to improve left ventricular function and prevent further deterioration of the heart muscle

Describe the importance of prevention in the general management of aortic regurgitation.

  • Prevention is key
  • It is important to treat underlying causes such as rheumatic fever and infective endocarditis before focusing on aortic valve replacement and other interventional techniques

What is mitral stenosis?

Mitral stenosis, similar to aortic stenosis, is obstruction to left ventricular inflow at the level of mitral valve due to a structural abnormality of the mitral valve

Mitral Stenosis.

Recall five causes of mitral stenosis.

  • Rheumatic fever
  • Carcinoid syndrome
  • Use of ergot/serotonergic drugs
  • Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE )
  • Mitral annular calcification due to aging
  • Amyloidosis
  • Rheumatoid arthritis — via inflammation and fibrosis or amyloidosis
  • Whipple disease
  • Congenital deformity of the valve

What is carcinoid syndrome?

Carcinoid syndrome is a group of symptoms caused by the release of hormones and other chemicals, mainly serotonin, from carcinoid tumors —a type of neuroendocrine tumour — These tumours most commonly originate in the small intestine, appendix, rectum, or lungs, and they can secrete various bioactive substances

What is systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)?

Lupus, or systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), is a chronic autoimmune disease that occurs when the body's immune system attacks its own tissues and organs

What is amyloidosis?

  • Amyloidosis is a rare group of diseases caused by the abnormal accumulation of a protein called amyloid in various tissues and organs throughout the body
  • Amyloid proteins are typically misfolded or abnormally structured proteins that cannot be broken down properly by the body
  • Over time, they deposit in tissues, disrupting normal function, which

can lead to organ failure if untreated What is whipple disease?

  • Whipple disease is a rare, chronic infectious disease caused by the bacterium Tropheryma whipplei
  • It primarily affects the small intestine, leading to malabsorption (difficulty absorbing nutrients), but it can also affect multiple other organs, including the joints, heart, lungs, brain, eyes, and central nervous system

Describe how carcinoid syndrome can lead to mitral stenosis.

  • Serotonin and other vasoactive substances released by carcinoid tumours can lead to fibrosis (thickening) of the heart valves
  • NOTE: This usually affects the tricuspid and pulmonic valves because serotonin and other substances are metabolised in the lungs, so they don’t typically reach the left side of the heart.

What is the commonest cause of mitral stenosis?

In developing countries, rheumatic fever is the main cause of mitral stenosis

Pathophysiology Of Mitral Stenosis.

Describe the pathophysiology of mitral stenosis.

  • The basis of mitral stenosis is recurrent inflammation of the mitral valve — This can be caused by rheumatic fever, infective endocarditis, age-related calcification or congenital stenosis
  • There is hence fibrous deposition and calcification of the mitral valve leaflets and chordae tendineae leading to thickening and shortening of chordae tendineae — This causes fusion of the mitral valve leaflets
  • This leads to a decrease in area of the orifice leading to obstructed blood flow through the mitral valve which causes impaired emptying of the left atrium and impaired filling of the left ventricle
  • This ultimately results in decreased stroke volume and cardiac output - congestive heart failure and the subsequent increase in right ventricle pressure secondary to lung pressure build up leads to hypertrophy of right ventricle → right sided heart failure

Chordae Tendinae.

Describe how mitral stenosis can result in right-sided heart failure.

  • This occurs via what can be seen as a chain reaction
  • In mitral stenosis, the narrowing of the mitral valve leads to decreased left ventricular filling, decreased left atrial emptying and hence increased left atrial pressure
  • Decreased left atrial emptying → build-up of blood in the pulmonary veins → pulmonary congestion
  • Since the right ventricle pumps blood into the pulmonary arteries → veins, the increased pulmonary vein pressure means the right ventricle has to work harder to overcome the steeper pressure gradient
  • Over time, this leads to hypertrophy of right ventricle → right sided heart failure

Describe three clinical findings of mitral stenosis.

  • Opening snap, mid-diastolic murmur — opening snap = sound when stenotic valves are opening
  • Atrial fibrillation — due to left atrial hypertrophy
  • Right sided heart failure — cardiogenic shock/ congestive heart failure
  • Left atrial enlargement → compression of surrounding structures (rare)

Describe three findings in the clinical history of mitral stenosis.

  • History of rheumatic fever
  • Dyspnoea
  • Murmur
  • Opening snap / loud S1 in early stages
  • Dysphagia - due to atrial enlargement
  • Haemoptysis

What is haemoptysis?

Haemoptysis is the medical term for coughing up blood from your lungs or airways

How can atrial enlargement lead to dysphagia?

  • Anatomically, the left atrium is located in front of the oesophagus
  • Left atrial enlargement is a potential cause of dysphagia by mechanical compression especially when fluid overload in decompensated heart failure leads to LA dilation

Left Atrium And Oesophagus.

Recall three investigations used in the diagnosis of mitral stenosis.

  • ECG
  • Chest X-Ray
  • Transthoracic electrocardiography

Describe the management of mitral stenosis.

  • Progressive asymptomatic → No therapy required
  • Severe asymptomatic → No therapy generally required, adjuvant balloon valvotomy
  • Severe symptomatic → diuretic, balloon valvotomy, valve replacement & repair adjunct (additional) beta-blockers

What is balloon valvotomy?

  • Balloon valvotomy is a procedure involves the use of a balloon catheter to widen a narrowed heart valve and improve blood flow
  • It is commonly used to treat certain types of heart valve stenosis, including mitral stenosis, pulmonary valve stenosis, and, less commonly, aortic stenosis

How is a diuretic useful in the treatment of mitral stenosis?

  • Diuretics are medications that promote the removal of excess fluid from the body by increasing urine production
  • This helps to manage fluid overload, reduce pulmonary congestion, and relieve the symptoms of heart failure.

What is mitral regurgitation?

  • Mitral regurgitation is the most common type of valvular heart disease
  • It is caused by the disruption in any part of the mitral valve apparatus

What happens in mitral regurgitation?

Mitral regurgitation is characterised by abnormal reversal of blood flow from the left ventricle to the left atrium.

Recall three causes of acute mitral regurgitation.

  • Mitral valve prolapse
  • Rheumatic heart disease
  • Infective endocarditis
  • Consequence of valvular surgery
  • Prosthetic mitral valve dysfunction

Recall three causes of chronic mitral regurgitation.

  • Rheumatic heart disease
  • Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE )
  • Scleroderma
  • Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
  • Drug-related

What is scleroderma?

Scleroderma, also known as systemic sclerosis, is a rare autoimmune disease characterised by the abnormal growth of connective tissue, leading to hardening and thickening of the skin and, in some cases, internal organs

Describe the pathophysiology of mitral regurgitation.

The basis of mitral regurgitation is a faulty mitral valve which allows blood to flow from the left ventricle to the left atrium. This can be caused by: — Coronary Artery Disease (IHD & MI/ Myocarditis - dilation of valve leaflets and tethering of chordae tendineae) — Papillary muscle rupture - valve leaflets flail — Infective endocarditis (IE) - vegetations on the leaflets
