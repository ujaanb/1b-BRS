Overview

What is the vascular system?

  • The vascular system is one of the largest and the most extensive networks in the body — Lymphatic system is also very extensive
  • It is also known as the circulatory system, and is responsible for the transport of blood, nutrients, oxygen, hormones, and waste products throughout the body

What are endothelial cells?

Endothelial cells are specialised cells that form the inner lining of blood vessels and lymphatic vessels

Describe the significance of a faulty endothelial layer.

(see lecture diagram / notes)

When dysfunctional, the endothelium contributes to more diseases than any other organ Recall the three layers of blood vessels.

  • Tunica adventitia
  • Tunica media
  • Tunica intima

Describe the structure of the tunica adventitia.

The tunica adventitia contains vasa vasorum and nerves

What are vasa vasorum?

  • The vasa vasorum are small blood vessels that supply the walls of large blood vessels, such as arteries and veins
  • NOTE: The term "vasa vasorum" is Latin for "vessels of the vessels."

Describe the function of the vasa vasorum.

  • The vasa vasorum are integral to the tunica adventitia and partially to the tunica media
  • They provide oxygen and nutrients to these outer layers of the vessel wall, which are too thick to receive adequate diffusion from the blood flowing within the vessel lumen
  • They also remove waste products and carbon dioxide from these outer layers

Describe the function of the nerves within the tunica adventitia.

  • The nerves within the tunica adventitia (nervi vasorum) are integral to the autonomic regulation of the smooth muscle within the tunica media
  • This helps the blood vessels respond to pressure changes (baroreceptors) and chemical changes (chemoreceptors) within the blood and regulate the blood flow within them
  • NOTE: Nervi vasorum can also communicate with the vasa vasorum to regulate blood flow there too

Describe the structure of the tunica media.

The tunica media contains smooth muscle cells and it has an external elastic membrane

Describe the structure of the tunica intima.

  • The tunica intima contains the endothelium and it has an internal elastic membrane
  • It also contains a subendothelial layer deep to it which contains connective tissue and smooth muscle

What is the endothelium?

The endothelium is the term given to the layer of cells formed by endothelial cells within blood vessels

Blood Vessel Structure.

Which two types of blood vessel are exempt from this typical three-layer structure?

  • Capillaries
  • Venules

Describe the structure of capillaries and venules.

Capillaries and venules are formed by endothelium, supported by mural cells (pericytes) and a basement membrane

Capillary And Venule Structure.

Describe the function of capillaries.

Capillaries is where the exchange of nutrients and oxygen between blood and tissues occur

Describe the width of a capillary.

  • Capillaries are about 1/10 the width of a hair (5 to 10 μm)
  • This thin wall allows them to be well-adapted to perform their function

Capillaries.

Describe the function of the endothelium.

  • The endothelium acts as a vital barrier separating blood from tissues
  • They form a one-cell deep monolayer within the blood vessels,

which enables contact inhibition

NOTE: Endothelial cells also communicate with each other during proliferation to enable accurate migration to form the endothelial layer

Describe how the structure of the endothelium enables it to perform its function.

  • The endothelium being a monolayer allows for efficient gas and nutrient exchange between the blood within capillaries and surrounding tissues
  • The endothelium also has tight cell-to-cell junctions which controls the passage of substances between tissues and blood

What is an endothelial cell-to-cell junction?

Endothelial cell-to-cell junctions describe the specialised structures that connect adjacent endothelial cells, forming a barrier that regulates the passage of substances between the bloodstream and surrounding tissues

Describe the lifespan of a typical endothelial cell.

In vivo, it is thought that endothelial cells live a long life and have a low proliferation rate, unless new vessels are required for angiogenesis

Describe three regulatory functions of the vascular endothelium.

  • Vascular tone
  • Haemostasis and thrombosis
  • Angiogenesis
  • Inflammation
  • Permeability
  • Tissue homeostasis and regeneration

Describe how the vascular endothelium is involved in angiogenesis.

  • The vascular endothelium is involved in the production of important molecules essential to angiogenesis
  • These include matrix products like fibronectin, laminin, collagen, proteoglycan and proteases and growth factors such as insulin-like growth factor, transforming growth factor and colony-stimulating growth factor

Describe how the vascular endothelium is involved in inflammation.

  • The vascular endothelium is involved in the production of inflammatory markers — These include IL-1, IL-6 and IL-8, leukotrienes and MHC II
  • They also produce adhesion molecules such as ICAMs, VCAMs and selectins — i.e to aid leukocyte extravasation

Describe how the vascular endothelium is involved in thrombosis and haemostasis.

  • Vascular endothelium produces anticoagulant factors such as antithrombin, heparin, prostacyclin, thrombomodulin and plasminogen activator
  • It also produces procoagulant factors such as von Willebrand factor, thromboplastin, thromboxane A2, Factor V, platelet activating factor and plasminogen activator inhibitor

Describe how the vascular endothelium is involved in vascular tone and permeability.

  • Vascular endothelium can produce vasoconstricting factors such as angiotensin conversion enzyme (ACE), thromboxane A2, leukotrienes, free radicals and endothelin
  • It can also produce vasodilatory factors such as nitric oxide (NO) and prostacyclin

Describe what is meant by the heterogenous nature of endothelial cells/blood vessels.

  • The heterogenous nature of blood vessels describes how not all blood vessels and endothelial cells are the same
  • There are structural, functional and molecular differences
  • This is because endothelial cells and microvasculature have organotypic (organ-specific) properties and different gene/protein expression profiles
  • EXAMPLE: The brain requires a very tight blood-brain barrier, other organs do not

Heterogeneity Of Endothelial Cells.

Describe how RNA sequencing is significant in the examination of endothelial cells.

  • RNA sequencing is the technique used to analyse the gene expression of different individual cells within different tissues
  • Every cell has the same DNA, however different genes are expressed within different cell types, to produce whichever protein is relevant to its function
  • The gene expression between different cell types within different tissues is analysed and this is mapped onto a chart
  • Cells are then grouped together based on how similar their gene expression is. This is called Seurat clustering
  • In the examination of endothelial cells, this Seurat clustering has helped us to understand how similar gene expression is between cells within certain organs

Seurat Clustering.

What is the Human Cell Atlas?

  • The Human Cell Atlas is an international collaborative consortium that charts the cell types in the healthy body, across time from development to adulthood
  • The cells are classified on the basis of their unique molecular characteristics, including gene expression, protein composition, and epigenetic markers

Human Cell Atlas.

What are angiocrine factors?

  • Angiocrine factors are biologically active molecules secreted by endothelial cells that regulate the behaviour of neighbouring cells in the surrounding tissue
  • NOTE: Understanding this is key to moving away from the old belief that blood vessels are just “pipes” that carry blood. Blood vessels are biologically active structures with multiple functions.

Describe how endothelial cells use angiocrine factors.

  • Angiocrine factors themselves are essential for the maintenance of tissue homeostasis and regeneration
  • The angiocrine profile of each tissue-specific microvascular endothelium is different — This means the angiocrine factors used vary based on the environment
  • Conversely, the tissue-specific microenvironment influences the phenotype of endothelial cells
  • It is clear to see that there is a two-way interaction between the endothelium and their tissue-specific environment

What is the most abundant cell type in the heart?

  • Endothelial cells
  • NOTE: Endothelial cells “crosstalk” with cardiomyocytes which is essential for the functioning of the heart

Describe how endothelial cells interact with cardiomyocytes.

  • Endothelial cells communicate with cardiomyocytes using these angiocrine factors
  • They use paracrine signalling to influence functions such as cardiac development (during embryogenesis), vascularisation and cardiac repair/regeneration

Endothelial Cells And Cardiomyocytes.

What is angiogenesis?

Angiogenesis is the physiological process by which new blood vessels form from pre-existing vessels

Describe three physiological applications of angiogenesis.

  • Development
  • Menstrual cycle
  • Wound healing

How is angiogenesis useful in the menstrual cycle?

  • Angiogenesis is crucial to the remodelling and build-up of the endometrium after it has broken down at the beginning of the menstrual cycle
  • This is because it needs to supply oxygen and nutrients to the regenerating tissue

How is angiogenesis useful in wound healing?

Angiogenesis allows for oxygenation and nutrient delivery, which are essential for collagen synthesis, fibroblast activity, and extracellular matrix formation - all necessary for tissue repair

Angiogenesis In Tissue During Wound Healing.

Describe three pathological applications of angiogenesis.

  • Cancer
  • Retinopathies
  • Atherosclerosis
  • Chronic inflammatory diseases
  • Ischaemic diseases
  • Vascular malformations
  • NOTE: Many of these pathological applications of angiogenesis arise as a result of ischaemia or poor oxygen supply to tissues. Angiogenesis is usually a compensatory mechanism to try and increase oxygen supply to these tissues

What is the angiogenic switch?

The angiogenic switch refers to the transition of a tissue or tumour from a non-angiogenic state (where blood vessel growth is inactive) to an angiogenic state (where blood vessel growth is actively induced)

Describe how tumours can grow using host vasculature.

  • Tumours use the angiogenic switch in their favour to enable the further growth and proliferation of their tumours
  • Initially when a tumour is of a smaller size, the tumour can use existing host cell vasculature to meet its aerobic and nutritional needs
  • However when a tumour becomes larger, it begins to secrete angiogenic factors that induce angiogenesis
  • This angiogenesis is done to facilitate the growth and metastasis of the tumour

How can this angiogenic switch be combatted?

Anti-angiogenic drugs in the clinic, in combination with chemotherapy, can be used for a number of solid tumours to prevent their growth and metastasis

Angiogenesis In Cancer.

Describe the difference between bleeding and thrombosis.

  • Bleeding occurs as a result of insufficient haemostasis in response to vessel injury or insult
  • Haemostasis is when there is normal blood clotting in response to an injury
  • Thrombosis is when there is excessive or spontaneous clot formation

Summary Of Coagulation.

What is von-Willebrand disease?

  • This is a condition in which von Willebrand factor (vWF) is either reduced or defective — It leads to dysfunctional primary haemostasis
  • NOTE: Most forms of von Willebrand disease are mild

Describe the physiological function of von Willebrand factor (vWF).

  • To anchor platelets to exposed collagen within the endothelial wall — i.e using GlpIb receptor
  • Stabilising Factor VIII (Eight), protecting it from damage — hence if vWf is low, Factor VIII may also be low
  • NOTE: This explains why primary haemostasis is not usually achieved in individuals with von Willebrand disease

Describe the main characteristic of von Willebrand disease.

  • Mucosal bleeding
  • NOTE: The mucosal lining is typically the innermost layer of hollow organs and structures within the body that come into contact with the external environment

Mucosal Membrane.

Describe the main treatment of von Willebrand disease.

Replacement therapy — i.e vWF, DDAVP (desmopressin)

Describe when replacement therapy may not be sufficient to help patients with von Willebrand disease.

  • In some VWD patients, severe and intractable bleeding from the gastrointestinal tract is due to vascular malformations (abnormal formation of new blood vessels) in the gut blood vessels
  • Treatment with products that replace vWF in the blood is not sufficient to control this bleeding
  • This is because vWF has angiogenic functions, alongside its haemostatic functions

Angiodysplasia In The Gastrointestinal (Gi) Tract.

Describe the effects of DDAVP in treatment of von Willebrand disease.

DDAVP is effective because it works to increase levels of von Willebrand factor (vWF) and Factor VIII

Describe the haemostatic functions of von Willebrand factor (vWF).

  • To anchor platelets to exposed collagen within the endothelial wall
  • Stabilising Factor VIII (Eight), protecting it from damage — hence if vWf is low, Factor VIII may also be low
  • NOTE: Factor VIII is an important procoagulant factor

Describe the other main function of von Willebrand factor (vWF).

Endothelial vWF controls blood vessel formation (angiogenesis) and integrity, partly by regulating growth factor signalling — i.e VEGFR2 (vascular endothelial growth factor receptor) ; Angiopoietin-2

What is the main protagonist in atherosclerosis?

Endothelial activation

Describe the three main differences between a resting and activated endothelium.

  • A resting endothelium is anti-inflammatory, anti-proliferative and anti-thrombotic
  • An activated endothelium is pro-inflammatory, pro-proliferative and pro-thrombotic

When is there a physiological need for the endothelium to be activated?

  • When you cut yourself
  • If there is an infection in the serum
  • Hypoxia, to form new blood vessels (angiogenesis)

Recall three cardiovascular risk factors for an activated endothelium.

  • Smoking
  • Viruses
  • Mechanical stress
  • Inflammation
  • High blood pressure
  • OxLDL
  • High glucose
  • NOTE: These risk factors activate the endothelium unnecessarily, unlike the physiological triggers listed above

Describe three ways an activated endothelium can lead to atherosclerosis.

  • Thrombosis — transition from anti-thrombotic → pro-thrombotic
  • Senescence — cells getting older, which increases the potency of their atherosclerotic effects such as inflammation, plaque formation and plaque instability
  • Leukocyte recruitment — transition from anti-inflammatory → pro-inflammatory
  • Permeability — blood vessels become more permeable in the activated state

Describe the general steps involved in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis.

  • Endothelial dysfunction occurs as a result of risk factors or any other cause — This results in endothelial activation
  • This leads to fatty streak formation which is the beginning of the development of the necrotic core of the atherosclerotic plaque — Foam cells, VSMCs and cholesterol all contribute to the plaque
  • Over time, there is then development into an advanced, complicated lesion of atherosclerosis

What is the role of leukocyte adhesion?

Leukocyte adhesion is important for the transmigration of leukocytes from the bloodstream into the tissues where infection needs to be fought — i.e to sites of injury

Describe generally the steps involved in the leukocyte adhesion cascade.

  • Endothelial activation leads to the upregulation of adhesion molecules such as integrins and selectins
  • Using these molecules, leukocytes travel along the bloodstream towards the site of injury via rolling, arrest, adhesion and intravascular crawling
  • Finally, paracellular or transcellular transmigration allows the transfer of leukocytes from the bloodstream to the site of injury

Leukocyte Adhesion Cascade.

When does leukocyte recruitment from the blood to tissues usually occur?

In response to infection and during inflammation

Describe how leukocyte recruitment is affected in atherosclerosis.

  • Endothelial activation is present in atherosclerosis by means of one mechanism or another — NOTE: In atherosclerosis, the endothelium is not only activated in sites of injury, that is the first problem
  • This endothelial activation leads to the upregulation of adhesion molecules and leukocyte recruitment begins
  • The leukocytes adhere physiologically to the host cell endothelium, however they cannot get through the subendothelial layer, as there is a big atherosclerotic plaque blocking its path
  • This leads to the accumulation of leukocytes within the atherosclerotic plaque

Describe the difference between a capillary and a post-capillary venule.

  • Capillaries are the primary site of exchange between the blood and surrounding tissues. — They allow for the diffusion of oxygen, carbon dioxide, nutrients, and waste products
  • Post-capillary venules collect deoxygenated blood from capillaries and begin the return of this deoxygenated blood to the venous system — They are also the primary site of inflammatory responses

Capillaries And Post-Capillary Venules.

Describe how monocytes become foam cells in atherosclerosis.

  • Monocytes leak from the blood into the subendothelial layer as a result of increased vascular permeability induced by endothelial activation
  • This causes them to differentiate into macrophages at the site of the plaque
  • Macrophages take up oxidised LDL that has been modified as a result of free radical release and they become foam cells

Describe how vascular permeability is changed in atherosclerosis.

In atherosclerosis, vascular permeability is increased as a result of the increased endothelial activation
