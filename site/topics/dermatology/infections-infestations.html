Overview

What is Staphylococcus aureus?

Staphylococcus aureus is a spherically shaped, gram-positive bacteria that is frequently found in the respiratory tract and on the surface of the skin

Describe an adaptation of Staphylococcus aureus that allows it to inflict harm on hosts.

Staphylococcus aureus expresses virulence factors that confer pathogenic properties — Virulence factors are molecules, structures, or properties that enable a pathogen to establish itself, replicate, and cause disease in a host, enhancing its potential for causing harm

Describe the main virulence factor that enhances the potency of Staphylococcus aureus.

Staphylococcus aureus has receptors that allow it to bind to to fibrin which is found in abundance in wound surfaces such as ulcers and dermatitis

Recall three other virulence factors that enhance the potency of Staphylococcus aureus.

  • Haemolysin
  • PVL
  • Leukocidin
  • Staphylococcal enterotoxins (SEs)
  • TSST-1
  • Alpha-toxin
  • Exfoliative toxin
  • Phenol-soluble modulins (PSMs)
  • SspA
  • SspB

Staphylococcus Aureus.

Recall three conditions induced by Staphylococcus aureus.

  • Ecthyma
  • Impetigo
  • Cellulitis
  • Folliculitis

What is ecthyma?

Ecthyma is a deep form of impetigo, a skin infection, characterised by small, shallow, punched-out ulcers with thick, brown-black crusts and surrounding erythema

Ecthyma.

What is impetigo?

Impetigo is a common, highly contagious bacterial skin infection, often appearing as red sores or blisters that burst and form honey-coloured crusts, particularly around the nose and mouth, and is most common in young children

Impetigo.

What is cellulitis?

Cellulitis is a bacterial skin infection of the lower dermis and subcutaneous tissue, causing a painful, red, swollen, and warm area, and can potentially spread to the lymph nodes or bloodstream if left untreated

Cellulitis.

What is folliculitis?

Folliculitis is a common skin condition where hair follicles become inflamed, often appearing as small, red, and sometimes pus-filled bumps, and can occur anywhere on the body where there is hair

Recall two manifestations of folliculitis.

  • Furunculosis
  • Carbuncles

What is furunculosis?

Furunculosis, commonly known as boils, is a deep infection of a hair follicle, resulting in pus-filled bumps on the skin

What are carbuncles?

A carbuncle is a cluster of furuncles (boils) that form a connected area of infection

Folliculitis, Furuncles And Carbuncles.

What is Staphylococcus scalded skin syndrome (SSSS)?

Staphylococcus scalded skin syndrome (SSSS) is a serious skin infection caused by a toxin produced by certain strains of Staphylococcus aureus bacteria leading to widespread blistering and peeling of the skin

Staphylococcus Scalded Skin Syndrome (Ssss).

Describe how else Staphylococcus aureus can have negative effects.

Staphylococcus aureus can superinfect (worsen) other dermatoses such as atopic eczema, herpes simplex virus (HSV), leg ulcers

What is streptococcus?

Streptococcus is a spherically shaped, gram-positive bacteria that typically arranges in chains or pairs

Describe three virulence factors for streptococcus.

  • Streptococcus pyogenes attaches to epithelial surfaces using lipoteichoic acid portion of fimbriae — fimbriae are thin, hairlike appendages found on the surface of some bacteria that help them attach to surfaces
  • Has M-protein (anti-phagocytic) & hyaluronic acid capsule (anti-phagocytic)
  • Produces erythrogenic exotoxins
  • Produces streptolysins S and O — damages cell membranes

Recall three conditions induced by Streptococcus.

  • Ecthyma
  • Impetigo
  • Cellulitis
  • Erysipelas
  • Scarlet fever
  • Necrotising fasciitis

What are erysipelas?

Erysipelas is a skin infection involving the dermis layer of the skin, but it may also extend to the superficial cutaneous lymphatics

Erysipelas And Cellulitis.

What is Scarlet fever?

Scarlet fever is a bacterial infection that typically presents with a sore throat, fever, and a characteristic rash, often described as sandpaper-like

What is necrotising fasciitis?

Necrotising fasciitis is a rare but serious bacterial infection that damages the deep tissues under the skin, including the fascia (a layer of tissue that separates muscles and organs) and can lead to tissue death

Necrotising Fasciitis.

Describe how else Streptococcus can have negative effects.

Streptococcus can also superinfect other dermatoses such as atopic eczema, herpes simplex virus (HSV), leg ulcers

Streptococcus.

Recall the two main types of folliculitis.

  • Infectious
  • Non-infectious

Describe the manifestations of folliculitis.

Folliculitis may manifest as follicular erythema as well as pustular erythema

Describe the implications of eosinophilic folliculitis.

Eosinophilic (non-infectious) folliculitis is associated with HIV

What is Panton-Valentine leukocidin (PVL)?

Panton-Valentine leukocidin (PVL) is a toxin and virulence factor produced by certain strains of Staphylococcus aureus (SA)

Describe the significance of Panton-Valentine leukocidin (PVL) in folliculitis.

Recurrent cases may arise from nasal carriage of Staphylococcus aureus, particularly strains expressing Panton- Valentine leukocidin (PVL)

Describe the treatments for folliculitis.

  • Antibiotics (usually flucloxacillin or erythromycin)
  • Incision and drainage is required for furunculosis (progression of folliculitis)

What is a furuncle?

  • Furuncles (boils) are deep, follicular abscesses caused by staphylococcal infection
  • NOTE: An abscess is a buildup of a pus that can affect any part of your body

What is a carbuncle?

Carbuncles are a collection of deep, follicular abscesses (furuncles) caused by staphylococcal infection

Describe the clinical significance of carbuncles.

Carbuncles are more likely to lead to complications such as cellulitis and septicaemia

Describe the difference between a furuncle and a carbuncle.

  • Furuncles (boils) are deep, follicular abscesses caused by staphylococcal infection
  • Carbuncles are a collection of these furuncles and are formed

when more than one furuncle interacts and joins with another

Furuncles And Carbuncles.

Describe three factors that can cause recurrent infection by staphylococcus aureus.

  • Establishment as a part of the resident microbial flora — Abundant in nasal flora
  • Immune deficiency — Hypogammaglobulinaemia — i.e antibodies (immunoglobulins) — HyperIgE syndrome – deficiency — Chronic granulomatous disease — AIDS — Diabetes Mellitus

Describe the effects of Panton-Valentine leukocidin (PVL).

  • Leukocyte destruction
  • Tissue necrosis

Describe the difference in potency between strains of Staphylococcus aureus with and without Panton-Valentine leukocidin (PVL).

Strains of Staphylococcus aureus with Panton-Valentine leukocidin (PVL) cause higher morbidity, higher mortality and have higher transmissibility

Describe three cutaneous effects of Panton-Valentine leukocidin (PVL).

  • Recurrent and painful abscesses
  • Folliculitis
  • Cellulitis
  • These are often painful, more than 1 site, recurrent and present in contacts

Describe three extracutaneous effects of Panton-Valentine leukocidin (PVL).

  • Necrotising pneumonia
  • Necrotising fasciitis
  • Purpura fulminans

What is purpura fulminans?

Purpura fulminans is a life-threatening, rapidly progressive thrombotic disorder characterised by a highly thrombotic subtype of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) that leads to rapid blood clotting in small blood vessels, causing purpuric skin lesions and necrosis

What is fascia?

  • Fascia is a general term used to describe connective tissue that is found external to muscles, bones and other organs
  • It is a thin casing of connective tissue that surrounds and holds every organ, blood vessel, bone, nerve fibre and muscle in place

Purpura Fulminans.

Recall how the risk of acquiring Panton-Valentine leukocidin (PVL) Staphylococcus aureus can be categorised.

The risk of acquiring Panton-Valentine leukocidin (PVL) can be summarised using the 5 C’s

Recall the 5 C’s.

  • C lose Contact — e.g. hugging, contact sports
  • C ontaminated items — e.g. gym equipment, towels or razors.
  • C rowding –crowded living conditions — e.g. military accommodation, prisons and boarding schools.
  • C leanliness (of environment)
  • C uts and grazes — having a cut or graze will allow the bacteria to enter the body

Describe the treatment of Panton-Valentine leukocidin (PVL) Staphylococcus aureus.

  • Consult local microbiologist / guidelines
  • Antibiotics (often tetracycline)
  • Decolonisation – — often Chlorhexidine body wash for 7 days
  • Nasal application of mupirocin ointment 5 days
  • Treatment of close contacts

What is pseudomonal folliculitis?

Pseudomonas folliculitis also known as hot tub folliculitis or hot tub rash, is a skin infection caused by the Pseudomonas aeruginosa bacteria, often acquired from contaminated water in hot tubs, pools, or spas

Describe the causes of pseudomonal folliculitis.

Associated with hot tub use, swimming pools and depilatories and sharing wet suits

Describe how pseudomonal folliculitis presents.

  • Appears 1-3 days after exposure, as a diffuse, truncal eruption — i.e on the trunk
  • The presentation is follicular erythematous papules

Describe the prognosis of pseudomonal folliculitis.

  • Most cases are self-limited, no treatment is required
  • Rarely: abscess, lymphangitis and fever

Describe the treatment for pseudomonal folliculitis.

Severe or recurrent cases can be treated with oral ciprofloxacin

Pseudomonal Folliculitis.

Recall the three layers of the skin and their arrangement.

  • Epidermis (outermost)
  • Dermis (middle)
  • Subcutaneous tissue (inner)

What are the two layers of the dermis and their arrangements?

  • Papillary dermis (outer)
  • Reticular dermis (inner) Images showing the arrangements of the skin and the dermis layers.

Describe the clinical features of cellulitis.

Tender swelling with ill-defined, blanching erythema or oedema

Recall a predisposing factor for cellulitis.

  • Oedema
  • NOTE: Oedema may be caused by cellulitis and it is also a predisposing factor

Recall the commonest causative agents for cellulitis.

  • Streptococcus pyogenes
  • Staphylococcus aureus

Describe the treatment for cellulitis.

Systemic antibiotics — oral or intravenous

Cellulitis.

Recall the two causes of impetigo.

  • Streptococci
  • Staphylococci

Describe the difference between streptococcus and staphylococcus- induced impetigo.

  • Staphylococcus aureus induced impetigo causes blisters as the blisters are caused by exfoliative toxins A & B
  • Streptococci-induced impetigo is hence known as non-bullous

Describe how Staphylococcus aureus causes blisters in impetigo.

  • Staphylococcus aureus has the exfoliative toxins A & B
  • These toxins split the epidermis by targeting desmoglein I, an adhesive protein in the epidermis

Describe the areas of the body usually affected by impetigo.

Face — perioral, ears, nares (nostrils)

Describe the treatment for impetigo.

Topical +/- systemic antibiotics

Impetigo (2).

What is impetiginisation?

  • Impetiginisation is the process of developing impetigo in the context of atopic dermatitis
  • It leads to the formation of gold crust and typically occurs when Staphylococcus aureus infects a pre-existing skin lesion
  • NOTE: Impetigo is a common, contagious bacterial skin infection, while "impetiginisation" refers to the secondary infection of existing skin lesions (like eczema or cuts) with the same bacteria that cause impetigo (typically Staph. aureus)

Impetiginisation.

What is syphilis?

Syphilis is a sexually transmitted bacterial infection

Recall the causative agent of syphilis.

Treponema pallidum

Describe the clinical features of syphilis.

  • Primary infection by syphilis causes chancre
  • Painless regional lymphadenopathy one week after the primary chancre

What is chancre?

A chancre is a painless ulcer with a firm indurated (hardened) border

Chancre.

Describe how long after exposure it takes for chancre to be revealed.

Chancre appears within 10-90 days — i.e one week to three months

Describe the prognosis of syphilis if untreated.

If syphilis is left untreated, it may progress to secondary syphilis

Describe how soon secondary syphilis succeeds primary syphilis.

Secondary syphilis begins ~50 days after chancre

Describe three clinical symptoms of secondary syphilis.

  • Malaise
  • Fever
  • Headache
  • Pruritus
  • Loss of appetite
  • Iritis

Describe three clinical manifestations of secondary syphilis.

  • Rash (88-100%) — Pityriasis rosea-like rash
  • Alopecia (‘moth-eaten’)
  • Mucous patches
  • Lymphadenopathy
  • Residual primary chancre
  • Condylomata lata — wart-like lesions
  • Hepatosplenomegaly
  • Sometimes the initial chancre are still evident

What is pityriasis rosea rash?

A pityriasis rosea rash is a skin condition characterised by a rash, often starting with a single, large patch (herald patch) followed by smaller, scaly patches

Why is secondary syphilis known as the “great mimicker”?

Secondary syphilis can look like a lot of other skin conditions, so it is a good idea to conduct tests before diagnosing — It has a low threshold for testing

What is lues maligna?

Lues maligna are a rare manifestation of secondary syphilis that are pleomorphic skin lesions including pustules, nodules and ulcers with necrotising vasculitis

Describe when lues maligna is commonly found.

More frequent in HIV manifestation

Describe the cause of lues maligna.

Lues maligna is caused by blockage of blood vessels by bacteria itself

Lues Maligna.

Describe the prognosis of secondary syphilis if untreated.

Secondary syphilis → Tertiary syphilis

Syphilis (2).

Describe the clinical manifestation of tertiary syphilis.

  • Tertiary syphilis is characterised by Gumma Skin lesions - nodules and plaques — These are mucosal lesions (located on the mucosa) and they may extend to and destroy the nasal cartilage
  • Cardiovascular disease
  • Neurosyphilis (general paresis or tabes dorsalis)

Describe the appearance of a Gumma skin lesion.

Gumma skin lesions are lesions that extend peripherally while central areas heal aberrantly with scarring and atrophy

Gumma Skin Lesions.

Describe the investigations done in the diagnosis of syphilis.

  • Clinical findings
  • Serology — may not be positive in primary syphilis
  • Strong index of suspicion required in secondary syphilis as it is the “great mimicker”
  • NOTE: If there is suspicion in primary syphilis due to chancre, just treat

Describe the treatment for tertiary syphilis.

Intramuscular (IM) benzylpenicillin or oral tetracycline — depends on whether patient is allergic to penicillin

Describe a special consideration to make for syphilis when trying to make a diagnosis.

  • Syphilis is known as “the great mimicker” meaning it can look like a lot of other conditions
  • It is hence useful to always have it in the back of the mind as a differential diagnosis

What is Borreliosis (Lyme disease)?

Borreliosis is a tick-borne bacterial infection

Describe the causative agent of Borreliosis.

A tick from the Ixodes family infected with Borrelia burgdorferi

Describe the clinical manifestation of Borreliosis (Lyme disease).

An annular (ring-shaped) erythema develops at the site of the bite of a Borrelia-infected tick from the Ixodes species

What is annular erythema?

Annular erythema describes ring-shaped, erythematous (red) lesions, that often start as small raised spots and enlarge centrifugally

Annular Erythema.

Describe the initial cutaneous manifestation of Borreliosis (Lyme disease).

  • Erythema migrans (only in 75%)
  • This is an Erythematous papule at the bite site, with progression to annular erythema of ›20cm
  • NOTE: ~25% of Borreliosis patients do not get any cutaneous manifestations

Describe the symptoms of Borreliosis.

1-30 days after infection → fever, headache

Describe the cutaneous manifestations of Borreliosis (Lyme disease) as it progresses.

Multiple secondary lesions develop - similar but smaller to initial lesion

Describe three clinical features of Borreliosis.

  • Neuroborreliosis
  • Arthritis – painful and swollen large joints (knee is the most affected joint)
  • Carditis

What is neuroborreliosis?

Neuroborreliosis is a neurological complication of Lyme disease that affects the nervous system, leading to a range of neurological symptoms

Describe three clinical features of neuroborreliosis.

  • Facial palsy / other CN palsies
  • Aseptic meningitis
  • Polyradiculitis — spinal nerve root inflammation

Describe the serology of Borreliosis.

Serology not sensitive

Describe the histopathology of Borreliosis.

Histopathology (biopsy) is non-specific

Describe the nature of diagnosing Borreliosis.

High index of suspicion required for diagnosis, like secondary syphilis

Describe the treatment of Borreliosis.

  • Doxycycline
  • Amoxicillin
  • Azithromycin Should you treat Borreliosis even if you are unsure of the diagnosis?
  • Yes.
  • The risk of progression is worse than the risk of antibiotics. Due to the low levels of sensitivity and specificity for Borreliosis testing, it is commonly treated before being confirmed as a diagnosis

Borreliosis.

What is herpes simplex virus (HSV)?

Herpes simplex virus (HSV), known as herpes, is a common infection that can cause painful blisters or ulcers

Recall the two types of herpes simplex virus (HSV).

  • HSV-1
  • HSV-2

Describe the difference between HSV-1 and HSV-2.

  • HSV-1 is contracted after direct contact with contaminated saliva/other infected secretions
  • HSV-2 is contracted via sexual contact

Describe why herpes simplex virus (HSV) is transmitted easily.

Transmission can occur even during asymptomatic periods of viral shedding

Describe the clinical presentation of herpes simplex virus (HSV).

  • Primary and recurrent vesicular eruptions
  • Favour orolabial and genital regions

Describe how herpes simplex virus (HSV) can cause long-term problems for hosts.

  • Herpes simplex virus (HSV) replicates at the mucocutaneous site of infection
  • It travels by retrograde axonal flow to dorsal root ganglia,

where it can reside and become latent

(see lecture diagram / notes)

After a while, it can become reactivated and then uses anterograde axonal flow to cause recurrent infection Recall how long it takes for symptoms to appear after exposure to herpes simplex virus (HSV).

Symptoms present within 3-7 days of exposure

Recall three preceding symptoms of herpes simplex virus (HSV) infection.

Tender lymphadenopathy, malaise, anorexia — ± Burning, tingling

Describe how herpes simplex virus (HSV) affects organs around the body.

  • Genital involvement – often excruciatingly painful→ urinary retention
  • Systemic manifestations – aseptic meningitis in up to 10% of women

Describe the prognosis of herpes simplex virus (HSV) infection.

  • Crusting and resolution within 2-6 weeks
  • Reactivation may then occur

Recall three causes of herpes simplex virus (HSV) reactivation.

  • Spontaneous
  • UV
  • Fever
  • Local tissue damage
  • Stress

Herpes Simplex Virus (Hsv).

What is eczema herpeticum?

This is a type of atopic dermatitis (eczema) that is caused by the dysregulation of the immune system seen in eczema leading to increased susceptibility to herpes simplex virus (HSV) — It spreads across the body very fast and is a medical emergency

Recall what causes eczema herpeticum.

Herpes simplex virus (HSV) infection

What is a clinical feature of eczema herpeticum and how can it be treated?

  • Monomorphic, punch-like (excoriated) erosions (red holes in the skin) — It looks like someone hole-punched the skin
  • It can be treated with intravenous antivirals as it is caused by viral infections (typically herpes simplex virus )

Why is incorrect diagnosis of eczema herpeticum so dangerous?

  • Atopic eczema medications such as corticosteroids, retinoids can suppress immune system function
  • This leads to the body being more vulnerable to HSV and can result in encephalitis

Describe a characteristic feature of eczema herpeticum.

Typically there is a background of poorly-controlled eczema

Eczema Herpeticum.

What is herpetic whitlow?

Herpetic whitlow, also known as a "whitlow finger," is a painful finger infection caused by the herpes simplex virus (HSV), typically appearing as blisters or sores on the fingertip

Describe the causative agent of herpetic whitlow.

HSV 1 more commonly than HSV 2

Describe the clinical presentation of herpetic whitlow. Infection of digits

  • Pain
  • Swelling
  • Vesicles (vesicles may appear later)

Recall how herpetic whitlow is commonly misdiagnosed.

  • Herpetic whitlow is commonly misdiagnosed as paronychia or dactylitis as the two conditions look similar to herpetic whitlow
  • This is why it is important to carry out swabs to test these conditions

Herpetic Whitlow.

What is neonatal HSV infection?

This is when newborn babies are exposed to herpes simplex virus (HSV) during vaginal delivery — It can be caused by HSV 1 or 2

Describe a factor that increases the potency of neonatal HSV infection.

Risk is higher when HSV is acquired near time of delivery

Describe the clinical presentation of neonatal HSV infection.

  • Localised usually – scalp or trunk
  • Vesicles → bullae erosions

Describe how the mortality rate for HSV infection varies with treatment.

  • Mortality ›50% without treatment → encephalitis for survivors
  • Mortality 15% with treatment → neurological deficits for survivors

Describe the treatment for neonatal HSV infection.

Intravenous (IV) antivirals

Neonatal Hsv Infection.

Recall which population severe/chronic HSV infection is commonly found in.

Immunocompromised patients — e.g. HIV / transplant recipient

Recall the most common presentation of severe/chronic HSV infection.

  • Chronic, enlarging ulceration
  • Multiple sites or disseminated

Describe the clinical presentations of severe/chronic HSV infection.

  • Often atypical — e.g. verrucous, exophytic or pustular lesions
  • Involvement of respiratory or GI tracts may occur

Describe how severe/chronic HSV infection is diagnosed.

Swab for Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) — this is the gold standard

Describe the treatment for HSV infection.

Don’t delay —

when in doubt, just treat

Oral valacyclovir or acyclovir 200mg five times daily in immunocompetent localised infection — Intravenous 10mg/kg TDS (3x per day) X 7-19 days

Herpes Simplex Virus (Hsv).

Recall the three types of fungal skin infections.

  • Superficial
  • Deep/soft tissue
  • Disseminated (systemic)

What is pityriasis versicolor?

Pityriasis versicolor is a superficial fungal skin infection causing scaly, discoloured patches

Recall the causative agent of pityriasis versicolor.

Malassezia spp. (e.g. M. furfur) overgrowth

Describe the clinical manifestation of pityriasis versicolor.

  • Hypopigmented, hyperpigmented or erythematous macules +/- fine scale
  • NOTE: A macule is a flat, discolored area of skin

When does pityriasis versicolor begin?

Pityriasis versicolor begins during adolescence (when sebaceous glands become active)

Describe when pityriasis versicolor flares.

Pityriasis versicolor flares when temperatures and humidity are high — e.g. in summer months

Describe how pityriasis versicolor is treated.

Topical azole (antifungal)
