Overview

Recall the two main types of skin lesion.

  • Pigmented
  • Non-pigmented

Describe the difference between pigmented and non-pigmented lesions.

  • Pigmented lesions are typically brown or black in colour
  • Non-pigmented lesions are typically reddish or skin-like colour

Recall the two types of pigmented skin lesion.

  • Melanocytic
  • Non-melanocytic

Recall two melanocytic pigmented skin lesions.

  • Melanocytic naevi (mole)
  • Solar lentigo
  • Lentigo maligna
  • Malignant melanoma

Which of these are malignant?

  • Lentigo maligna
  • Malignant melanoma

Recall two non-melanocytic pigmented skin lesions.

  • Seborrhoeic keratosis
  • Dermatofibroma
  • Skin tags
  • NOTE: These lesions may get their colours from non-melanocytic sources such as blood (haemoglobin), keratin, or exogenous pigments like those from tattoos

Skin Tag.

What does the term lentigo mean?

A lentigo is a small, clearly defined patch of skin that is darker than the surrounding skin as a result of an increased number of melanocytes

Recall two non-pigmented skin lesions.

  • Actinic keratosis — scaly skin caused by UV light
  • Bowen’s
  • Squamous cell carcinoma
  • Basal cell carcinoma
  • Intradermal naevi
  • Clear cell acanthoma
  • Porokeratosis — group of conditions with abnormal keratinisation
  • Psoriasis

Epidermis.

What is a melanoma?

A melanoma is a serious type of skin cancer that arises from melanocytes

What is a malignant melanoma?

  • A malignant melanoma is the “nastiest” type of skin cancer and it arises from dysfunctional melanocytes
  • NOTE: A malignant melanoma = melanoma

Describe briefly the pathogenesis of a malignant melanoma.

  • Melanocytes become abnormal
  • They start proliferating — → Atypical cells — → Atypical architecture
  • The abnormal cells then appear abnormal clinically by proliferating up to the superficial layers of the epidermis (stratum corneum) from the basal layer

Malignant Melanoma Pathogenesis.

What is lentigo maligna?

This is a type of melanoma where the abnormal, malignant melanocytes remain within the epidermis and do not proliferate to any other skin layers (i.e the dermis)

Recall another name for lentigo maligna.

Melanoma in situ — this makes sense as it describes well the extent of spread of melanocytes; only in the epidermis, the site of origin

Describe the physical characteristics of lentigo maligna.

  • Irregular shape
  • Light & dark brown colours
  • Size usually ›2.0 cm

Lentigo Maligna.

What is dermoscopy?

  • Dermoscopy is an important dermatological imaging technique that is known to be the “ midway point” between a biopsy and image seen by the naked eye — i.e it can can help us to see things invisible to the naked eye, but not as well as a biopsy would
  • It is performed using a dermatoscope

Dermoscopy.

What is lentigo maligna melanoma?

Lentigo maligna melanoma is when a melanoma has spread from the epidermis to another layer of the skin (i.e the dermis)

Lentigo Maligna Melanoma.

What is a nevus?

A nevus is a benign (non-cancerous) growth on the skin that results from a proliferation of cells — These cells are usually melanocytes, but not always

What is a junctional nevus?

  • A junctional nevus (mole) is a melanocytic benign growth on the skin (melanocytic nevus) clustered at the dermal-epidermal junction — It typically appears as a flat, pigmented spot (mole)
  • NOTE: By definition, a junctional nevus is melanocytic

Junctional Nevus.

Describe how the development of a malignant melanoma from a junctional naevus can vary.

A junctional naevus is benign, however the melanocytes within this nevus may become abnormal/malignant and proliferate, and at this point junctional naevus → melanoma

When a malignant melanoma is proliferating from the dermal- epidermal junction, it can either proliferate vertically or horizontally

NOTE: Melanomas may also arise from normal skin, not just from junctional naevi

Describe the varied clinical presentations of horizontally and vertically spread malignant melanomas.

  • Vertically growing melanomas would present as raised nodules on the skin
  • Horizontally growing melanomas would present as flat, coloured surfaces on the skin

Malignant Melanomas From Junctional Naevi.

What is a superficial spreading malignant melanoma?

A superficial spreading malignant melanoma is a form of melanoma that proliferates horizontally

Describe the development of a superficial spreading malignant melanoma.

There is lateral proliferation of malignant melanocytes, which allows the lesion to appear flat on the skin

Superficial Spreading Malignant Melanoma.

What is regression in the context of a melanoma?

  • This is when the skin in the affected area begins to lose its extra pigment and returns to its original colour
  • It occurs sometimes when the immune system is trying to eliminate the melanoma

Regressive Melanoma.

What is the ABCDE rule?

The ABCDE rule is an acronym that can help patients to detect and

identify potential dermatological malignancies especially in cases of pigmented lesions

It is useful for self-monitoring of a patient’s skin as regular screening for everyone is impractical

Describe how the ABCDE rule is used in the diagnosis of superficial spreading malignant melanomas.

  • Asymmetry — one half of the spot is unlike the other half
  • Border — the spot has an irregular, scalloped or poorly defined border
  • Colour — the spot has varying colours from one area to the next, such as shades of tan, brown, black or various areas of white, red or blue
  • Diameter — while melanomas are usually ›6mm in diameter, or about the size of a pencil eraser, when diagnosed, they can be smaller. the larger the diameter, the greater the chances of the lesion being cancerous
  • Evolving — the spot looks different from the rest or is changing in size, shape or colour

Describe when we may have to keep monitoring a lesion of the skin.

If a patient notifies you that their lesion has started to get larger or evolve, even if the ABCD rule states otherwise, you should monitor

Abcde Rule.

Describe the different types of proliferation involved in the development of nodular malignant melanomas.

Nodular malignant melanomas, which appear as raised bumps on the skin arise from vertical proliferation of malignant melanocytes

Nodular Malignant Melanoma.

Describe how nodular malignant melanomas can arise within superficial spreading melanomas.

These types of melanomas arise from downward proliferation of malignant melanocytes following previous horizontal growth

Describe the physical appearance of nodular malignant melanomas arising within superficial spreading melanomas.

Nodule developing within irregular plaque (dark patch)

Describe the prognosis of nodular malignant melanomas arising within superficial spreading melanomas.

  • Prognosis will become WORSE
  • This is an alarming condition and the nodule must be taken out surgically

Why do melanocytes proliferate downwards rather than

upwards? • Cancer cells aren’t just expanding blindly — they’re chemotactically guided toward areas rich in: ◦ Oxygen and nutrients ◦ Growth factors ◦ Matrix to attach to and remodel (via enzymes like matrix metalloproteinases, MMPs) • Those signals are all below, not above. • That’s why malignant melanocytes express proteases that degrade the basement membrane — enabling downward invasion

Malignant Melanoma.

What is acral lentiginous melanoma?

  • Acral lentiginous melanoma is a melanoma that develops on the palms, soles, or under the nails, and is the most common type of melanoma in people with darker skin
  • NOTE: Acral = peripheral or extremity parts of the body, specifically the hands, feet, and nails, as well as the tip of the nose and ears

Describe how cancers on the feet usually present.

  • Due to the pressure that is applied to the soles of our feet when we walk, it is difficult to develop nodular melanomas
  • Hence, most melanomas on the sole of the feet are usually flat

Acral Lentiginous Melanoma.

What is longitudinal melanonychia?

Longitudinal melanonychia is a benign condition that develops on the nail plate, characterised a longitudinal band across the length of the nail plate — It can arise from melanocytes within the nail matrix proliferating into the nail plate

The Nail.

What is a subungual melanoma?

  • Subungual melanoma is a form of malignant melanoma that develops on the nail plate, and is characterised by thicker longitudinal bands across the nail plate — It can result from the aforementioned nail matrix melanocytes proliferating aberrantly
  • NOTE: Subungual = situated or occurring under a fingernail or toenail

Describe the difference between a longitudinal melanonychia and a subungual melanoma.

  • A longitudinal melanonychia is benign and is characterised by a longitudinal band down the nail
  • A subungual melanoma is malignant and is characterised by a wider band across the nail
  • The wider the melanoma is, the more alarming and dangerous it is

Subungual Haematoma.

What is amelanotic melanoma?

Amelanotic melanoma is a type of melanoma where the malignant melanocytes do not produce or have very little melanin

Describe the distinctive characteristic of amelanotic melanomas.

Amelanotic melanomas do not have melanin, so they appear to be non-pigmented even though it is derived from melanocytes

Amelanotic Melanoma.

Recall the six main types of malignant melanoma.

  • Lentigo maligna melanoma
  • Superficial spreading melanoma
  • Nodular melanoma
  • Acral lentiginous melanoma
  • Subungual melanoma
  • Amelanotic melanoma

Describe how we can easily determine the prognosis of melanomas.

Breslow thickness is a technique used to estimate the prognosis of a melanoma

Describe how Breslow thickness is used to determine the prognosis of melanomas.

  • The melanoma is cut out of the skin, and taken to the lab for processing by the histopathologist
  • They then measure the depth of the melanoma from the stratum granulosum (2nd most superficial layer) to the bottom of the tumour
  • The deeper the melanoma, the more dangerous the tumour

Recall seven risk factors for melanoma development.

  • Genetic markers (e.g. CDKN2A mutations)
  • Family history of dysplastic nevi or melanoma
  • Ultraviolet irradiation
  • Sunburns during childhood
  • Intermittent burning exposure in unacclimatised fair skin
  • Number (›50) and size (›5 mm) of melanocytic nevi
  • Congenital nevi
  • Number of atypical nevi (›5)
  • Atypical/dysplastic nevus syndrome
  • Personal history of melanoma
  • High socioeconomic status
  • Skin type I, II
  • Equatorial latitudes
  • DNA repair defects (e.g. xeroderma pigmentosum)
  • Immunosuppression

Skin Types (I, Ii).

Describe the first line of management for melanomas.

Primary, urgent excision down to subcutaneous fat with a 2mm peripheral margin

Describe the follow-up management of a melanoma.

After a primary excision, a wide excision is performed

What is a wide excision?

A wide excision is a surgical procedure used to remove a cancerous or problematic area, along with a surrounding margin of healthy tissue

Describe how a wide excision is performed to manage melanomas.

  • The margin of a melanoma wide excision is determined by Breslow depth
  • The greater the Breslow depth, the wider the margin is — - 5mm for melanoma in situ (lentigo maligna) — - 10mm for deeper lesions
  • NOTE: This is because the deeper the melanoma is, the greater the potential that abnormal melanocytes have spread wider in the skin is. These melanocytes may cause recurrence, so should be excised when possible

Describe the importance of a wide excision.

A wide excision prevents local recurrence or persistent disease by removing some healthy tissue alongside affected tissue and attempting to remove all affected areas

What is a sentinel lymph node?

A sentinel lymph node is the first lymph node that drains a specific area, like a tumour, and is the most likely to contain cancer cells if they have spread from the primary tumour

What is a sentinel lymph node biopsy?

  • The sentinel lymph node is identified using an injected dye
  • A biopsy is taken from this lymph node and analysed. If there are any abnormal melanocytes present in this lymph node, then the melanoma has metastasised to the lymph node

Describe the general management of a melanoma.

  • Primary excision → Wide local excision to all melanomas
  • Sentinel lymph node biopsy

Recall the most frequent site of metastasis for a melanoma.

Lymph node

Describe the investigations performed for the management of a metastasised melanoma. If there is metastasis of the melanoma (Stage III/IV):

  • PET-CT
  • MRI brain

What is a pyogenic granuloma?

Pyogenic granuloma is a benign nodule that usually forms at the site of a piercing — It can form as the blood vessels at the site of a injury/irritation are trying to heal — i.e in young people after a piercing

Describe the difference between a melanoma and a granuloma.

Melanoma is a potentially lethal skin cancer, while granulomas are benign (non-cancerous) growths, often appearing as raised bumps or nodules

Pyogenic Granuloma.

Describe the difference between pigmented and non-pigmented skin cancers.

  • Pigmented lesions are typically brown or black in colour
  • Non-pigmented lesions are typically reddish or skin-like colour

Recall four types of non-pigmented skin cancers.

  • Actinic keratosis- precancerous
  • Bowen’s disease
  • Squamous cell carcinoma
  • Basal cell carcinoma

What are the primary cells that make up the epidermis called?

Keratinocytes

What is actinic keratosis?

  • Actinic keratosis is a pre-cancerous condition of the skin characterised by thick, scaly, flaky or crusty skin
  • It is caused by ultraviolet (UV) light exposure leading to abnormal keratinocytes

Recall an alternative name for actinic keratosis.

Solar keratosis

Describe the potential prognosis of actinic keratosis.

  • Actinic keratosis is a pre-cancerous condition caused by ultraviolet (UV) light exposure leading to abnormal keratinocytes
  • This may progress →squamous cell carcinoma in situ which is when more of these keratinocytes become damaged/proliferate but the damage is limited to the epidermis
  • This may progress → invasive squamous cell carcinoma which is

when the keratinocyte damage has spread to the dermis and other layers of the skin

(see lecture diagram / notes)

Microscopy Of Actinic Keratosis.

Recall the risk of actinic keratosis progressing to squamous cell carcinoma.

Progression to SCC: 0.025–16% per year for any single lesion

Actinic Keratosis.

What is Bowen’s disease?

Bowen’s disease is squamous cell carcinoma in situ, which is when UV light has damaged keratinocytes and it has spread through the epidermis

Describe the difference in clinical appearance of Bowen’s disease and actinic keratosis.

Bowen’s disease is much redder, flakier and upon dermoscopy, more vasculature can be seen

Squamous Cell Carcinoma (Scc) On The Nail.

Recall five treatments for actinic keratosis and Bowen’s disease.

  • 5-fluorouracil cream — destroys abnormal cells in the top layer of the skin, long-term
  • Cryotherapy
  • Imiquimod cream
  • Photodynamic therapy
  • Currettage and cautery — surgical removal via scraping or heat
  • Excision
  • NOTE: Treatments are selected according to the needs and wishes of a patient. Excision is not always required, unlike melanoma

What is a squamous cell carcinoma (SCC)?

  • This is a skin cancer of the squamous cells of the skin, such as the keratinocytes that has spread through to the dermis and/or other layers of the skin than the epidermis
  • NOTE: Squamous cells refers to the shape of the cell which is flat, and keratinocytes are flattened within the epidermis. Keratinocytes are a type of squamous epithelial cell that form the stratified squamous
