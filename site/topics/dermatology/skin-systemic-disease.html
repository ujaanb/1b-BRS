Overview

Describe why it is important to consider systemic diseases when assessing the health of the skin.

  • Rashes may be more than skin deep
  • Comprehensive assessment coupled with dermatological diagnostic skills can: — Prevent or reduce internal organ damage by early diagnosis — Allow detection of internal malignancy

Describe the investigations performed for dermatological conditions.

  • Blood tests
  • Microbiology
  • Imaging
  • Skin biopsy
  • Specific
  • NOTE: The investigations that are chosen will depend on clinical findings

Describe what blood tests are performed to investigate for dermatological conditions.

  • Full blood count
  • Renal profile — lupus can cause kidney inflammation (lupus nephritis)
  • Liver function tests — lupus can cause liver inflammation
  • Inflammatory markers
  • Autoimmune serology

Describe what microbiology is performed to investigate for dermatological conditions.

  • Viral/Bacterial serology
  • Swabs for bacteria C&S (culture and sensitivity), viral PCR
  • Tissue culture / PCR

Describe how imaging is performed to investigate for dermatological conditions.

  • Internal organ involvement is assessed using imaging techniques
  • Vascular supply of organs is also assessed

Describe what specific tests are performed to investigate for dermatological conditions.

  • Urinalysis
  • Nerve conduction studies
  • Endocrine investigations

What is a punch biopsy?

A punch biopsy is a procedure where a small, cylindrical piece of skin and underlying tissue is removed using a circular cutting tool, and the tissue is then examined under a microscope to diagnose skin conditions or potential cancers

Describe the role of a punch biopsy in the diagnosis of lupus erythematosus.

A sample of skin usually taken under local anaesthetic will usually be sent for another test such as histology in which the patterns, shapes and cell populations within the tissue can be analysed

Punch Biopsy.

What is pancytopenia?

A reduction in red blood cell, white blood cell and platelet levels

Describe the significance of ESR/CRP.

Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) are biomarkers of inflammation, indicating potential infections, autoimmune disorders, or other inflammatory conditions

What is ANA?

  • ANA refers to antinuclear antibodies, a type of autoantibody that the immune system produces that can be a sign of autoimmune diseases
  • NOTE: ANAs are antibodies that target the cell nucleus

Describe the significance of ANA.

A positive ANA test indicates that ANAs are present in the blood,

which can be a sign of an autoimmune disease, but it's not a definitive diagnosis What is lupus erythematosus?

Lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease that causes the body's immune system to attack its own tissues

Recall the two types of lupus erythematosus.

  • Systemic Lupus Erythematosus
  • Cutaneous (discoid) Lupus Erythematosus

Describe the difference between these two forms of lupus.

  • Systemic lupus erythematosus affects the entire body, potentially causing inflammation in joints, organs, and blood vessels
  • Cutaneous (discoid) lupus erythematosus primarily affects the skin

Describe the relationship between these two forms of lupus.

These two types of lupus often overlap in symptoms and signs

Lupus Erythematosus.

Recall the three types of findings in systemic lupus.

  • Mucocutaneous
  • Haematological
  • Immunological
  • Joint/Organic

Recall three mucocutaneous findings of lupus.

  • Cutaneous lupus - acute
  • Cutaneous lupus - chronic
  • Oral ulcers
  • Alopecia — hair loss

Recall three joint/organ findings of lupus.

  • Synovitis
  • Serositis (pleurisy/pleuritis or pericarditis)
  • Renal disorder
  • Neurological disorder
  • Photodistributed (sun-exposed) rashes

What is synovitis?

  • Synovitis is inflammation of the synovial membrane that lines the joints
  • The synovial membrane is responsible for the production of synovial fluid, which nourishes and lubricates the joint

Synovitis.

What is serositis?

Serositis refers to inflammation of the serous membranes,

which are the tissues lining the lungs, heart, and abdomen

The peritoneum, pericardium and pleura are all serous membranes

Anatomy Of The Lung Pleura.

Recall three haematological findings of lupus.

  • Haemolytic anaemia
  • Thrombocytopenia
  • Leukopenia
  • NOTE: All three together lead to pancytopenia

Recall three immunological findings of lupus.

  • Anti-nuclear antibody (ANA)
  • Anti-dsDNA antibodies
  • Anti-Sm antibodies
  • Antiphospholipid antibodies
  • Low Complement
  • Direct Coomb’s test

What is anti-dsDNA?

Anti-dsDNA antibodies are a type of antinuclear antibody (ANA) that targets double-stranded DNA

What is anti-Sm?

  • Anti-Sm antibodies are directed against the Sm (Smith) antigen, which is a complex of proteins associated with small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (snRNPs)
  • Anti-Sm antibodies are highly specific for systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)

What are antiphospholipid antibodies?

  • Antiphospholipid antibodies are autoantibodies that mistakenly target phospholipid-binding proteins, leading to an increased risk of blood clotting (thrombosis) and pregnancy complications
  • NOTE: Phospholipid-binding proteins interact with phospholipids within cell membranes to induce changes to the cell according to the requirements dictated by the extracellular environment

Apoptotic Cell Clearance (Immune Tolerance)

When cells undergo apoptosis, they expose phosphatidylserine (PS).

MFG-E8 (Lactadherin) and Annexin V (these are both PBPs) bind PS and help phagocytes (like macrophages) recognise and engulf dying cells

What is a direct Coomb’s test?

The direct Coomb’s test, also known as the direct antiglobulin test (DAT), detects antibodies or complement proteins that are already attached to the surface of red blood cells (RBCs), helping diagnose conditions like autoimmune haemolytic anaemia

Recall three ways that systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) can manifest on the skin.

  • Chilblains — due to systemic microvascular involvement
  • Photodistributed rash
  • Cutaneous vasculitis — due to systemic vascular dysfunction
  • Alopecia — systemic alopecia disrupts the hair cycle
  • Livedo reticularis — due to systemic vascular dysfunction
  • Subacute cutaneous lupus (SCLE)

Recall two ways that cutaneous/discoid lupus erythematosus can manifest on the skin.

  • Discoid lupus erythematosus
  • Subacute cutaneous lupus (SCLE)

What are chilblains?

Chilblains is a condition that causes inflamed swollen patches and blistering on the hands and feet caused by exposure to damp air that's cold but not freezing

Chilblains.

What are photodistributed rashes?

Photodistributed rashes are skin reactions or eruptions that occur or worsen due to exposure to sunlight or artificial light

Photodistributed Rashes.

What is livedo reticularis?

Livedo reticularis is a skin condition characterised by a net-like or mottled pattern of reddish-blue discoloration, often appearing on the legs, arms, or torso

Livedo Reticularis.

What are palpable purpura?

Palpable purpura are raised, firm, purple or reddish-purple spots on the skin that can be felt when touched, caused by inflammation of small blood vessels (vasculitis)

Palpable Purpura.

What is subacute cutaneous lupus?

Subacute cutaneous lupus erythematosus (SCLE) is a subtype of cutaneous lupus erythematosus characterised by red, scaly, raised, and/or ring-shaped rashes that typically appear in sun- exposed areas like the neck, scalp, arms, and upper back

Subacute Cutaneous Lupus (Scle).

What is discoid lupus?

  • Discoid lupus erythematosus (DLE) is characterised by persistent, localised, red-pink or dark, scaly areas, often on the face, scalp, and ears, that can lead to scarring and hair loss
  • NOTE: Discoid = round which describes the shape of the lesions often when they first arrive on the skin

Discoid Lupus.

Describe how lupus can be identified neonatally.

  • A ring-like or annular rash can allow immediate diagnosis of neonatal lupus, which is important because there is a 50% risk of heart block
  • It is important to perform an ECG

What is dermatomyositis?

Dermatomyositis is an autoimmune connective tissue disease

which affects the skin and the muscles

It generally causes muscle weakness and a distinctive skin rash

Describe which body parts are usually affected by dermatomyositis.

  • Skin
  • Muscles

Describe how dermatomyositis affects the muscles.

Proximal extensor inflammatory myopathy (muscles) — i.e pathology of the muscles close to the centre of the body

Describe how dermatomyositis affects the skin.

Photo-distributed pink-violet rashes favour the scalp, periocular regions and extensor surfaces (skin)

Recall four clinical features of dermatomyositis.

  • Gottron’s papules
  • Ragged cuticles
  • Shawl sign
  • Heliotrope rash
  • Photosensitive erythema

What are Gottron’s papules?

Gottron's papules are flat-topped, red or violet-coloured bumps that appear on the dorsal surfaces of the knuckles (metacarpophalangeal joints) and finger joints (proximal and distal interphalangeal joints)

What are ragged cuticles?

  • Cuticles are the thin layers of dead, clear skin located at the base of the nails, acting as a protective barrier against bacteria and debris
  • Ragged cuticles describes when this layer becomes dry, cracked, irregular or uneven

Ragged Cuticles.

What is Shawl sign?

The Shawl sign is a characteristic rash in dermatomyositis, appearing as a red, flat, or sometimes raised area across the shoulders, upper back, and sometimes extending to the neck and upper arms.

Shawl Sign.

What is a heliotrope rash?

A heliotrope rash is a characteristic reddish-purple rash, often accompanied by swelling, that appears on the upper eyelids and is a sign of dermatomyositis

Heliotrope Rash.

What is photosensitive erythema?

Photosensitive erythema is a skin condition where the skin becomes inflamed and red (erythema) when exposed to sunlight or other forms of light, often causing a rash, itching, or burning sensation

Photosensitive Erythema.

Describe what influences the subtype of dermatomyositis that presents in a patient.

Subtypes of dermatomyositis can be predicted by autoantibody profile

Describe the use of the autoantibody profile in determining the subtype of dermatomyositis.

Different subtypes of dermatomyositis have different clinical features that can be predicted by autoantibody profile

Recall three tests that can be conducted to diagnose dermatomyositis.

  • Blood tests including ANA, CK, LFTs
  • Skin biopsy
  • LFT — ALT often increased
  • Electromyography (EMG) — to assess the health and function of your skeletal muscles
  • Muscle MRI
  • Screening for internal malignancy — CT scan CAP (Chest, Abdomen, Pelvis) — this is not so much for diagnosis, but for management of the condition

What is ALT?

ALT stands for alanine aminotransferase, an enzyme primarily found in the liver that helps break down proteins

Describe the significance of increased ALT.

An ALT (alanine transaminase) blood test measures the amount of the enzyme ALT in your blood, which is primarily found in the liver and released into the bloodstream when liver cells are damaged, indicating potential liver problems • ALT is elevated in dermatomyositis due to muscle cell damage and potential liver involvement • NOTE: ALT is primarily found in the liver, but also in smaller amounts in the kidneys, heart, and muscles

Describe the significance of increased creatine kinase (CK).

  • A creatine kinase (CK) blood test measures the amount of the enzyme CK in your blood, which can indicate muscle damage or heart-related conditions
  • Elevated CK levels may suggest skeletal muscle, heart, or brain damage or degeneration

What is vasculitis?

Vasculitis describes a group of rare conditions that damage blood vessels by causing inflammation or swelling

Describe a clinical finding that can be made when further investigating vasculitis.

  • Antibody presence
  • This can be detected using a biopsy for direct immunofluorescence

What is IgA vasculitis?

IgA vasculitis is a disease that causes the antibody immunoglobulin A to collect in small blood vessels, which then become inflamed and leak blood

Recall an alternative name for IgA vasculitis.

Henoch-Schönlein purpura

Recall three clinical features of IgA vasculitis (Henoch-Schönlein purpura).

  • Abdominal pain
  • Bleeding
  • Arthralgia
  • Arthritis
  • IgA- associated glomerulonephritis - may develop later —

can lead to kidney failure Recall three types of vasculitis classifications based on size of affected vessels.

What is Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms (DRESS)?

This is where patients develop a rash and systemic upset incorporating haematological and solid‐organ disturbances

What is lymphadenopathy?

Lymphadenopathy is a disease of the lymph nodes, in which they are abnormal in size or consistency

Describe the significance of atypical lymphocytes.

Atypical lymphocytes (reactive lymphocytes) are a sign that your body is fighting off an infection (viral or bacterial) or responding to other stimuli like autoimmune diseases or drug reaction

What happens to lymphocytes during viral infection?

(see lecture diagram / notes)

Describe the significance of peripheral hypereosinophilia.

Peripheral hypereosinophilia can indicate various underlying conditions, including allergic reactions and parasitic infections

What is mycoplasma?

  • Mycoplasma is a genus of bacteria that, like the other members of the class Mollicutes, lack a cell wall, and its peptidoglycan, around their cell membrane
  • The absence of peptidoglycan makes them naturally resistant to antibiotics such as the beta-lactam antibiotics that target cell wall synthesis

Describe how DRESS is diagnosed.

Diagnosis of DRESS is based on a scoring criteria

Recall two of the criteria used to diagnose DRESS.

  • Fever ≥ 38.5°C
  • Lymphadenopathy ⩾ 2 sites, › 1cm
  • Circulating atypical lymphocytes
  • Peripheral hypereosinophilia ›0.7 × 109
  • Internal organs involved - (liver, kidneys, cardiac)
  • Negative ANA
  • Hepatitis
  • Mycoplasma, chlamydia
  • Skin involvement — ›50% Body Surface Area — Cutaneous eruption suggestive of DRESS (i.e characteristic rash morphologies) — Biopsy suggestive of DRESS

Recall two internal organs that are involved in DRESS.

  • Liver (hepatitis) — associated with the majority of deaths
  • Kidneys (interstitial nephritis)
  • Heart (myocarditis)
  • Brain
  • Thyroid (thyroiditis)
  • Lungs (interstitial pneumonitis)

Describe the mechanism of DRESS.

Underlying mechanism not known

Describe the onset of DRESS.

DRESS starts 2-6 weeks after drug exposure

Recall the most common organ involved in DRESS.

Liver is the most common internal organ involved — majority of deaths associated with this

Recall five triggers of DRESS.

  • Allopurinol (to treat gout)
  • Sulfonamides
  • Anti-epileptics (carbamazepine, phenytoin, lamotrigine)
  • Antibiotics (vancomycin, amoxicillin, minocycline, piperacillin- tazobactam)
  • Ibuprofen

Recall five rash morphologies of DRESS.

  • Urticated papular exanthem - widespread papules
  • Maculopapular (morbilliform) eruption — Morbilliform refers to a rash that resembles measles, characterised by widespread, pink to red flat or slightly raised spots (macules and papules)
  • Widespread erythema (Erythroderma)
  • Head / neck oedema
  • Erythema multiforme-like

Erythema Multiforme.

Describe the treatment of DRESS.

  • Withdrawal of culprit — difficult to identify in cases of polypharmacy
  • Corticosteroids are first line treatment — may require months of treatment

Describe the prognosis of DRESS.

Mortality 5-10%

Drug Reaction With Eosinophilia And Systemic Symptoms

What is pruritus?

  • Pruritus generally refers to itching without a rash and is suggestive of internal cause
  • NOTE: An internal cause is generally suggested when there is itching without a rash

Recall two haematological causes of pruritus.

  • Lymphoma
  • Polycythaemia

What is a lymphoma?

A lymphoma is a cancer derived from lymphocytes — The lymphocytes would be found anywhere in the bloodstream, not the bone marrow

What is polycythaemia?

The presence of too many (specifically red) blood cells in circulation

Recall five other causes of pruritus.

  • Uraemia
  • Cholestasis
  • Iron deficiency or iron overload
  • HIV / Hepatitis A/B/C
  • Cancer
  • Drugs (NB opiates / opioids)
  • Psychogenic
  • Pruritus of old age

  • Full Blood Count, LDH
  • Renal profile
  • Liver function tests
  • Ferritin
  • X-Ray Chest
  • HIV / Hepatitis A/B/C

What is ferritin?

Ferritin is a protein inside cells that stores iron
