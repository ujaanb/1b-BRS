Overview

What is the term used to describe early embryo nutrition?

Histiotrophic

Why is this term used?

  • “ Histio ” means tissue (histology). “ Trophic” means feeding
  • Histiotrophism displayed by the fetus is in relation to the fact it breaks down endometrial tissues and relies on uterine gland secretions to survive
  • It achieves this using its syncitiotrophoblast population

What switch occurs from the first to the second trimester and why does it occur?

  • From histiotrophism to haemotrophism
  • This is because the histiotrophic nutrition is not enough to sustain the fetus through its entire growth

Through which structure is this achieved?

A haemochorial-type placenta

What is a haemochorial-type placenta?

A placenta where the maternal blood is in direct contact with fetal membranes

What adaptation of the placenta allows the haemochorial-type placenta to enable fetal-maternal exchange?

  • Chorionic villi
  • These finger-like protrusions from the chorion of the embryo allow the embryo to come into direct contact with the maternal circulation

Describe how the demands of the fetus on the placenta change throughout pregnancy.

Fetal oxygen and nutrient demands increase as pregnancy progresses

Describe an adaptation of fetus that helps to cope with this demand.

Branching of chorionic villi increases with progression through pregnancy to increase surface area for exchange

Chorionic Villus Branching Through Pregnancy.

Describe the difference between the cytotrophoblasts and syncitiotrophoblasts.

  • The cytotrophoblasts are used as a source from which syncitiotrophoblast cells can arise via differentiation
  • The syncitiotrophoblasts are invasive cells and they invade into the uterine endometrium in order to allow the embryo to implant itself

What happens to the inner cell mass of the embryo during the peri- implantation window?

The inner cell mass forms two populations within it — The epiblast and hypoblast

Describe the positioning of the epiblast and hypoblast.

The epiblast is outer in relation to the hypoblast

Describe the difference between the epiblast and the hypoblast.

  • The epiblast is used to form the actual fetal tissues
  • The hypoblast is used to form some extra-embryonic cells (of the yolk sac) — the yolk sac provides gas and nutrients/food for the embryo before development of the placenta

What happens after this peri-implantation window and when does it occur?

Bilaminar disc formation (~ day 12)

What occurs during bilaminar disc formation?

  • Some of the epiblast and all of the hypoblast pull away from the top layer of the epiblast to form a disc shaped-structure that has a hole in the middle
  • The bottom layer of this disc has two layers made of epiblast and hypoblast
  • This hole in between is called the amniotic cavity and the embryo is now ready for gastrulation

Origins Of The Placenta.

What are the intervillous spaces of the placenta?

These spaces are maternal, placental regions located around the fetus’ chorionic villi that enable exchange of substances between the mother and the fetus

Recall another name for the intervillous spaces?

Lacunae

Describe the role of spiral artery remodelling in fetal development.

  • Spiral arteries are used to supply the intervillous spaces (lacunae) of the placenta
  • In order to meet the demands of the fetus, the spiral arteries are de- spiralised, broadened out and resistance within them is decreased

Placental Structure.

What is the chorion?

It is a surrounding layer of cells found within the embryo during development, normally at the stage where it invades the placenta — It surrounds the early conceptus unit

What are chorionic villi?

Chorionic villi are tiny, finger-like projections of the chorionic cytotrophoblast found within the placenta

Describe the function of the chorionic villi.

To provide substantial surface area for exchange of gas, nutrients and waste between the fetus and its mother

Describe the stages involved in chorionic villus formation.

  • Primary: outgrowth of the cytotrophoblast and branching of these extensions
  • Secondary: growth of the fetal mesoderm into the primary villi
  • Tertiary: growth of the umbilical artery and umbilical vein into the villus mesoderm, providing vasculature

Chorionic Villus Formation (2).

Describe how the positioning of the chorionic villi allow for absorption of nutrients from the blood.

  • The villus are positioned within the maternal blood spaces (intervillous spaces), basically floating around in the blood circulation — This provides a large surface area for exchange to occur
  • The villi are also arranged in a convoluted knot of vessels and vessel dilation slows blood flow enabling exchange between maternal and fetal blood

Describe how the structure of the chorionic villi changes throughout pregnancy to meet the needs of the fetus.

  • The villi are covered in cytotrophoblast cells throughout pregnancy
  • However, at the beginning of pregnancy, the cytotrophoblast layer is thick, and it becomes thinner to allow a shorter distance across

which exchange can occur

  • In early pregnancy, the villi are ~150-200µm in diameter, approx. 10µm in trophoblast thickness between capillaries and maternal blood
  • In late pregnancy, the villi thin to 40µm, vessels move within villi to leave only 1-2µm trophoblast separation from maternal blood
  • NOTE: Chorionic villi contain fetal capillaries

Conversion Of Spiral Arteries.

What issues can arise if the spiral artery isn’t remodelled correctly?

(see lecture diagram / notes)

What is the spiral artery?

  • Spiral arteries are small arteries which supply blood to the endometrium of the uterus for maternal-fetal exchange
  • They supply the maternal blood spaces within the endometrium and they are a branch of the uterine arteries

Describe how the process of spiral artery remodelling actually works.

  • Extra-villus trophoblast (EVT) cells coating the villi invade down into the maternal spiral arteries, forming endovascular EVT
  • Endothelium and smooth muscle is broken down – and instead the EVT coats inside of vessels
  • This leads to conversion and turns the spiral artery into a low pressure, high capacity conduit for maternal blood flow

Describe the difference between extra-villus trophoblast (EVT) cells and endovascular extra-villus trophoblast (EVT) cells.

Extra-villus trophoblast (EVT) cells originate within the cytotrophoblast layer of the chorionic villi, before invading the spiral artery

When these EVT cells invade the spiral artery and coat the inside of the vessels, they become extra-villus trophoblast (EVT) cells

(see lecture diagram / notes)

Spiral Artery Conversion.

Describe how the endovascular extra-villus trophoblast (EVT) cells perform spiral artery remodelling.

  • EVT cell invasion triggers endothelial cells to release chemokines, recruiting immune cells
  • Immune cells invade spiral artery walls and begin to disrupt vessel walls
  • EVT cells secrete and break down normal vessel wall extracellular matrix (ECM) and replace with a new matrix known as fibrinoid

Describe what hence happens in failed conversion of spiral arteries during remodelling.

  • In failed conversion, smooth muscle remains, immune cells become embedded in vessel wall and vessels become occluded by RBCs
  • NOTE: So it's not just that we haven't remodelled them properly, we haven't remodelled them properly, and then there's consequences on top of that as well (immune cells stuck in wall)

Successful Vs. Failed Spiral Artery Remodelling.

Describe the general consequences of failed spiral artery remodelling.

  • Unconverted spiral arteries are vulnerable to pathological change including intimal hyperplasia (of the tunica intima layer) and atherosis
  • This can lead to perturbed flow and local hypoxia, free radical damage and inefficient delivery of substrates into the intervillous space
  • Retained smooth muscle may allow residual contractile capacity → perturb blood delivery to the intravillous space
  • Atherosis can also occur in basal (non-spiral) arteries that would not normally be targeted by trophoblast

What is pre-eclampsia?

  • Pre-eclampsia is a serious pregnancy complication defined as new onset hypertension (in a previously normotensive woman) with BP ≥140 mmHg systolic and/or ≥90 mmHg diastolic
  • Occurring after 20 weeks’ gestation

What is eclampsia?

  • Eclampsia is the onset of seizures in a woman with pre-eclampsia
  • NOTE: Pre-eclampsia is the precursor to eclampsia

Epidemiology Of Pre-Eclampsia.

How is pre-eclampsia diagnosed?

  • New onset hypertension (in a previously normotensive woman), BP ≥140 mmHg systolic and/or ≥90 mmHg diastolic
  • Occurring after 20 weeks’ gestation

Describe the general clinical features of pre-eclampsia.

  • Reduced fetal movement and/or amniotic fluid volume (by ultrasound) — in 30% cases
  • Oedema common but not discriminatory for pre-eclampsia
  • Proteinuria
  • Headache — in around 40% of severe PE patients
  • Abdominal pain — in around 15% of severe PE patients
  • Visual disturbances, seizures and breathlessness associated with severe PE and risk of eclampsia (seizures)

Recall the distinct forms of pre-eclampsia.

  • Early onset: ‹34 weeks
  • Late onset: ›34 weeks

Describe the characteristics of early onset pre-eclampsia.

  • Associated with fetal and maternal symptoms
  • Changes in placental structure

Describe the characteristics of late onset pre-eclampsia.

  • More common (90%)
  • Mostly maternal symptoms
  • Fetus generally OK
  • Less overt/no placental changes

Which is more common, early or late onset pre-eclampsia?

Late onset (90%) — ›34 weeks

Layers Of The Blood Vessel.

What are the risks of pre-eclampsia to the mother during pregnancy? Mother

  • Damage to kidneys, liver, brain and other organ systems — emissions from distressed placenta → damage to kidneys via endothelial damage
  • Possible progression to eclampsia — characterised by seizures, loss of consciousness
  • HELLP syndrome — H aemolysis, El evated Liver Enzymes, L ow P latelets
  • Placental abruption — separation of the placenta from the endometrium

What is HELLP syndrome?

HELLP syndrome is a life-threatening pregnancy complication usually considered to be a variant of pre-eclampsia

What are the risks of pre-eclampsia to the fetus during pregnancy? Fetus

  • Preterm birth
  • Reduced fetal growth — Intrauterine Growth Restriction (IUGR)
  • Pregnancy loss/stillbirth
  • Fetal death (500,000/ year worldwide)

Can pre-eclampsia occur after birth?

Yes.

Describe what exactly is wrong with spiral artery conversion in pre- eclampsia.

  • EVT invasion of maternal spiral arteries is limited to decidual/endometrial layer
  • Hence, spiral artery remodelling occurs, but not sufficiently deep enough into the myometrial layer of the uterus — spiral arteries are not extensively remodelled

Describe the consequence of this aberrant remodelling.

  • Placental perfusion is restricted
  • Placental ischaemia occurs

What is the difference between the decidua and the endometrium?

The decidua is a specialised, temporary lining of the uterus formed from the endometrium

Spiral Artery Origin.

What is placental growth factor?

Placental growth factor (PlGF) is a protein belonging to the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) family that is released in large amounts by the placenta during pregnancy

Describe the role of placental growth factor.

VEGF related, pro-angiogenic factor released in large amounts by the placenta

What is soluble VEGFR1 (Flt1)?

This is a soluble receptor for VEGF-like factors such as Placental growth factor

Describe the role of soluble VEGFR1 (Flt1).

Soluble receptor for VEGF-like factors which binds soluble angiogenic factors to limit their bioavailablility — higher soluble VEGFR (Flt1) levels leads to lower placental growth factor (PlGF) bioavailability

Describe how the balance of sFlt1 and PlGF is altered in pre-eclampsia.

  • In pre-eclampsia, there is lowered PlGF levels and increased sFlt1 levels
  • This increases the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio, decreasing PlGF bioavailability leading to endothelial dysfunction

Explained Further.

Describe how this alteration in Flt1 and PlGF levels leads to to pre-eclampsia. Healthy placenta

  • Releases PLGF and VEGF into the maternal circulation. These growth factors bind receptors on the endothelial surface to promote vasodilation, anti- coagulation and ‘healthy’ maternal endothelial cells Pre-eclampsia placenta
  • Releases sFLT1, which acts as a sponge - mopping up PLGF and VEGF and stopping them binding to the endothelial surface receptors
  • In the absence of these signals, the endothelial cells become dysfunctional

Bioavailability.

Recall another biochemical perpetrator in pre-eclampsia other than PlGF/Flt1.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs)

What are extracellular vesicles?

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are tiny (nano-meter scale) lipid-bilayer laminated vesicles released by almost all cell types

Recall three types of extracellular vesicle.

  • Exosomes
  • Oncosomes — released by tumour cells
  • Microvesicles
  • SASP

Describe when extracellular vesicles are produced.

They are produced generally when cells are in trouble or dying

Describe the contents of extracellular vesicles.

  • They contain diverse cargos, including mRNAs, proteins and microRNAs (miRNAs) — they also lipids, nucleic acids and proteins
  • The cargos are dependent on the cell from which they are released from

Describe the effect of extracellular vesicles.

They can influence cell behaviour (locally and at distance)

Describe how extracellular vesicle (EV) number changes in pre-eclampsia. Changes observed in EV number and composition in PE

  • Overall increase in EVs in the maternal circulation
  • Increase in endothelial-derived EVs (indicative of maternal circulation defects)
  • However, there is a decrease in placenta-derived EVs — the reason behind this is unknown

What exactly about extracellular vesicles (EVs) contributes to the pathogenesis of pre-eclampsia?

  • It is the cargos that the EVs are carrying that determine its effects
  • Hence, while there may be a reduced number of placenta-derived EVs in pre-eclampsia, the cargos may be distressing, leading to negative effects

Describe what the experiment with mice and extracellular vesicles (EVs) informs us on the role of extracellular vesicles (EVs) in pre-eclampsia.

  • EVs from normotensive and hypertensive placentas were injected into mice
  • The EVs from the normotensive placentas did not have negative effects, but the EVs from the hypertensive placentas had negative effects

Where are most placenta-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) thought to be derived from?

Syncitiotrophoblasts — Syncitiotrophoblast-derived extracellular vesicles (SDEVs)

  • Current theory: existing maternal genetic pre-disposition to cardiovascular disease,

which manifests during the ‘stress-test’ of pregnancy What is placentation?

Recall two possible maternal/environmental factors that lead to abnormal placentation.

  • Placental Th1 Predominance

Recall three problems that occur during placentation that can lead to placental ischaemia.

  • Proliferative › Invasive Trophoblasts
  • Superficial invasion
  • Narrow maternal vessels

Recall two consequences of placental ischaemia.

  • Disrupted EVs
  • Small for gestational age

Recall the major consequence of placental ischaemia and what this leads to.

  • Increased circulating sFlt1 and sEng (soluble endoglin, implicated in pre-eclampsia)
  • This leads to systemic vascular dysfunction

Recall four effects of systemic vascular dysfunction.

  • Proteinuria
  • Hypertension
  • Visual disturbances/Headache/Cerebral Oedema & Seizures (Eclampsia)
  • HELLP Syndrome/Coagulation abnormalities
