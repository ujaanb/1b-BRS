Overview

What are early life impacts?

Early life impacts refer to the profound and lasting effects experiences during childhood have on a person's physical, mental, and emotional well-being throughout their lifespan

Recall three challenges a fetus may face in utero that may have lasting impacts on its health.

  • Fetal infection in utero
  • Maternal nutrition (under/over)
  • Maternal illness
  • Maternal stress
  • Maternal medication
  • Environmental factors/exposures

What is the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) hypothesis?

The Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) hypothesis suggests a link between rate of weight gain from childhood through to adulthood and risk of a coronary event

Describe what is explained within the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) hypothesis.

  • On average, adults who had a coronary event had been small at birth and thin at two years of age
  • Thereafter put on weight rapidly
  • The risk of coronary events was more strongly related to the rate of change of childhood BMI, rather than to the BMI attained at any particular age of childhood

Explain the reasoning behind the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) hypothesis.

  • Undernutrition in utero → Overnutrition as a child
  • This overnutrition leads to increased risk of ‘Metabolic Syndrome’,

which in turn leads to increased risk of cardiovascular events What is metabolic syndrome?

  • Metabolic syndrome is a group of conditions that increase the risk of heart disease, stroke and type 2 diabetes
  • These conditions include high blood pressure, high blood sugar, too much fat around the waist, and high cholesterol or triglyceride levels

What is meant by “fetal programming”?

This is the concept of programming the foetus as a result of the environmental stimuli that it's experiencing in the womb in preparation for particular conditions later in life

Describe how fetal programming affects development in utero.

Programming in utero may lead to changes which influence development and physiology

What are predictive adaptive responses (PARs)?

This is where an organism modifies its development in response to environmental cues, anticipating a future environment that may be different from the current one

Describe how predictive adaptive responses (PARs) impact development in utero.

  • PARs are proposed to be developmental adaptations taken to prepare the fetus for its future environment
  • PARs don’t benefit the fetus immediately, but are taken in anticipation of the environment they will be exposed to

Describe how a mismatch between predictive adaptive responses (PARs) and actual environment may lead to disease risk later in life.

If a fetus acquires PARs in anticipation of a particular post-natal environment, but then encounters a different environment to that predicted, it will be mal-adapted, potentially raising the risk of ill- health in later life

Developmental (Uterine) Vs. Mature Environment.

Recall three factors that may negatively impact fetal developmental responses.

  • Maternal health and environment
  • Fetal nutrient demand › supply
  • Endocrine environment
  • Placental vascular supply

Recall four fetal developmental responses to its environment in utero.

  • Altered endocrinology/metabolism
  • Changes in foetal bone, lean and fat mass
  • Altered blood flow/vascular loading
  • Altered immune responses

Recall some conditions which are believed to have fetal origins.

  • Cardio-vascular
  • Metabolic
  • Lung and immune mediated diseases
  • Neurodevelopmental disorders

Describe the three main mechanisms thought to influence fetal physiology and programming.

  • Hormonal effects (especially glucocorticoid exposure)
  • Epigenetic modifications
  • Irreversible developmental changes in organ size/structure

Recall the enzyme that usually regulates fetal glucocorticoid exposure.

11-beta hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase 2 (11BHSD 2)

Describe how this enzyme regulates fetal glucocorticoid exposure.

It protects the fetus from glucocorticoid exposure by converting cortisol into inactive cortisone

Describe how a reduction in this enzyme’s levels can affect fetal development.

  • Reduction in 11BHSD2 expression or increased maternal GCs may lead to greater fetal GC exposure
  • This, in turn, ‘programmes’ fetal growth, development and metabolism — Wider HPA axis dysregulation, changes in GC receptor expression

Glucocorticoids In Fetuses.

What is an epigenetic change?

Epigenetic changes are genetic changes that modify the expression of genes without modifying DNA sequence

Recall three types of epigenetic change.

  • DNA methylation
  • Post-translational (protein) modification of histones — DNA is wrapped around histones
  • Non- coding RNAs

Describe the factor that influences the nature of epigenetic changes a fetus experiences.

In-utero exposures can modify the types or levels of these epigenetic marks, leading to altered/dysregulated gene expression

Can epigenetic changes be passed down?

  • Yes.
  • Acquired epigenetic changes can be passed down across generations

Recall four changes that can result from fetal epigenomic changes.

  • Fetal growth restriction
  • Increased capacity to store energy → obesity
  • Adaptations in metabolic pathways → Diabetes mellitus
  • Adaptations in terminally differentiated cell numbers →

Describe a result of increased capacity to store energy.

Obesity

Describe a result of adaptations in metabolic pathways.

Diabetic mellitus

Recall two cell types that are affected as a result of adaptations in terminally differentiated cell numbers.

  • Cardiomyoctes
  • Neuronal cells

Recall the effect of adaptations in terminally differentiated cardiomyocyte numbers.

  • Hypertension
  • Cardiovascular disorders

Recall the effect of adaptations in terminally differentiated neuron numbers.

Risk of stroke, schizophrenia, depression, cognitive dysfunction

Recall the three key windows where epigenetic changes occur in development and may lead to vulnerability and how they are affected.

  • Gametogenesis — parent-specific epigenetic marks are established during the development of sperm and oocytes
  • Early development — very early embryos undergo widespread erasure and re- patterning of epigenetic marks during which these gamete- specific marks are erased and new epigenetic profiles established.
  • Organogenesis and fetal growth — epigenetic marks influence timing and onset of cell-type-specific gene expression, influencing how cells differentiate
  • NOTE: Post-natal period and adulthood and ageing are also vulnerable periods post-natally

Epigenetic Changes: Vulnerable Windows.

Describe how in utero programming can affect the development of organ systems.

  • Environmental stimuli have been shown to impact the development of key organ systems, pre-disposing to adult disease
  • Examples include: — Fetal hypoxia → reduced nephron numbers → increased risk of hypertension/renal disease in adulthood

Describe how in utero programming can lead to metabolic syndrome/diabetes mellitus.

Fetal undernutrition → reduced beta cell mass/altered muscle insulin sensitivity → impaired glucose control in adulthood

What are Primordial Germ Cells (PGCs)?

Primordial Germ Cells (PGCs) are the embryonic precursor cells of oocytes and spermatozoa

Describe how changes to Primordial Germ Cells (PGCs) can affect generations.

  • PGCs undergo epigenetic reprogramming during embryogenesis
  • These cells then give rise to sperm and egg – which transmit these epigenetic marks to the next generation — i.e. the exposed fetus’ offspring
  • Experimental data from animal models indicates that fetal germ cell development is sensitive to environmental impacts — e.g diet, pharmaceuticals
