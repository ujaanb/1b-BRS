Overview

Recall the three hormones involved in the control of serum calcium levels.

  • Vitamin D (calcitriol)
  • Parathyroid hormone (PTH)
  • Calcitonin

Which two of these hormones are known to have substantial physiological effects?

  • Vitamin D (calcitriol)
  • Parathyroid hormone (PTH)

Where is vitamin D produced?

Synthesised in the skin or intake via diet

Where is parathyroid hormone (PTH) produced?

Parathyroid gland

Where is calcitonin produced?

Parafollicular cells (of the thyroid gland)

What is the role of vitamin D and parathyroid hormone (PTH)?

Main regulators of calcium (& phosphate) homeostasis

What is the role of calcitonin?

(see lecture diagram / notes)

Can reduce calcium acutely, but no negative effect if parafollicular cells are removed

Recall the two main forms of vitamin D and where they are derived from.

  • Vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol) - diet
  • Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) - skin

Describe the steps involved in the synthesis and metabolism of vitamin D from the skin.

  • 7-dehydrocholesterol (found in the skin) → pre-vitamin D3 → vitamin D3 in the skin
  • Vitamin D3 is hydroxylated to 25-hydroxycholecalciferol in the liver — This is catalysed by 25-hydroxylase and the vitamin D3 is not biologically active here
  • 25-hydroxycholecalciferol is hydroxylated to 1,25 dihydroxycholecalciferol (calcitriol) in the kidney — This is catalysed by 1-alpha-hydroxylase and is the vitamin D3 is now biologically active

Describe the steps involved in the synthesis and metabolism of vitamin D from the the diet.

  • Vitamin D2 is hydroxylated to 25-hydroxyergocalciferol in the liver — This is catalysed by 25-hydroxylase and the vitamin D2 is not biologically active here
  • 25 hydroxyergocalciferol is hydroxylated to 1,25 dihydroxyergocalciferol (calcitriol) in the kidney — This is catalysed by 1-alpha-hydroxylase and is the vitamin D2 is now biologically active

How can 25-OH cholecalciferol be used clinically and why is it used over 1,25(OH)2 cholecalciferol?

  • Measuring calcitriol (1,25-OH vitamin D) directly in the serum is very difficult
  • Because 25-OH cholecalciferol is a precursor to calcitriol (active vitamin D), measuring 25-OH cholecalciferol is a way to mitigate the difficulties and find an alternative way to measure vitamin D levels

When can 25-OH cholecalciferol not be used clinically and why?

  • 25-OH cholecalciferol cannot be used as a measure for vitamin D levels in patients with renal failure
  • This is because their kidney does not produce the 1-alpha- hydroxylase required to convert 25-OH cholecalciferol into calcitriol, so we have no idea how much vitamin D3 they actually have

What is the major role of vitamin D (calcitriol) in the body?

  • To increase Ca2+ and PO4 3- reabsorption from the gut and kidney to the blood
  • This increases serum calcium and phosphate

Describe the effects that vitamin D (calcitriol) has on the gut.

It increases Ca2+ and PO4 3- absorption from the gut back into the blood

Describe the effects that vitamin D (calcitriol) has on the kidney.

It increases Ca2+ and PO4 3- reabsorption from the kidney back into the blood

Describe the effects that vitamin D (calcitriol) has on the bone.

By increasing serum calcium and phosphate levels, vitamin D provides building blocks for bone

Effects Of Calcitriol.

What is the major role of parathyroid hormone (PTH) in the body?

To increase serum calcium levels

Describe the effects that parathyroid hormone (PTH) has on the bone.

Parathyroid hormone increases Ca2+ resorption from the bone back into the blood

Describe the effects that parathyroid hormone (PTH) has on the kidney.

  • Parathyroid hormone increases Ca2+ reabsorption into the blood from the kidney
  • Parathyroid hormone increases PO4 3- excretion, unlike vitamin D
  • Parathyroid hormone increases 1-alpha hydroxylase activity

Describe the effects that parathyroid hormone (PTH) has on the gut.

  • Parathyroid hormone acts indirectly on the gut, as it stimulates 1- alpha-hydroxylase activity → increased serum calcitriol
  • This in turn increases calcium and phosphate reabsorption from the gut to the blood

Describe the effects that parathyroid hormone (PTH) has on calcitriol.

  • Parathyroid hormone (PTH) increases the activity of 1-alpha- hydroxylase
  • This enzyme is used to convert 25-OH hydroxycholecalciferol → 1,25-OH hydroxycholecalciferol

Parathyroid Hormone (Pth) Action.

What is FGF23?

Fibroblast-related growth factor (FGF23) is a growth factor used to regulate serum phosphate levels

What effect does FGF23 have on serum phosphate?

It lowers serum phosphate

Describe the two ways that FGF23 regulates phosphate levels.

  • FGF-23 can block the sodium-phosphate co-transporter located in the proximal tubular cells, so that it cannot be reabsorbed from the urine back to the blood, increasing urine phosphate levels and so more is excreted
  • FGF-23 also decreases calcitriol levels, and this also reduces reabsorption of phosphate from the gut

How does parathyroid hormone (PTH) reduce phosphate levels?

Parathyroid hormone (PTH) reduces phosphate levels by inhibiting the sodium-phosphate co-transporter located in the proximal tubular cells, and allowing phosphate to be excreted without reabsorption

Recall three symptoms of hypocalcaemia.

  • C onvulsions
  • A rrthymia
  • T etany (involuntary muscular contraction)
  • P araesthesia (tingling)
  • MNEMONIC: CATs go numb (paraesthesia).
  • Chvosteks’ sign
  • Trosseau’s sign

Explain the effects that hypocalcaemia has on patients. (CHATGPT) Hyperexcitability

  • Ca2+ blocks Na+ influx into nerve and skeletal muscle cells
  • Hence, hypocalcaemia leads to increased Na+ into the cells and increased stimulation of these cells
  • This leads to hypocalcaemia causing patients to be jittery due to increased membrane excitability

What is Chvostek’s sign and how is it used diagnostically?

  • It is a test where you tap the facial nerve just below the zygomatic arch (cheek)
  • If there is twitching of facial muscles, this is seen to be increased excitability and there is likely to be hypocalcaemia.
  • There is also facial paraesthesia in patients that display Chvostek’s sign

What is Trosseau’s sign and how is it used diagnostically?

A blood pressure cuff is inflated and this leads to a carpopedal spasm due to increased excitability

Recall three clinical features of hypercalcaemia.

  • Stones - kidney effects — Nephrocalcinosis - caused by kidney having to filter too much calcium, and calcium getting clogged up in the kidneys
  • Abdominal moans - GI effects — Anorexia, nausea, dyspepsia (heartburn and bloating), constipation, pancreatitis
  • Psychic groans — Fatigue, depression, impaired concentration, comas
  • “Stones, abdominal moans and psychic groans”

Recall three causes of hypercalcaemia.

  • Hyperparathyroidism (too much PTH, even in states of high calcium) — i.e parathyroid gland adenoma
  • Malignancy — Certain cancers (squamous cell carcinomas) secrete PTH-related peptide that acts at PTH receptors — ALTERNATIVELY (NOT IN LECTURE): Osteoclasts are activated by cancers which releases more calcium into the blood from the bone)
  • Vitamin D excess (rare)

Why does hypercalcaemia negatively affect muscle contraction? (CHATGPT)

When extracellular calcium is elevated, the membrane becomes less permeable to sodium ions (which are crucial for action potentials).

Describe how malignancy can lead to hypercalcaemia.

  • Certain cancers (i.e squamous cell carcinomas) can metastasise to other areas of the body.
  • The metastasis of these cancers can lead to cells releasing what is known as PTH-related peptide
  • This is a molecule that mimics PTH, so it has the exact same effects,

which would lead to excess PTH and hence excess serum calcium What is PTH-related peptide?

PTH-related peptide is a molecule released by malignant cells that mimics PTH — Its presence can lead to hypercalcaemia

Describe the relationship between parathyroid hormone (PTH) and calcium levels.

  • PTH has the natural effects of increasing serum calcium levels
  • When serum calcium levels fall, PTH levels increase

When serum calcium rises, PTH levels decrease What is primary hyperparathyroidism?

This is a condition where there is excess parathyroid hormone (PTH) so calcium levels also increase

Recall a common cause of primary hyperparathyroidism.

Parathyroid adenoma

Describe how a parathyroid adenoma (primary hyperparathyroidism) leads to excess calcium levels.

  • Parathyroid adenomas lead to the increased and excessive production of parathyroid hormone (PTH)
  • This leads to increased serum calcium levels and there is no negative feedback from calcium as the parathyroid adenoma acts independently, ignoring physiological homeostatic mechanisms of feedback

What is the treatment of primary hyperparathyroidism?

Parathyroidectomy to treat the underlying malignancy

Explain the risks of untreated primary hyperparathyroidism.

  • Osteoporosis — via excess PTH activity (osteoblasts → osteoclasts)
  • Renal calculi (kidney stones)
  • Decreased mental function/mood (psychic groans)

What is secondary hyperparathyroidism?

Secondary hyperparathyroidism is a physiological response to low serum calcium, where the body secretes extra PTH to compensate for these low calcium levels

Describe the difference between primary and secondary hyperparathyroidism in terms of calcium levels.

  • Primary hyperparathyroidism involves a problem (usually adenoma) with the parathyroid glands, causing independent excessive production — PTH levels are high, calcium levels are high
  • Secondary hyperparathyroidism is a physiological response to low serum calcium, where the body secretes extra PTH to compensate for these low levels — PTH levels are high, calcium levels are low
  • Primary = high calcium, Secondary = low calcium

What is the most common cause of secondary hyperparathyroidism?

  • Vitamin D deficiency
  • This in turn, will cause low calcium levels which is the mechanism behind secondary hyperparathyroidism

Recall three possible mechanisms behind vitamin D deficiency.

  • Poor diet
  • Reduced sunlight
  • Renal failure

Describe how secondary hyperparathyroidism is treated.

Vitamin D replacement therapy

How does vitamin D replacement therapy work?

(see lecture diagram / notes)

Give patient 25-hydroxy vitamin D

  • Patient converts this to 1,25 dihydroxycholecalciferol (calcitriol) via 1-alpha-hydroxylase
  • It can be given in two forms: — Ergocalciferol = 25-hydroxy vitamin D2 — Cholecalciferol = 25-hydroxy vitamin D3

In which patients does vitamin D replacement therapy not work?

  • Patients with renal failure
  • This is because these patients are completely unable to make calcitriol so t hey cannot metabolise 25-OH hydroxycholecalciferol → 1,25-OH hydroxycholecalciferol (active vitamin D)

What treatment is given to these patients instead?

Alfacalcidol (active vitamin D)

Why isn’t Alfacalcidol given to all patients then?

This isn’t given to all patients, because it is cheaper to administer 25- hydroxy vitamin D, which obviously cannot be metabolised by patients with renal failure

What is tertiary hyperparathyroidism?

  • A condition where low calcium levels caused by an underlying condition →excess PTH release (and hyperplasia of parathyroid glands) and over a longer period of time → parathyroid hyperplasia causes calcium levels to start increasing again and hypercalcaemia
  • This is rare

Recall two causes of tertiary hyperparathyroidism.

  • Chronic renal failure
  • Chronic vitamin D deficiency — very rare in the UK as it is caused by severe malnutrition
  • Either way, the mechanism is that prolonged hypocalcaemia drives the changes seen In the UK, which type of patients is tertiary hyperparathyroidism most common?
  • Patients with renal failure

Summary Of Hyperparathyroidism.

Describe how we can differentiate between primary and secondary hyperparathyroidism.

  • Primary hyperparathyroidism involves high PTH levels and high serum calcium
  • Secondary hyperparathyroidism involves high PTH levels and low or low/normal serum calcium
