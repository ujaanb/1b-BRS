Describe the difference between a microvascular and macrovascular complication of diabetes.

Arteries, Capillaries And Veins.

Recall three microvascular complications of diabetes.

  • Retinopathy
  • Nephropathy
  • Neuropathy

Recall three macrovascular complications of diabetes.

  • Cerebrovascular disease
  • Ischaemic heart disease
  • Peripheral vascular disease

Describe the relationship between HbA1c levels and one’s risk of developing diabetic microvascular complications.

As one’s HbA1c levels increase, one’s risk of developing diabetic microvascular complications also increase

Hba1C & Risk Of Microvascular Complications.

What is our HbA1c target to reduce the risk of microvascular complications?

  • ‹53 mmol/mol (‹7%)
  • NOTE: HbA1c is just an average, it may not be able to respond to hypoglycaemic episodes that a patient experiences

Why is the unit for HbA1c mmol/mol?

It measures the number of moles of glycated haemoglobin per mole of total haemoglobin, expressed as mmol/mol

Recall another major factor that increases one’s risk of developing microvascular complications of diabetes.

Hypertension

Blood Pressure And Risk Of Microvascular Complications.

Recall three other factors that increases one’s risk of developing microvascular complications of diabetes.

  • Duration of diabetes — longer exposure to hyperglycaemia = greater risk of damage
  • Smoking — endothelial dysfunction
  • Genetic factors — some people develop complications despite reasonable glycaemic control
  • Hyperlipidaemia — endothelial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, deposits → vascular occlusion
  • Hyperglycaemic memory — inadequate glucose control early on can result in higher risk of complications LATER, even if HbA1c improved

Describe the general mechanism of damage that these factors use to cause microvascular complications.

  • The stressor, which is usually hyperglycaemia or hyperlipidaemia, induces endothelial dysfunction within small blood vessels
  • This endothelial dysfunction means that capillaries can become leaky and also not allow the smooth passage of blood that they usually do
  • The capillary leakage is significant because it can lead to the leakage of cytokines which can cause inflammation within peripheral tissues
  • The lack of smooth passage of blood is significant because it can lead to tissue ischaemia, observed in retinopathy, neuropathy and nephropathy

What is AGE-RAGE?

AGE-RAGE refers to the interaction between Advanced Glycation End Products (AGEs) and their receptor, known as the Receptor for Advanced Glycation End Products (RAGE) — Advanced glycation end products (AGEs) are compounds formed

when proteins or lipids react with sugars in the blood

This interaction plays a critical role in the pathophysiology of various chronic diseases, including diabetic complications, atherosclerosis, and inflammation-driven disorders

What is diabetic retinopathy?

Diabetic retinopathy is a complication of diabetes that affects the blood vessels in the retina, the light-sensitive layer at the back of the eye responsible for vision

Blood Supply Of The Eye.

Describe generally how prevalent/dangerous diabetic retinopathy is.

Diabetic retinopathy is the main cause of blindness in people of working age and the main cause of visual loss in people with diabetes

How is diabetic retinopathy detected clinically?

Screening programmes

Describe the importance of screening in detecting diabetic retinopathy.

  • The early stages of retinopathy are all asymptomatic, therefore screening is needed
  • The aim of screening is hence to detect retinopathy early when it can be treated before it causes visual disturbance / loss

Describe how screening is conducted for diabetic patients in the UK.

There is annual retinal screening in the UK for all diabetes patients

Describe the role of the retina.

  • The retina is responsible for capturing the light rays that enter the eye, much like the film's role in photography
  • These light rays are converted to electrical impulses and sent to the brain for processing, via the optic nerve.

Retina.

What is the optic disc?

  • The optic disc, also known as the blind spot, is the region of the retina where the optic nerve fibres exit the eye and form the optic nerve
  • The area lacks photoreceptor cells (rods and cones), which are r esponsible for detecting light and forming visual images
  • As a result, no visual information is detected in this region of the retina.

What is the difference between the macula and the fovea?

The macula is the broader central retinal region responsible for sharp, clear central vision and colour perception, while the fovea is a specialised area within the macula that provides the sharpest detail and is essential for tasks requiring fine focus

Macula And Fovea.

Describe what a normal retina looks like on imaging.

A normal retina should have a recognisable macula, an optic disc and blood vessels which don’t look too big

Physiological Retina.

Recall the three stages of diabetic retinopathy in order of severity.

  • Background retinopathy
  • Pre-proliferative retinopathy
  • Proliferative retinopathy

Recall another eye condition that can be caused by diabetes.

Maculopathy

What is background retinopathy?

Background retinopathy is the most benign/harmless retinopathy secondary to diabetes

Describe the features of background retinopathy.

  • Hard exudates (cheese colour, lipid)
  • Microaneurysms (”dots”)
  • Blot haemorrhages

Background Retinopathy.

Describe the features of pre-proliferative retinopathy.

Generally, the same features as background retinopathy are present

What distinguishes pre-proliferative retinopathy from background retinopathy is the presence of soft exudates which are larger than hard exudates What are exudates in diabetic retinopathy?

  • In diabetic retinopathy, exudates refer to deposits in the retina caused by damage to the small blood vessels (microvasculature) as a result of chronic hyperglycaemia in diabetes
  • They result from the leakage of fluid and lipids from damaged blood vessels

Describe the difference between soft and hard exudates in background and pre-proliferative retinopathy.

  • Hard exudates result from the leakage of lipids and proteins from damaged retinal blood vessels. — background retinopathy
  • Soft exudates, also known as cotton wool spots, are caused by retinal ischaemia (lack of adequate blood flow and oxygen supply to the retina) — pre-proliferative retinopathy

Pre-Proliferative Retinopathy.

What is proliferative retinopathy?

Proliferative retinopathy is the most advanced stage of diabetic retinopathy, characterised by the growth of abnormal new blood vessels (neovascularisation) on the retina or the optic disc

Describe the features of proliferative retinopathy.

The distinctive feature of proliferative retinopathy is visible new vessels on the optic disc or elsewhere in the retina

Describe why neovascularisation occurs in proliferative retinopathy and the problems it causes.

  • Neovascularisation is a compensatory mechanism used by the vasculature of the retina that is usually secondary to ischaemia within the major retinal vessels
  • New blood vessels are formed to try and replace the blood supply, but the problem is these new vessels are really fragile and are highly susceptible to haemorrhage

Proliferative Retinopathy.

What is maculopathy?

  • Diabetic maculopathy refers to damage to the macula (the central part of the retina) caused by diabetes, leading to visual impairment
  • It is a common complication of diabetic retinopathy and primarily affects central vision, which is crucial for activities such as reading and recognising faces

Describe the features of maculopathy.

  • Hard exudates / oedema near the macula — This can compromise clear, sharp vision which is the function of the macula
  • Maculopathy is the same disease as background retinopathy, but it happens to be near the macula

Maculopathy.

Describe how background retinopathy is treated.

Continued surveillance

Describe how pre-proliferative retinopathy is treated.

  • Early panretinal photocoagulation
  • If left alone will progress to new vessel growth

Describe how proliferative retinopathy is treated.

Panretinal photocoagulation

Describe how diabetic maculopathy is treated.

Using anti-VEGF (anti-vascular endothelial growth factor) injections directly into the eye to prevent endothelial inflammation

What is pan-retinal photocoagulation?

  • Pan-retinal photocoagulation (PRP) is a laser treatment used to treat advanced stages of diabetic retinopathy, particularly proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) and sometimes diabetic maculopathy
  • The goal of PRP is to shrink and scar new, abnormal blood vessels (neovascularisation) in the retina, which can lead to serious complications like retinal haemorrhage

What stage of diabetic retinopathy can be observed rather than treated?

Background retinopathy

Pan-Retinal Photocoagulation.

What is diabetic nephropathy?

Diabetic nephropathy is a type of kidney disease that occurs as a complication of diabetes mellitus — It is characterised by damage to the kidney's blood vessels,

which can impair the kidneys' ability to filter waste and excess fluid from the blood Describe two reasons why diabetic nephropathy is important.

  • Associated with progression to end-stage renal failure requiring haemodialysis
  • It is a healthcare burden
  • It is associated with increased risk of cardiovascular events

What is the significance of the urine albumin:creatinine ratio (UACR)?

  • The urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) is an important diagnostic tool used to assess kidney function, specifically to detect early kidney damage and monitor proteinuria (excess protein in the urine), which is a sign of kidney dysfunction.
  • Healthy kidneys usually prevent large amounts of albumin from leaking into the urine

Can microalbuminuria be picked up on a urine dipstick?

  • No.
  • This is why urine albumin:creatinine ratio (UACR) needs to be used

Recall the value of albumin in urine which is classified as microalbuminuria, proteinuria and nephrotic syndrome.

  • Microalbuminuria = ›3 mg/mmol
  • Proteinuria = ACR › 30mg/mmol
  • Nephrotic Syndrome › 3000mg/24hr

Describe how we assess for microalbuminuria.

  • Microalbuminuria is assessed for using a laboratory urine analysis
  • This is because it cannot be detected on a urine dipstick until it has become proteinuria (› 30mg/mmol)

Describe how the urine albumin:creatinine ratio (UACR) can be used clinically in surveillance for diabetic nephropathy.

Every patient with diabetes mellitus should have an annual urine sample to quantify the urine albumin:creatinine ratio - UACR

Why must the urine albumin:creatinine ratio (UACR) be repeated in order to confirm microalbuminuria?

  • False positive results are quite common for UACR — i.e in events of fever, urine infection
  • A positive UACR hence needs to be repeated to confirm microalbuminuria

Describe the mechanism behind diabetic nephropathy.

  • In diabetes the two main problems are usually hyperglycaemia and hypertension
  • These both lead to increased pressure in the glomerular capillaries (glomerular hypertension)
  • This unusually forces protein into urine, leading to proteinuria, a decrease in glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and ultimately renal failure

Describe how the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) works.

  • Aldosterone production is regulated by renin
  • Renin is secreted by juxtaglomerular apparatus (JGA) cells when systolic blood pressure decreases
  • Renin production leads to the production of angiotensin I from angiotensinogen
  • Angiotensin II is then produced from angiotensin I via ACE (angiotensin conversion enzyme)
  • Angiotensin II can then activate the enzymes: hydroxlase 21, hydroxylase 11, hydroxylase 18, side chain cleavage enzyme and 3 hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase in the adrenal gland which leads to aldosterone production from the zona glomerulosa of the adrenal cortex
  • Angiotensin II also causes vasoconstriction

What effect do ACE inhibitors (ACEi) and angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) have?

ACE inhibitors (ACEi) and angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) are both anti-hypertensive drugs that have their effects by inhibiting angiotensin II, albeit via different mechanisms

Describe how ACE inhibitors (ACEi) lead to the effects they do.

  • ACE inhibitors (ACEi) are antihypertensives which block ACE
  • This prevents the conversion of angiotensin I → angiotensin II which means the production of aldosterone and the other vasoconstrictive effects of angiotensin are inhibited

Describe how angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) lead to the effects they do.

  • Angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) are antihypertensives which block angiotensin (II) receptors
  • This means angiotensin II cannot have the usual effects it has which also means the production of aldosterone and the other vasoconstrictive effects of angiotensin are inhibited

Recall the suffix usually given to an ACE inhibitor (ACEi).

-pril — i.e Ramipril

Recall the suffix usually given to an angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB).

-sartan — i.e irbesartan

Why is it that all diabetes patients with microalbuminuria/proteinuria should have an ACEi/ARB even if normotensive?

  • This is because ACEi and ARB therapy not only reduce blood pressure, but they are very helpful in inhibiting the progression of diabetic nephropathy (→ proteinuria) via their action in the RAS Is there a benefit to ACEi and ARB combination therapy?
  • There is no benefit to having both ACEi/ARBin in combination
  • NOTE: However, there is benefit to having a higher dose of just ARB or ACEi ACE INHIBITORS AND ANGIOTENSIN RECEPTOR BLOCKERS (ARBs).

Recall a condition that microalbuminuria increases the risk of.

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) — This is because the existence of microalbuminuria is shows that there is endothelial dysfunction and systemic inflammation - all of which contribute to atherosclerosis and cardiovascular events

Recall three facets involved in the management of diabetic nephropathy.

  • Aim for optimal glycaemic control (HbA1c ‹53 mmol/mol)
  • ACEi/ARB even if normotensive as soon as patient has microalbuminuria
  • Reduce BP (aim ‹130/80 mmHg) usually through ACEi or A2RB
  • Stop smoking
  • Start an SGLT-2 inhibitor (reduces reabsorption of glucose) if patient has T2DM — it reduces risk of progression of chronic kidney disease

What is the earliest feature of diabetic nephropathy?

Microalbuminuria

What is diabetic neuropathy?

  • Diabetic neuropathy is a type of nerve damage that occurs as a complication of diabetes mellitus — Diabetes mellitus is the most common cause of neuropathy and hence lower limb amputation
  • High blood sugar (glucose) levels over time damage the nerves and their blood supply, leading to various forms of neuropathy

What are vasa nervorum?

Vasa nervorum are the small blood vessels that supply the nerves

Vasa Nervorum.

Describe how the vasa nervorum are implicated in diabetic neuropathy.

Diabetic neuropathy usually results when the vasa nervorum get blocked

Recall three risk factors of diabetic neuropathy.

  • Age
  • Duration of diabetes
  • Poor glycaemic control
  • Height — the longer nerves in lower limbs of tall people are more susceptible to damage
  • Smoking
  • Presence of diabetic retinopathy — because it indicates that there is damage to small vessels elsewhere in the body, and vasa nervorum may be next

Where in the body is diabetic neuropathy most common and why?

  • Feet — It is known as a “glove and stocking distribution” (hands are commonly affected too)
  • This is because the longest nerves in the body supply the feet and the longest axons (those to the feet) have the highest metabolic stress and are furthest from the neuronal cell body

Glove And Stocking Distribution.

Describe the danger of diabetic neuropathy to patients.

  • The danger is that patients with diabetic neuropathy may not sense an injury to the foot — i.e stepping on a nail
  • This risks further aggravation of this damage, as the individual has no insight into what has happened

What is a skin ulcer?

  • The formation of a break on the skin or on the surface of an organ
  • An ulcer forms when the surface cells die and are cast off — Skin ulcers can result in complete loss of the epidermis and often portions of the dermis and even subcutaneous fat

What is diabetic foot ulceration?

Diabetic foot ulceration refers to an open wound or sore that develops on the foot of a person with diabetes, often due to a combination of factors like nerve damage, poor blood supply, and increased susceptibility to infections

Describe how diabetic neuropathy is monitored in people with diabetes.

Annual foot check

Recall three components of an annual foot check.

  • Look for foot deformity, ulceration
  • Assess sensation — using monofilament, ankle jerks (show physiological sensory function)
  • Assess foot pulses (dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial) — this is to assess chances of a wound healing. if no blood supply, wound is likely to develop into an ulcer

What is a monofilament?

A monofilament is a thin, single-strand fiber that is commonly used in medical settings, especially for testing sensory nerve function in patients with conditions such as diabetes.

Monofilament.

Recall two risk factors for diabetic foot ulceration.

  • Reduced sensation to feet — peripheral neuropathy
  • Poor vascular supply to feet — peripheral vascular disease as it prevents healing of wounds, leading to ulcer formation
