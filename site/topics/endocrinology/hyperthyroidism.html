The Thyroid Gland.

In which cells is thyroid hormone (T4) produced?

  • Thyroid follicular cells
  • NOTE: This is different to parafollicular cells, associated with the parathyroid gland

Describe how thyroid hormone is produced (from TSH/TSH-R to release into blood).

  • TSH binds onto its receptor TSH-R on the thyroid follicular cells — This TSH/TSH-R interaction also induces release of thyroglobulin (TG) from the nucleus and this moves into the colloid — Thyroid peroxidase (TPO) is also produced
  • This opens Na+ and I- ion channels, and I- flows from the blood into the follicular cell — I- derived from the diet
  • Iodine (I-) then moves into the colloid and undergoes oxidation to become I and its negative charge is removed
  • Thyroglobulin in the colloid undergoes iodination to become either MIT (monoiodotyrosine) or DIT (diiodotyrosine). This is catalysed by TPO and H2O2 — These can be used to form T3 and T4 in the coupling reaction
  • The T3 and T4 are still bound to thyroglobulin and so they move back into the follicular cell, where the lysosome performs proteolysis and cleaves the hormones from thyroglobulin
  • The hormones can now be released into the blood

T3 And T4 Synthesis.

Describe the control of thyroid activity via the HPT axis.

  • The hypothalamus produces thyroid releasing hormone (TRH)
  • The pituitary produces thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH)
  • The thyroid produces thyroxine (T3/T4)

Control Of Thyroid Activity.

What level of TSH will you find in a patient with primary hypothyroidism, where the thyroid gland has been destroyed by the immune system?

  • High TSH
  • The body is trying to compensate and correct the low levels of thyroxine seen in hypothyroidism

Describe how TSH is used in monitoring the effectiveness of T4 replacement therapy.

  • In patients with hypothyroidism, T4 is low and TSH rises to compensate for this
  • In T4 replacement therapy, the aim is to get T4 levels back up to normal and TSH levels to fall
  • This shows the HPT axis is responding to the treatment, and it is effective

What is Grave’s disease?

Grave’s disease is an autoimmune disease which involves antibodies binding to and stimulating the TSH receptor in the thyroid

Describe what happens in Grave’s disease.

  • In Graves' disease, the immune system mistakenly produces antibodies called thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulins (TSIs)
  • These antibodies target and stimulate the thyroid-stimulating hormone receptor (TSH-R) on thyroid follicular cells
  • This leads to the thyroid gland producing excessive amounts of thyroid hormones -triiodothyronine (T3) and thyroxine (T4) Is Grave’s disease a form of hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism?
  • Hyperthyroidism

Recall three physical manifestations of Grave’s disease.

  • Smooth goitre
  • Exophthalmos
  • Pretibial myxoedema

Recall five clinical signs of Grave’s disease.

  • Smooth goitre
  • Exophthalmos
  • Pretibial myxoedema
  • Perspiration
  • Facial flushing
  • Muscle wasting
  • Shortness of breath
  • Warm, velvety skin
  • Palpitation, Tachycardia
  • Breast enlargement, gynaecomastia in males
  • Loss of weight
  • Oligomenorrhoea/Amenorrhoea
  • Localised myxoedema
  • Increased appetite
  • Diarrhoea
  • Tremor
  • Clubbing of fingers

What is exophthalmos?

Exophthalmos is the protrusion of one or both eyes anteriorly out of the orbit due to an increase in orbital contents within the rigid bony orbit

Describe how Grave’s disease can lead to exophthalmos.

The antibodies produced in Grave’s disease against the thyroid (thyroid stimulating immunoglobulins, TSIs) can bind to muscles behind the eye and cause exophthalmos

What is pretibial myxoedema?

Pretibial myxoedema is a skin condition associated with Graves' disease, marked by thickening and swelling of the skin, usually over the shins (pretibial area)

Describe how Grave’s disease can lead to pretibial myxoedema.

Pretibial myxoedema in Graves' disease is caused by an autoimmune process that stimulates fibroblasts in the skin to produce glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), leading to water retention and characteristic skin changes. • The autoimmune response can also lead to inflammation which can cause redness, warmth, and swelling of the affected skin TIBIA.

Pretibial Myxoedema.

Describe the difference between myxoedema and pretibial myxoedema.

Myxoedema generally refers to the severe skin and tissue changes that occur due to long-standing hypothyroidism (an underactive thyroid) — i.e “myxoedema coma”

What is a myxoedema coma?

Pretibial myxoedema is a localised skin condition often associated with Graves' disease (an autoimmune hyperthyroidism) — Unlike general myxoedema, it’s limited primarily to the shins (pretibial area)

Recall a common clinical feature of Grave’s disease.

Smooth goitre

Goitre.

What is the first line of investigation performed to diagnose Graves’ disease?

  • TSH-receptor antibody (TRAb) test (antibody against the TSH receptor)
  • Levels of this antibody are measured in Grave’s disease, and it is positive

Describe a physical examination that can be performed to diagnose Grave’s disease.

Diffuse enlargement and engorgement of thyroid gland — Palpation can be done to identify this

What scanning technique is typically used to visualise the thyroid gland?

Radioactive iodine uptake on scintigram

Describe how scintigraphy works to help us identify thyroid abnormalities.

  • In thyroid scintigraphy, radioactive iodine isotopes are administered to the patient. — These isotopes emit gamma rays, which can be detected by a gamma camera to create images.
  • The thyroid gland requires iodine to produce thyroid hormones, thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3).
  • This means that if there is high thyroid activity, more iodine will be absorbed and a more intense image is produced, and vice-versa for low thyroid activity
  • NOTE: Since thyroid cells are the only cells in the body that can absorb iodine, this technique is fairly specific to the thyroid

Describe what should be seen in a thyroid gland affected by Grave’s disease compared to one in a healthy individual on a scintigram.

Since there is high thyroid activity in Grave’s disease, the scintigram would show us a very intense image all over the thyroid (symmetrical)

Scintigraphy In Thyroid Pathologies.

What is toxic nodular thyroid disease?

  • This is a form of hyperthyroidism which involves the hyperactivity of one or more benign thyroid nodules — There may be one or multiple nodules and they produce thyroxine Is toxic nodular thyroid disease an autoimmune disease?
  • No.

Recall another form of toxic nodular thyroid disease.

Multinodular goitre — This is when there is more than one benign, overactive adenoma in the thyroid

Describe the difference in the clinical features of Grave’s disease and toxic nodular thyroid disease.

Since toxic nodular thyroid disease is not autoimmune or antibody- mediated, there is no: — Pretibial myxoedema — Exophthalmos

Describe what should be seen in a thyroid gland affected by toxic nodular thyroid disease compared to one in a healthy individual on a scintigram.

On thyroid scintigraphy, due to the fact only the nodules are hyperactive in thyroxine production, these “hot nodules” would be the only areas that appear darker on the scan

Thyroid Scintigraphy - Toxic Nodular Thyroid Disease.

Describe and explain the effects of thyroxine on the sympathetic nervous system.

  • Thyroxine can be thought of as a metabolic accelerator
  • It is known to sensitise beta-adrenoceptors to ambient levels of adrenaline and noradrenaline — i.e the beta-adenergic receptors become much more sensitive

What is a beta-adrenoceptor?

Beta-adrenoceptors (beta-adrenergic receptors) are G-protein coupled receptors that respond to the neurotransmitters adrenaline and noradrenaline

Recall three ways that the effects of thyroxine on the sympathetic nervous system can affect patients.

  • Tachycardia
  • Palpitations
  • Tremor in hands
  • Lid lag

What is lid lag?

Lid lag refers to a condition where the upper eyelid fails to follow the downward movement of the eyeball smoothly

When the patient looks down, the upper eyelid remains higher than normal, exposing more of the sclera (the white part of the eye) above the iris

This means that when the patient looks down, there is visible white space between the iris and the eyelid which should not be present physiologically

Lid Lag.

Recall five clinical features of hyperthyroidism.

  • Weight loss despite increased appetite
  • Breathlessness
  • Palpitations, tachycardia
  • Sweating
  • Heat intolerance
  • Diarrhoea
  • Lid lag and other sympathetic features GRAVES’ DISEASE.

What is thyroid storm?

Thyroid storm is severe, untreated hyperthyroidism

Describe the clinical urgency of thyroid storm.

It is a medical emergency and has a 50% mortality rate if left untreated

How is thyroid storm confirmed?

Blood results confirm hyperthyroidism (very high T3/T4)

Describe three clinical features of thyroid storm.

  • Hyperpyrexia (›41ºC)
  • Accelerated tachycardia / arrhythmia
  • Cardiac failure
  • Delirium / frank psychosis
  • Hepatocellular dysfunction; jaundice

What is hyperprexia?

Hyperpyrexia is the term for exceptionally high fever (greater than 41ºC)

Recall the three main treatment options for hyperthyroidism.

  • Drugs
  • Surgery
  • Radioiodine

Recall the four main non-surgical treatments used in the treatment of hyperthyroidism.

  • The thionamides
  • Potassium iodide
  • Radioiodine
  • Beta-blockers

Which of these four drug classes act differently to the rest, and what is the difference?

  • Beta-blockers
  • The thionamides, potassium iodide and radioiodine work to reduce the production of thyroid hormone
  • Beta-blockers instead work to alleviate the symptoms experienced by excess thyroid hormone — This is obviously due to the fact thyroxine causes hypersensitisation of beta-receptors, so beta-blockers help to reduce the sympathetic activation

Which hyperthyroid conditions are the thionamides used to treat?

  • Graves’ disease
  • Toxic thyroid nodule/toxic multinodular goitre

Describe the mechanism of action through which thionamides function.

  • Thionamides work via the inhibition of thyroid peroxidase (TPO) and H2O2 and hence they inhibit T3/T4 synthesis and secretion
  • NOTE: Thyroid peroxidase (TPO) is vital for the conversion of thyroglobulin → MIT (monoiodotyrosine) or DIT (diiodotyrosine)

Recap: Thyroid Hormone Production.

Describe how long thionamides may take to work.

  • Biochemical effect - hours
  • Clinical effect – weeks
  • This means the drugs act very quickly, but it may take longer before the patient reports any effects

Describe how we may treat patients who are on thionamides, during this period of latency between consumption and clinical effect.

Propanolol (beta-blocker) to control sympathetic symptoms — i.e tremor, tachycardia

What is propranolol?

  • Propranolol is a non-selective beta-blocker that is used to reduce the efficacy of the sympathetic nervous system — The fact it is non-selective means it will not just work locally, but will work systemically to address most sympathetic symptoms
  • They can be given alongside thionamides to help manage symptoms before thionamides have their effect

Describe two unwanted actions of thionamides.

  • Agranulocytosis (usually reduction in neutrophils) — This is rare and reversible on withdrawal of drug
  • Rashes (relatively common)

Recall two thionamides.

  • Carbimazole (CBZ)
  • Propylthiouracil (PTU)

How long should anti-thyroid drugs be used for?

  • Usually in Grave’s disease, we aim to stop anti-thyroid drug treatment after 18 months — This is because after this time period, the antibodies tend to settle down
  • However with nodular thyroid disease, we must review the patient periodically including thyroid function tests that check for remission/relapse

What is a beta-blocker used for?

  • Beta-blockers are used to treat symptoms caused by hyper- sympathetic action such as tremor, tachycardia, sweating etc.
  • They work by blocking beta-adrenoceptors which are used to facilitate the actions of adrenaline and noradrenaline
  • Beta-blockers effectively reduce the efficacy of these hormones adrenaline and noradrenaline

What is the role of beta-blockers in thyrotoxicosis?

  • Non-selective (beta-1 & beta-2) beta-blockers such as propranolol achieve the desired anti-thyroid effects in the interim
  • They allow for reduced tremor, slower heart rate and less anxiety

Describe how iodide is administered for patients with hyperthyroidism.

Doses given are usually at least 30 times the average daily requirement

When is iodide usually used to treat hyperthyroidism?

  • In cases of thyroid storm
  • To prepare a hyperthyroid patient for surgery and inhibit T3/T4 production
  • NOTE: NOT used in patients opting for medical treatment of Graves’

What is the Wolff-Chaikoff effect?

  • The Wolff-Chaikoff effect is a temporary protective response of the thyroid gland to high levels of iodine
  • It occurs when the thyroid gland becomes saturated with iodine,

which blocks the production of thyroid hormones Describe the mechanism of action of potassium iodide.

  • Potassium iodide works in two ways: — Inhibits iodination of thyroglobulin — Inhibits H2O2 generation + thyroperoxidase (TPO)
  • It effectively uses the Wolff-Chaikoff effect to induce shutdown of thyroid activity

Describe the impact that potassium iodide (KI) can have on users.

  • Hyperthyroid symptoms reduce within 1-2 days — This is due to the Wolff-Chaikoff effect
  • The vascularity and size of the thyroid gland reduce within 10-14 days

What is (propyl)thiouracil?

Propylthiouracil (PTU) is a part of the thionamide class of medications commonly used as anti-thyroid medications

Recall three problems that may arise with thyroid surgery.

  • Risk of voice change — This is due to the proximity of the recurrent laryngeal nerve to the thyroid gland
  • Risk of losing parathyroid gland
  • Scars
  • Anaesthetics

What is radioiodine?

Radioiodine is a radioactive form of iodine, usually Iodine-131 (I-131)

Describe what radioiodine is used for.

Radioiodine is used to treat hyperthyroidism

Describe how radioiodine works.

  • It works by using a radioactive isotope of iodine, typically Iodine-131 (I-131), which selectively targets thyroid cells
  • I-131 emits beta-radiation (which has a tissue-penetrating effect) and gamma radiation (used in imaging)
