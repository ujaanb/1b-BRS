Overview

What are the five types of endocrine cells present in the anterior pituitary?

  • Somatotrophs
  • Lactotrophs
  • Corticotrophs
  • Thyrotrophs
  • Gonadotrophs

Recall the six hormones produced by the anterior pituitary.

  • Growth hormone
  • Adrenocorticotrophic hormone (ACTH)
  • Prolactin
  • Thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH)
  • Follicle stimulating hormone (FSH)
  • Luteinising hormone (LH)

Describe the process of how the anterior pituitary is stimulated via the hypothalamo-pituitary portal system.

  • Axon terminals of the hypothalamic neurosecretory cells release regulatory factors (inhibitory or stimulatory) into the median eminence via parvocellular neurons
  • The factors travel to the relevant cells in the anterior pituitary via the portal circulation
  • Pituitary gland then secretes relevant hormone into the blood

Describe the difference between primary and secondary failure of the gonads, adrenal cortex and thyroid glands.

  • Primary disease is where the gland (gonad, adrenal cortex or thyroid) itself fails
  • Secondary disease is where there is inadequate signalling from the hypothalamus or anterior pituitary

Describe the difference between primary and secondary hypothyroidism and relevant hormone levels in each.

  • Primary hypothyroidism refers to conditions such as the (autoimmune) destruction of the thyroid gland — T3 & T4 fall, TSH increases (to compensate for low T3 and T4 levels) — NOTE: We don’t measure TRH but that would also be high
  • Secondary hypothyroidism may involve a pituitary tumour damaging thyrotrophs — Patients will be unable to make TSH, TSH falls and subsequently T3 & T4 fall

Describe the difference between primary and secondary hypoadrenalism and relevant hormone levels in each.

  • Primary hypoadrenalism could involve destruction of the adrenal cortex (eg autoimmune) — Cortisol falls, ACTH increases to counteract this fall — NOTE: We don’t measure CRH but that would also be high
  • Secondary hypoadrenalism could involve destruction of corticotrophs in the pituitary, leading to less ACTH and hence less cortisol

Describe the difference between primary and secondary hypogonadism and relevant hormone levels in each.

  • Primary hypogonadism could involve destruction of testes (eg mumps) or ovaries (eg chemotherapy) — Testosterone (men) or oestrogen (women) fall, LH & FSH increase — We don’t measure GnRH but that would also be high — NOTE: About 1 in 3 unvaccinated males who get mumps after puberty gets a swollen testicle (orchitis)
  • Secondary hypogonadism could involve pituitary tumour damaging gonadotrophs. — This means the pituitary gland can’t make LH/FSH. — LH/FSH then fall and in turn testosterone/oestrogen fall

Recall three causes of hypopituitarism.

  • Congenital (rare) Acquired
  • Infection — i.e meningitis
  • Tumours — i.e adenomas, metastases, cysts
  • Radiation
  • Traumatic brain injury
  • Pituitary surgery
  • Inflammatory — hypophysitis
  • Pituitary apoplexy - haemorrhage
  • Peri-partum infarction (Sheehan’s syndrome)

Describe how congenital hypopituitarism presents in individuals and its cause.

  • It leads to them having a deficiency of growth hormone and at least one other anterior pituitary hormone
  • They have a short stature and a hypoplastic (underdeveloped) pituitary gland

What is the cause of congenital hypopituitarism?

Congenital hypopituitarism is usually due to mutations of transcription factor genes needed for normal anterior pituitary development

What is the name given to total loss of anterior and posterior pituitary function?

Panhypopituitarism

What does the term hypopituitarism typically refer to?

The term hypopituitarism often describes anterior pituitary dysfunction, but certain processes - especially inflammation (hypophysitis) or surgery - may cause posterior pituitary dysfunction too

Describe how radiotherapy can lead to hypopituitarism.

  • The pituitary and the hypothalamus are both sensitive to radiation
  • The radiotherapy can be done directly to the pituitary or indirectly to a CNS tumour nearby

Which cell types within the pituitary are most sensitive to radiation?

GH and gonadotrophins are the most sensitive

Describe the follow-up undertaken for an individual that has had radiotherapy to the pituitary region.

The risk of hypopituitarism after radiotherapy persists up to 10 years

after radiotherapy, so annual assessment is required Describe five symptoms of hypopituitarism and which hormone deficiency causes them.

  • Reduced libido - FSH/LH
  • Secondary amenorrhea - FSH/LH
  • Erectile dysfunction - FSH/LH
  • Reduced pubic hair - FSH/LH
  • Fatigue - ACTH
  • Fatigue - TSH
  • Reduced quality of life - GH
  • Short stature (only in children) - GH
  • Inability to breastfeed - Prolactin

What is Sheehan’s syndrome?

Sheehan’s syndrome is a condition characterised by post-partum hypopituitarism secondary to hypotension — post partum haemorrhage - PPH

Where is Sheehan’s syndrome more commonly found?

Sheehan’s is more common in developing countries

What causes Sheehan’s syndrome?

  • Sheehan’s syndrome is a condition caused by the excess loss of blood from a woman after a pregnancy (post-partum haemorrrhage, PPH)
  • This loss of blood can be very severe in cases leading to pituitary infarction and hence dysfunction/hypopituitarism

Describe a change that occurs to the pituitary gland during pregnancy.

The anterior pituitary enlarges in pregnancy due to lactotroph hyperplasia

Describe three symptoms of Sheehan’s syndrome and which hormone deficiency causes them.

  • Lethargy/anorexia/weight loss - GH/ACTH/TSH
  • Inability to breastfeed - Prolactin
  • Amenorrhea following pregnancy - FSH/LH Is the posterior pituitary usually affected by Sheehan’s syndrome?
  • No.

What is the best radiological way to visualise the pituitary gland?

  • MRI scans
  • CT scans do not give us enough detail to visualise the pituitary

What is pituitary apoplexy?

  • Pituitary apoplexy is bleeding (haemorrhage) into or impaired blood supply of the pituitary gland — Here, the phrase bleeding into the pituitary means that a blood vessel inside the pituitary gland (or a tumour in it) has burst, and now blood is leaking into the tissue of the pituitary itself
  • NOTE: Apoplexy is bleeding into an organ or loss of blood flow to an organ

In which patients is pituitary apoplexy most common?

Pituitary apoplexy is often seen within patients that have an existing pituitary tumour (usually a non-functioning adenoma) — Tumours often have fragile blood supply

What is the significance of a patient presenting with pituitary apoplexy?

It typically has a dramatic presentation within these patients and may be the first presentation of a pituitary adenoma

Recall a cause of pituitary apoplexy.

Pituitary apoplexies can be precipitated by anti-coagulants (blood thinners)

Recall two symptoms of pituitary apoplexy.

  • Severe sudden onset headache
  • Visual field defects (via compression of the optic chiasm) — i.e bitemporal hemianopia
  • Cavernous sinus involvement may lead to diplopia (IV, VI), ptosis (III) — NOTE: Diplopia = double-vision

Ptosis.

Explain why we must take special care when trying to diagnose hypopituitarism.

  • Because a lot of the hormones secreted by the pituitary gland have diurnal variation. This means they may show very high/low levels at different times of the day, depending on when you measure them
  • It is important to take this into account when interpreting blood test results

Describe how cortisol, T4, FSH/LH and GH/ACTH levels vary through the day.

  • Cortisol – higher in the morning
  • T4 – circulating half-life of ~6 days
  • FSH/LH – cyclical in women
  • GH/ACTH - pulsatile

Describe how a dynamic pituitary function test is used in the diagnosis of hypopituitarism.

  • Dynamic pituitary function tests are designed to evaluate how well the pituitary responds to specific hormonal stimuli and can help uncover deficiencies or excesses in hormone production.
  • For example, GnRH stimulation can be used to assess the pituitary’s capacity to produce LH/FSH. TRH for TSH etc.

Describe what is done in a dynamic pituitary function test ACTH and GH release.

  • ACTH & GH can be referred to as ‘stress’ hormones
  • Insulin-induced hypoglycaemia (‹2.2mM) stimulates GH release and ACTH release (cortisol measured)

Describe the purpose of an MRI scan being used diagnostically for hypopituitarism.

  • A pituitary MRI may reveal specific pituitary pathology — i.e haemorrhage (apoplexy), adenoma
  • Empty sella – thin rim of pituitary tissue

Pituitary Apoplexy/Adenoma.

What is empty sella syndrome?

  • Empty sella syndrome is a condition in which the pituitary gland shrinks or becomes flattened
  • However, the sella turcica isn't actually empty. It's often filled with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)

Hypopituitarism - Empty Sella.

Which hormone do we not treat a lack of in hypopituitarism?

  • Prolactin
  • This is because besides inability to breastfeed, prolactin deficiency does not result in any significant physiological deficits

How is GH deficiency treated?

GH deficiency is treated with daily injections which replace the hormone in sufferers

How is GH deficiency diagnosed?

  • Confirm GH deficiency on dynamic pituitary function test
  • Induce hypoglycaemia (‹2.2mM) via insulin, this should lead to GH and ACTH release

Recall two ways we can measure the effectiveness GH injections.

Two measures are used to monitor treatment efficacy of GH deficiency: — QoL questionnaire - a lack of GH can lead to poor quality of life. Injections should improve QoL — Plasma IGF-1 levels (should be increased) — This is because GH stimulates the liver’s production of IGF-1

What are the dangers of growth hormone?

  • The most serious dangers include an increased risk of diabetes, cardiovascular issues, abnormal tissue growth (acromegaly-like changes), and possibly cancer
  • Careful medical supervision and regular monitoring are essential to mitigate these risks

How is TSH deficiency treated?

Levothyroxine (T4 replacement therapy) is used to treat this deficiency

Describe how the dose for T4 replacement therapy is determined when treating TSH deficiency.

  • TSH will be low as we are not replacing it, so you can’t use this to adjust dose as you do in primary hypothyroidism
  • We aim for a fT4 (free T4) above the middle of the reference range

How is ACTH deficiency treated?

  • The treatment of ACTH deficiency is centred around replacing cortisol and not ACTH
  • Two main options are used in the UK: — Prednisolone — Hydrocortisone

How often is prednisolone taken?

Once daily (AM)

How often is hydrocortisone taken?

Three times per day

Which type of patients need “sick day rules”?

Patients with ACTH deficiency and take replacement steroids
