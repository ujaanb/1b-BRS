Overview

What is infertility?

‘A disease of the reproductive system defined by the failure to achieve a clinical pregnancy after ≥12 months of regular unprotected sexual intercourse.’

How frequent is regular intercourse?

Regular intercourse is every 2-3 days

Describe the difference between primary and secondary infertility.

  • Primary infertility is when the couple has not had a live birth previously
  • Secondary infertility is when the couple has had a live birth more than 12 months previously Do miscarriages count as a clinical pregnancy?
  • No. Approximately how many couples are affected by infertility?
  • 1 in 7 couples (14%)

How many of these will go on to conceive in the next 12 months?

Half of these — i.e 7% of all couples

Recall a factor associated with a couple’s likelihood of seeking help for infertility.

  • 55% of couples will seek help (UK data)
  • Positive association with socioeconomic status

Recall the four causes of infertility in couples.

  • Male factor
  • Female factor
  • Combined male and female factor
  • Unknown factor

What is the most common cause of infertility in a couple?

  • Male factor (30%)
  • Female factor (30%)
  • Combined male and female factor (30%)
  • Unknown factor (10%)

Describe three ways that infertility affects couples.

  • Psychological distress
  • Problems with the wider family
  • Impact on couple’s wellbeing
  • Failing treatments causing problems

Recall three ways that infertility affects society.

  • Less births
  • Less tax income
  • Costs to investigate problems
  • Treatment costs

Recall the three types of male causes of infertility.

  • Pre-Testicular
  • Testicular
  • Post-Testicular

Recall a pre-testicular cause of infertility.

Congenital/Acquired Endocrinopathies — i.e Klinefelters’ syndrome

What is Klinefelter’s syndrome?

  • Klinefelter’s syndrome is a syndrome where males have one extra X chromosome — their genotype often ends up as 47XXY
  • NOTE: Other variants can include additional X chromosomes, such as 48,XXXY or 49,XXXXY, which may cause more severe symptoms.

Describe what happens to sufferers of Klinefelter’s syndrome.*

  • HPG axis issues
  • Low testosterone
  • Prolactin issues
  • The overall functioning of the testes is reduced

Recall five testicular causes of infertility.

  • Congenital
  • Cryptorchidism
  • Infection — STDs
  • Immunological — i.e antisperm antibodies
  • Vascular — varicocoele
  • Trauma/Surgery
  • Toxins — chemotherapy, drugs, smoking

What is a varicocoele?

  • A varicocoele is an enlargement of the veins that transport oxygen- depleted blood away from the testicle
  • It can cause testicular discomfort, deformation of sperm and infertility

What is cryptorchidism?

Cryptorchidism is the most prevalent congenital abnormality involving male genitalia, is characterised by the absence of at least 1 testicle from the scrotum. — Normally the testis descends through the inguinal canal to its normal anatomical position — In cryptorchidism, the testis does not descend completely, and it remains in the inguinal canal

Recall three post-testicular causes of infertility.

  • Congenital — i.e absence of vas deferens in cystic fibrosis
  • Obstructive Azoospermia — blockage/obstruction of sperm release
  • Erectile dysfunction — retrograde ejaculation — mechanical impairment — psychological
  • Iatrogenic — i.e vasectomy (cutting + sealing of vas deferens)

What is retrograde ejaculation?

Retrograde ejaculation is a condition where semen enters the bladder instead of exiting the body through the urethra

Retrograde Ejaculation.

What does the word “iatrogenic” mean?

An illness caused by medical examination or treatment

What test can be used to assess whether someone has ovulated?

Day 21 progesterone test

Describe why the day 21 progesterone test is used to assess for ovulation.

  • A day 21 progesterone test measures the level of progesterone in the blood on day 21 of the menstrual cycle
  • Progesterone levels increase after ovulation on day 14
  • This is because progesterone is produced by the corpus luteum,

which is the remnant of a follicle after release of the egg from a mature follicle (ovulation)

  • Hence, if ovulation has occurred, progesterone levels should usually peak at day 21 via release from this corpus luteum
  • NOTE: Alternatively, we can also use an ultrasound scan (USS) to check for the corpus luteum

Female Reproductive System.

Describe three roles of the ovaries in pregnancy.

Ovulation ◦ The ovaries are responsible for the release of a mature egg (oocyte) during ovulation, typically around day 14 of the menstrual cycle • Corpus luteum function ◦ Producing progesterone and oestrogen to support early pregnancy by maintaining the uterine lining and preventing uterine contractions • Hormonal support ◦ Sustaining the pregnancy until the placenta can take over hormone production in the second trimester • In summary, the ovaries are crucial in initiating and maintaining early pregnancy through the production of progesterone and oestrogen, ensuring a supportive environment for the embryo until the placenta

can take over this role Recall two ovarian causes of infertility.

  • Anovulation
  • Corpus luteum insufficiency

Describe the role of the fallopian tubes in pregnancy.

The fallopian tubes (also called uterine tubes or oviducts ) play a crucial role in pregnancy by facilitating the process of fertilisation and transporting the fertilised egg (zygote) to the uterus

Recall two tubal causes of infertility.

Tubopathy of: — Infection — Endometriosis — tissue similar to the lining of the uterus grows outside the uterus — Trauma

Describe the role of the uterus in pregnancy.

Implantation of the fertilised egg

Recall two uterine causes of infertility.

Unfavourable endometrium due to: — Congenital malformation — Infection/Inflammation/Scarring — Fibroids — non-cancerous growths that develop in or around the womb

Describe an adaptive role of the cervix in fertility.

  • The cervix secretes a thick mucus plug that seals the uterine opening, preventing bacteria from entering and harming the developing baby
  • During ovulation, the mucus becomes thin and watery, creating a favourable environment for sperm motility

Describe three roles of the cervix in fertility.

Barrier function ◦ The cervix secretes a thick mucus plug that seals the uterine opening, preventing bacteria from entering and harming the developing baby • Holding the pregnancy ◦ It remains closed and firm throughout most of the pregnancy to ensure that the baby stays safely in the uterus • Preparation for labour ◦ The cervix softens, thins, and dilates in preparation for childbirth • Facilitating Delivery ◦ During labour, the cervix dilates fully to allow the baby to pass

through the birth canal.

Describe the role of the pelvis in pregnancy.

  • The pelvis provides both a structural and functional foundation throughout pregnancy and childbirth
  • It acts as a stable base for the reproductive organs, allowing them to function optimally

Pelvis.

What is endometriosis?

  • Endometriosis is a disease in which tissue similar to the lining of the uterus grows outside the uterus
  • It affects ~5% of women and the abnormal tissue responds to oestrogen

Recall three symptoms of endometriosis.

  • Increased menstrual pain — due to oestrogen response
  • Menstrual irregularities
  • Deep dysparaeunia
  • Infertility

What is dysparaeunia?

It is lasting or recurrent genital pain that occurs just before, during or after sex

What is fibroids?

  • Fibroids is a benign tumour of the myometrium that occurs in 1-20% of pre-menopausal women — It can block the uterus, causing problems such as infertility, painful or irregular periods, ectopic pregnancy, and recurrent miscarriages
  • Like endometriosis, the growths respond to oestrogen

Describe the prevalence of fibroids.

Affects 1-20% of pre-menopausal women, and risk increases with age

Recall three symptoms of fibroids. Usually asymptomatic

  • Increased menstrual pain
  • Menstrual irregularities
  • Deep dysparaeunia
  • Infertility

Layers Of The Uterine Wall.

Describe the order of hierarchy in the reproductive hypothalamo-pituitary- gonadal (HPG) axis.

  • Kisspeptin
  • GnRH - hypothalamus
  • FSH/LH - anterior pituitary
  • Oestrogen/Testosterone - ovaries/testes

Describe how negative feedback is involved in the reproductive hypothalamo-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis.

Oestrogen and progesterone act on the hypothalamus to inhibit further release of kisspeptin

Hypothalamo-Pituitary-Gonadal (Hpg) Axis.

Which sex hormone performs negative feedback predominantly in men and women?

Oestrogen

Why is oestrogen the predominant hormone in the negative feedback mechanism?

  • Kisspeptin neurones do not have testosterone receptors
  • Also, testosterone is converted to oestrogen in men anyways via aromatisation

What is the effect of kisspeptin?

Kisspeptin is used to initiate the release of GnRH from the hypothalamus

Describe the effect of prolactin on the reproductive hypothalamo-pituitary- gonadal (HPG) axis.

  • Prolactin inhibits kisspeptin neurons
  • Hence, the entire HPG axis is inhibited by the actions of prolactin

Recall three hypothalamic causes of male infertility.

  • Congenital hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism
  • Acquired hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism
  • Hyperprolactinaemia
  • NOTE: The term hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism refers to insufficient GnRH release

Recall a cause of congenital hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism.

  • Anosmic hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism (Kallmann syndrome)
  • Normosmic hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism

Recall a cause of acquired hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism.

  • Low BMI
  • Excessive exercise
  • Stress
  • These are all features of Anorexia nervosa

Recall a pituitary cause of male infertility.

Hypopituitarism

Recall two causes of hypopituitarism.

  • Trauma
  • Surgery
  • Apoplexy
  • Infiltration
  • Radiation

Recall two gonadal causes of male infertility.

  • Congenital primary hypogonadism
  • Acquired primary hypogonadism

Recall a cause of congenital primary hypogonadism.

  • Klinefelter’s syndrome (47 XXY)
  • Cryptorchidism

Recall a cause of acquired primary hypogonadism.

  • Trauma
  • Chemotherapy
  • Radiation

Describe the difference between hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism and primary hypogonadism in men.

Although both conditions can be a major cause for male infertility, hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism refers to a problem with gonadotrophin releasing hormone (GnRH) produced in the hypothalamus, or FH/LSH in the anterior pituitary whilst primary hypogonadism refers to a direct problem with the testes

Describe how we can distinguish between hypopituitarism and primary hypogonadism.

  • Hypopituitarism - Low FSH/LH, Low Testosterone
  • Primary hypogonadism - High FSH/LH, Low Testosterone

Endocrine Male Infertility Across The Axis.

What is Kallmann syndrome?

Kallmann syndrome is a rare genetic disorder characterised by a combination of hypogonadotropic hypogonadism and an impaired sense of smell (anosmia or hyposmia)

Describe what happens in Kallmann syndrome.

  • Physiologically during embryonic development, GnRH neurons migrate from the olfactory placode (origin of the nose) to the hypothalamus — They can then become part of the HPA axis
  • In patients with Kallmann syndrome, there is a failure of migration of GnRH neurons from olfactory fibres to the hypothalamus, and so both systems get cut out
  • NOTE: In some forms, patients do not experience anosmia/hyposmia

Describe three clinical features of Kallmann syndrome.

  • Low GnRH (cannot be measured)
  • Low FSH/LH
  • Low testosterone
  • Anosmia/hyposmia

Explain why some patients with Kallmann syndrome may experience anosmia/hyposmia.

  • Physiologically during embryonic development, GnRH neurons migrate from the olfactory placode to the hypothalamus — They can then become part of the HPA axis
  • In patients with Kallmann syndrome, there is a failure of migration of GnRH neurons with olfactory fibres, and so both systems get cut out

Olfactory Bulb.

What is hyperprolactinaemia?

Abnormally high levels of prolactin in the blood

Describe the major effect of hyperprolactinaemia.

  • Prolactin binds onto kisspeptin neurons in the hypothalamus to inhibit kisspeptin release
  • This shuts down the HPA axis

Recall three negative effects that hyperprolactinaemia can have on the hormones involved in the HPG axis.

  • Inhibition of kisspeptin release
  • Low GnRH
  • Low FSH/LH
  • Low testosterone/oestrogen

Recall three clinical features of hyperprolactinaemia.

  • Low libido
  • Lack of facial hair
  • Oligomenorrhoea (›35d menses) / Amenorrhoea (3-6 months no menses)
  • Osteoporosis — due to lack of oestrogen

Describe the treatment for hyperprolactinaemia.

  • Dopamine agonist (Cabergoline) — Dopamine is an inhibitory factor for prolactin physiologically released by the hypothalamus
  • Surgery/Deep X-Ray Therapy (DXT)

Describe what happens to sufferers of Klinefelter’s syndrome.

  • HPG axis issues
  • Testosterone issues
  • Prolactin issues
  • The overall functioning of the testes is reduced, but LH and FSH is high

How rare is Klinefelter syndrome?

1/1100 births

  • Tall stature

  • Low libido

  • Reduced chest hair
  • Mildly impaired IQ
  • Narrow shoulders

  • Female type pubic hair pattern KLINEFELTER’S SYNDROME.

Sex Chromosome Disorders.

Why do marathon runners tape their nipples when running?

  • This is to prevent the stimulation of the nipples, which leads to prolactin release
  • This prolactin release leads to decreased production of testosterone

Which hormone determines the size of the testes?

  • FSH — Spermatogenesis on Sertoli cells can cause the testes to increase in size
  • LH — Leydig cells produce testosterone, which is essential for the development of male secondary sexual characteristics and also contributes to the overall function and structure of the testes

Describe why bodybuilders may have small testes?

  • Bodybuilders typically take steroid injections, so they have high testosterone levels
  • This sends negative feedback to the HPA axis, so kisspeptin, GnRH and LH/FSH are suppressed
  • NOTE: Testosterone is aromatised to oestrogen, which then acts on kisspeptin. Kisspeptin neurones do not have testosterone receptors, so they are not directly responsive to high testosterone levels

Recall three features of the medical history that can be useful in the diagnosis of male infertility.

  • Investigate duration of problem
  • Any previous children
  • Pubertal milestones
  • Assess for prolactin symptoms, testosterone deficiency or FSH/LH deficiency
  • Medications/drugs being used
  • BMI (high/low)
  • Anosmia
  • Sexual characteristics

Recall the three investigations done to investigate male infertility.

  • Semen analysis — i.e volume, concentration and motility
  • LH/FSH
  • Prolactin
  • Morning fasting testosterone — should be highest in the morning
  • Karyotyping — complete set of chromosomes
  • Testicular volume — using orchidometer

Recall the normal sperm concentration.

15 million/ml

Recall the normal sperm motility.

Motility is ~40%

Recall two imaging techniques used to investigate male infertility.

  • Scrotal ultrasound
  • Doppler scan
  • MRI of pituitary

Recall three treatments for male infertility. General lifestyle

  • Optimise BMI
  • Smoking cessation
  • Alcohol reduction/cessation Specific treatment
  • Dopamine agonist for hyperPRL
  • Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) — i.e gonadotrophin
  • Surgery

What is premature ovarian insufficiency (POI)?

  • Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) (previously known as premature ovarian failure), is a condition in which a woman's ovaries stop functioning normally before the age of 40 — It leads to early menopause or menopause-like symptoms
  • NOTE: Spontaneous ovulation and occasional pregnancies can still occur in some cases.

How common is conception in premature ovarian insufficiency (POI)?

Conception can happen in 20% of cases

Recall three treatments for premature ovarian insufficiency

(POI). • Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) • Calcium and Vitamin D Supplements : ◦ Oestrogen is important for bone development and is low in POI. So vitamin D and calcium are given to help with this deficiency • IVF with donor eggs for women who wish to conceive

Recall three causes of premature ovarian insufficiency (POI).

  • Autoimmune
  • Genetic — i.e Turner syndrome
  • Cancer therapy — i.e radiotherapy/chemotherapy

How is premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) diagnosed?

High FSH ›25 iU/L (x2 at least 4wks apart)

Why are two high FSH measurements required for the diagnosis of premature ovarian insufficiency (POI)?

  • Two measurements of high FSH ›4 weeks apart is indicative of prolonged high FSH
  • It shows and suggests that there is a reduced negative feedback mechanism, caused by oestrogen deficiency, on the anterior pituitary causing it to continually release FSH

What hormonal patterns would you see in Anorexia Nervosa-Induced Amenorrhoea?

  • Low FSH/LH
  • Low Oestrogen
  • Everything will be down, because Anorexia Nervosa-Induced Amenorrhoea causes problems at the very top of the HPA axis. This shuts it all down.
  • NOTE: Anorexia Nervosa-Induced Amenorrhoea = Acquired (Hypothalamic) Hypogonadotrophic Hypogonadism

Recall the average age for menopause in the UK.

51

What is polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS)?

  • Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) is a common hormonal disorder that is characterised by polycystic ovaries seen on ultrasound as multiple small follicles/cysts on the ovaries Approximately what proportion of women of reproductive age are affected by PCOS?
  • 5-15% of women of reproductive age
  • It is the most common endocrine disorder in women and the most common cause of infertility in women
  • NOTE: Often times, women with PCOS have a family history

How is polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS) diagnosed?

Patients must have two out of three of the Rotterdam Diagnostic Criteria

Recall the three components of the Rotterdam Diagnostic Criteria.

  • Oligo/Anovulation — i.e ‹8-9 cycles per year
  • Clinical/Biochemical Hyperandrogenism
  • Polycystic Ovaries — upon ultrasound (US) examination

Describe the difference between clinical and biochemical hyperandrogenism?

  • Clinical hyperandrogenism = Acne, hirsutism, alopecia (hair loss)
  • Biochemical hyperandrogenism = High androgens (i.e testosterone)

What is the key driver of polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS)?

  • Insulin resistance — → impaired glucose homeostasis and hyperinsulinaemia
  • This hyperinsulinaemia can increase androgen production

Recall three clinical features for polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS).

  • Irregular menses
  • Amenorrhoea
  • Infertility
  • Increased insulin resistance
