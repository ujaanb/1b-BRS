Overview

Recall the five types of endocrine cells present in the anterior pituitary.

  • Somatotrophs
  • Lactotrophs
  • Corticotrophs
  • Thyrotrophs
  • Gonadotrophs

What hormone do the somatotrophs of the anterior pituitary gland make?

Growth hormone (GH)

What hormone do the lactotrophs of the anterior pituitary gland make?

Prolactin (PRL)

What hormone do the thyrotrophs of the anterior pituitary gland make?

Thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH)

What hormone do the gonadotrophs of the anterior pituitary gland make?

  • Luteinising hormone (LH)
  • Follicle stimulating hormone (FSH)

What hormone do the corticotrophs of the anterior pituitary gland make?

Adrenocorticotrophic hormone (ACTH)

What is special about the regulation of growth hormone?

It has two regulatory factors which have opposing effects (stimulating and inhibitory) and is the only hormone that has this

Recall the two hypothalamic regulatory hormones for growth hormone (GH).

  • Growth hormone releasing hormone (GHRH)
  • Somatostatin

What is special about the regulation of prolactin?

  • It only has an inhibitory regulatory factor (dopamine) instead of having a stimulatory one which induces secretion
  • This is the way prolactin is regulated, we do not know why A pituitary tumour of which anterior pituitary cell type can cause acromegaly?
  • Somatotrophs

  • Lactotrophs A pituitary tumour of which anterior pituitary cell type can cause a TSHoma?
  • Thyrotrophs
  • NOTE: TSHomas (pure anterior pituitary tumours that produce excess TSH) are very rare A pituitary tumour of which anterior pituitary cell type can cause a gonadotrophinoma?
  • Gonadotrophs A pituitary tumour of which anterior pituitary cell type can cause Cushing’s disease?
  • Corticotrophs

What is a TSHoma?

  • TSHomas, or thyrotropin-secreting pituitary adenomas, are rare tumours that produce thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH)
  • NOTE: They are very rare

Describe the three ways a pituitary tumour is classified.

  • Radiological
  • Functional
  • Benign/Malignant

Recall three things we are looking for upon radiological examination of the pituitary gland.

  • Size — Microadenoma — Macroadenoma
  • Sellar or suprasellar
  • Compressing optic chiasm or not?
  • Invading cavernous sinus or not?

Describe the difference between a micro and a macro adenoma.

  • Microadenoma = ‹1cm (10mm)
  • Macroadenoma = ›1cm (10mm)

Describe the difference between sellar and suprasellar tumours.

  • A sellar tumour is one that is within the sella turcica
  • A suprasellar tumour is one that has grown above/out of the sella turcica

Which is more clinically significant, a sellar or suprasellar tumour and why?

  • Suprasellar tumour
  • This is because a suprasellar tumour is much more likely to compress the optic chiasm and cause visual disturbances which are very disruptive for the sufferer

What is the cavernous sinus?

The cavernous sinus is a large, complex network of veins and cranial nerves located on either side of the pituitary gland at the base of the brain.

Describe the significance of the pituitary gland invading the cavernous sinus.

  • The cavernous sinus contains structures such as the internal carotid artery, cranial nerves III, IV, V1, V2 & VI
  • Pituitary adenomas can compress these structures leading to complications such as strokes, ptosis, diplopia, loss of facial sensation and poor eye movement

Compressed Optic Chiasm.

Describe what we are looking for upon functional examination of the pituitary gland.

  • We are looking at whether the tumour secretes excess hormone(s) or not.
  • Excess secretion of a specific pituitary hormone — i.e prolactinoma = excess prolactin
  • No excess secretion of pituitary hormone — a.k.a Non Functioning Adenoma

What is a non-functioning adenoma?

  • A non-functioning pituitary adenoma (NFA) is a benign tumour that grows in the pituitary gland and doesn't produce hormones
  • They differ to functioning pituitary adenomas which secrete hormones like prolactin, growth hormone, or ACTH — NOTE: Non-functioning adenomas can commonly cause headaches due to the pressure exerted by these tumours within the skull

Describe what we are looking for upon benign/malignant examination of the pituitary gland.

  • Mitotic index measured using Ki67 index – benign is ‹3%
  • NOTE: Pituitary adenomas can have benign histology but display malignant behaviour

Describe the prevalence of pituitary carcinoma.

Pituitary carcinomas are very rare (‹0.5% of pituitary tumours)

What is the Ki-67 index?

The Ki-67 index is a measurement used to assess the proliferative activity (growth rate) of cells in a tissue, particularly in tumours.

Describe how the Ki-67 index is used in the assessment of a tumour.

  • A high Ki-67 index suggests a high proliferation rate, indicating a more aggressive tumour with a potentially poorer prognosis.
  • A low Ki-67 index indicates slower growth and often correlates with a better prognosis.
  • It is often considered alongside other histological factors to grade or stage a tumour

Describe and explain the effects that hyperprolactinaemia can have on the HPA axis.

  • Prolactin binds onto kisspeptin neurons in the hypothalamus to inhibit kisspeptin release
  • This effectively shuts down the HPA axis, reducing GnRH, FSH/LH, oestrogen/testosterone levels

What is oligomenorrhoea?

Oligomenorrhea means having infrequent menstrual periods, defined as intervals between periods longer than 35 days, or having fewer than 9 periods per year

What is a prolactinoma?

A prolactinoma is the commonest functioning pituitary adenoma that results in excess serum prolactin — Usually serum [prolactin] ›5000 mU/L

Describe how the size of a pituitary tumour can be important to us clinically for prolactinoma.

Serum [prolactin] is often proportional to tumour size

Recall three clinical symptoms of a prolactinoma.

  • Amenorrhoea/menstrual disturbance
  • Erectile dysfunction
  • Galactorrhoea
  • Subfertility
  • Reduced libido

What is galactorrhoea?

  • Galactorrhea is a milky nipple discharge unrelated to the usual milk production of breastfeeding Is galactorrhoea usually seen in men? Why?
  • No.
  • This is because oestrogen is also required to allow the release of milk from the breast, and men do not have as much oestrogen as women

Recall two physiological causes of an elevated prolactin.

  • Pregnancy/breastfeeding
  • Stress — Exercise, seizure, venepuncture
  • Nipple/chest wall stimulation

Recall two pathological causes of an elevated prolactin.

  • Primary hypothyroidism — TRH (elevated in primary hypothyroidism) has a weak secondary effect of inducing prolactin release from the anterior pituitary
  • Polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS)
  • Chronic renal failure — impaired excretion of prolactin

Describe a side effect of TRH other than TSH release.

  • Prolactin production
  • This explains why primary hypothyroidism (which shows elevated TRH/TSH) is often associated with hyperprolactinaemia

Recall three iatrogenic causes of an elevated prolactin.

  • Antipsychotics — via reduction in dopamine
  • Selective serotonin re-uptake inhibitors (SSRIs) — increased serotonin reduces dopamine levels
  • Anti-emetics — D2 receptor antagonism in pituitary → direct effect
  • High dose oestrogen — reduction in dopamine, stimulation of lactotrophs
  • Opiates — dopamine inhibition

[Extra notes] SEROTONIN AND PROLACTIN.

SSRIs increase serotonin levels in the brain by blocking reuptake. • Serotonin (5-HT) can modulate hypothalamic neurons, including those that control dopamine. ◦ Increased serotonergic activity inhibits dopamine release in the tuberoinfundibular pathway. ◦ Less dopamine → reduced inhibition on lactotrophs → prolactin levels rise.

What does iatrogenic mean?

An iatrogenic condition is a state of ill health or adverse effect caused by medical treatment

What is the next step to take once pathological elevation of prolactin has been detected in a patient?

Once a true pathological elevation of serum prolactin has been confirmed, a pituitary MRI can be organised

Describe the first line of treatment against a prolactinoma.

  • The first line of treatment against a prolactinoma is always medical, not surgical
  • Dopamine receptor agonists are the most common form of treatment — i.e cabergoline (bromocriptine)

What is cabergoline?

  • Cabergoline is a dopamine receptor agonist
  • NOTE: Cabergoline is safely used during pregnancy

What is the aim of prolactinoma treatment?

The aim is to normalise serum prolactin and shrink the prolactinoma

Describe the difference in treatment of microprolactinomas and macroprolactinomas.

  • Surgery is rarely required for microprolactinomas, but may be necessary for macroprolactinomas
  • Cabergoline dose also usually increases with the size of a prolactinoma — Microprolactinomas = low cabergoline dose, Macroprolactinomas = high cabergoline dose

Describe how dopamine receptor agonists work.

  • Dopamine receptor agonists work in the same way that dopamine would normally work
  • They stimulate D2 receptors and since dopamine physiologically works to inhibit prolactin secretion, this effect is mimicked

Dopamine Receptor Agonists.

Describe the difference between gigantism and acromegaly.

  • Gigantism presents in children
  • Acromegaly presents in adults
  • This is because in adults, the epiphyseal growth plates have already closed, so it is not possible for growth hormone to contribute to the continual growth of the physes and hence the entire body

What is unique about the clinical presentation of acromegaly?

  • It has an insidious presentation
  • This means that the symptoms often progressively get worse and the mean time from onset of symptoms to diagnosis is ~10 years — Patients often don’t notice any differences until late

Recall five clinical features of acromegaly.

  • Sweatiness
  • Headache
  • Coarsening of facial features — Macroglossia — Prominent nose
  • Large jaw - prognathism
  • Increased hand and feet size
  • Snoring and obstructive sleep apnoea
  • Hypertension
  • Impaired glucose tolerance/diabetes mellitus

Why is impaired glucose tolerance seen in patients with acromegaly?

  • Growth hormone is a counter-regulatory hormone that antagonises insulin — via down-regulation of insulin receptors and inhibition of signalling pathway
  • GH hence promotes insulin resistance by interfering with insulin's ability to signal cells to take up glucose from the bloodstream, leading to hyperglycaemia (elevated blood glucose levels).

Describe two mechanisms through which growth hormone propagates growth and development.

  • Growth hormone (GH) directly induces the growth and development of body tissues
  • It also acts on the liver to increase the expression of IGF-1 and IGF-2 (lesser) which can both increase growth and development in body tissues

What is another name for IGF-1?

Somatomedin

Describe how acromegaly is diagnosed. Serum IGF-1

  • Elevated serum IGF-1 can be used to diagnose acromegaly Growth hormone

Explain why IGF-1 is used in the diagnosis of acromegaly and not GH.

Growth hormone (GH) is secreted in a pulsatile manner, so measuring it is not very helpful in the diagnosis of acromegaly

What is the major risk that untreated acromegaly poses?

Increased cardiovascular risk in untreated acromegaly

What is the first line of treatment for acromegaly?

Surgical — Trans-sphenoidal surgery

Trans-Sphenoidal Surgery.

What is the aim of medical treatment for acromegaly?

The aim is to normalise serum GH and IGF-1

Recall two medical treatments used to treat acromegaly.

  • Octreotide (somatostatin analogues)
  • Cabergoline — dopamine agonist
  • Radiotherapy (slow) — may lead to hypopituitarism

Why are dopamine agonists such as Cabergoline used to treat acromegaly?

This is because GH-secreting pituitary tumours frequently express D2 receptors which can help us to inhibit the secretion of GH

What is Cushing’s syndrome?

A disease where there is too much cortisol in the blood

What is Cushing’s disease?

Cushing's disease is a disorder that occurs when the pituitary gland produces too much adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)

Describe the difference between Cushing’s syndrome and Cushing’s disease.

  • Cushing’s syndrome is a condition where there is too much cortisol in the blood
  • Cushing’s disease is a disease which specifically refers to a pituitary corticotroph adenoma secreting ACTH
  • NOTE: An example of Cushing’s syndrome without Cushing’s disease could be an adrenal tumour — i.e hyperplasia of adrenal cortex
  • REMEMBER: ACTH → cortisol release from adrenal cortex

Recall seven clinical features of Cushing’s syndrome.

  • Flushed skin
  • Stretchy skin
  • Purple striae (stretch marks)
  • Centripetal obesity
  • Increased height
  • Proximal myopathy
  • Poor wound healing
  • Easy bruising
  • Moon face
  • Red cheeks
  • Fat pads (buffalo hump)
  • Thin skin
  • Mental changes
  • Osteoporosis
  • Impaired glucose toleranc e
  • Pendulous abdomen

Recall two tests used in the investigation of Cushing’s disease.

  • 24h urine free cortisol
  • Late night cortisol
  • Oral dexamethasone

Describe the results we would expect for the 24h urine cortisol in those with Cushing’s disease.

Elevated 24h cortisol — This shows that there is increased cortisol secretion throughout the day SERUM CORTISOL IN CUSHING’S.
