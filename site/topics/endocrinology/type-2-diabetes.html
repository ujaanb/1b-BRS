Overview

What is Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus?

Type 2 diabetes mellitus is a condition in which the combination of insulin resistance and beta-cell failure result in hyperglycaemia

What is commonly associated with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus?

Obesity

How is Type 2 Diabetes generally managed?

The resultant chronic hyperglycaemia may initially be managed by changes to diet / weight loss and may even be reversible — With time, glucose lowering therapy including insulin, is needed

What is latent autoimmune diabetes in adults (LADA)?

  • Latent autoimmune diabetes in adults (LADA) is a form of autoimmune diabetes, similar to Type 1 diabetes
  • However, unlike typical Type 1 diabetes, LADA develops more slowly, and because it occurs in adults, it is often initially misdiagnosed as Type 2 diabetes.

Describe how we can generally distinguish between Type 2 diabetes and latent autoimmune diabetes in adults (LADA).

  • The two conditions are very similar in the way they present (i.e in adults, later on in life) but because LADA is typically caused by beta- cell failure and Type 2 diabetes is usually down to insulin resistance, C-peptide levels can be a good indicator of diabetic aetiology — C-peptide would be low in LADA, but slightly higher in Type 2 diabetes mellitus
  • Testing for pancreatic auto-antibodies is a common way of distinguishing between the two — LADA is autoimmune, T2DM is not

What is monogenic diabetes?

Monogenic diabetes is a rare type of diabetes caused by mutations in a single gene, distinguishing it from the more common types, Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes, which involve multiple genes and environmental factors

How can monogenic diabetes present?

Monogenic diabetes can present phenotypically as Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes — i.e MODY, mitochondrial diabetes

What is MODY?

  • Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a form of monogenic diabetes that typically arises due to a mutation in a single gene affecting insulin production or function
  • MODY follows an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, meaning it is passed down through families and can be traced across generations.
  • NOTE: MODY usually manifests before age 25

Diabetes Classification.

When may Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) typically present?

  • Traditionally T2DM was thought to be a condition of late adulthood
  • Now good evidence that it can present throughout every decade of life — T2DM may present in youth / young adults
  • Prevalence of Type 2 diabetes mellitus is increasing in all age groups but rapidly in early adulthood

Describe the epidemiology of Type 2 diabetes.

  • Type 2 diabetes has an ever-increasing prevalence, and is more commonly being diagnosed in younger people
  • It is largely prevalent in ethnic groups that move from the rural to the urban lifestyle

Epidemiology Of Type 2 Diabetes.

Recall three ways we can diagnostically monitor the development of type 2 diabetes.

  • Fasting glucose levels
  • 2 hour glucose tolerance test (OGTT)
  • HbA1c

Describe how fasting glucose levels can be used to determine the stages of development for Type 2 diabetes.

  • ≤6 mmol/L = normal
  • 6-7 mmol/L = impaired fasting glycaemia
  • ≥7 mmol/L = Type 2 diabetes

What is the 2 hour glucose tolerance test (OGTT)?

  • The OGTT results reveal how efficiently the body processes glucose over a 2-hour period
  • Usually, blood glucose levels will rise after consuming a glucose drink (75g) and then return to normal as insulin facilitates glucose uptake by cells
  • However, in those with Type 2 diabetes mellitus, there may be impaired glucose tolerance

Describe how the 2 hour glucose tolerance test (OGTT) can be used to determine the stages of development for Type 2 diabetes.

  • ≤7.7 mmol/L = normal
  • 7.7-11 mmol/L = impaired glucose tolerance
  • ≥11 mmol/L = Type 2 diabetes

Describe how HbA1c levels can be used to determine the stages of development for Type 2 diabetes.

  • ≤42 mmol/L = normal
  • 42-48 mmol/L = Pre-diabetes
  • ≥48 = Type 2 diabetes

Describe how impaired beta cell function and insulin resistance are related in Type 2 diabetes.

  • Insulin resistance is thought to be the main mechanism behind Type 2 diabetes
  • However, without impaired beta cell function, there would simply be an i ncrease in insulin production sufficient to overcome the level of resistance and never lead to diabetes

What actually happens is, the level of insulin resistance gets to a point where beta cells are no longer able to produce enough insulin to overcome the level of resistance, leading to the chronic hyperglycaemia seen in Type 2 diabetes

Hence, both impaired beta cell function and insulin resistance are drivers of Type 2 diabetes

Development Of Type 2 Diabetes.

Describe the difference between absolute and relative insulin deficiency.

  • Absolute insulin deficiency is when there is little to no production of insulin, usually secondary to beta cell impairment
  • Relative insulin deficiency is when there is still insulin production, however it is not enough to overcome the insulin resistance — This leads to the hyperglycaemia seen in type 2 diabetes

Beta Cell Function At Diagnosis Of Type 2 Diabetes.

Describe why ketoacidosis is not normally observed in Type 2 diabetes.

  • Usually in Type 2 diabetes, there is relative insulin deficiency meaning there is production of insulin, but it is just not enough to overcome the insulin resistance
  • Insulin is physiologically important in preventing excessive lipolysis and hence production of NEFAs → acetyl CoA (via beta-oxidation) → ketone production
  • This means its presence in those with Type 2 diabetes is usually enough to prevent ketoacidosis
  • NOTE: In individuals with insulin resistance, insulin’s ability to prevent lipolysis is impaired, but it is not completely ineffective.

Describe the effects that long-term Type 2 diabetes can have on pancreatic output.

In long-duration type 2 diabetes, beta-cell failure may cause progression from relative to complete insulin deficiency

Long-Term Type 2 Diabetes.

Describe the pathophysiology of Type 2 diabetes.

  • Type 2 diabetes is down to both insulin resistance and insulin secretion defects (beta-cell failure) — Fatty acids are important in the pathogenesis and complications — Genes and the intrauterine environment and adult environment are also very important
  • NOTE: T2DM is a heterogenous condition meaning people develop T2DM at variable BMI, ages and progress differently. There is no “one size fits all”

What is the main perpetrator behind reduction in insulin sensitivity seen in patients with Type 2 diabetes mellitus?

  • Excess visceral adiposity (fat) — i.e liver, pancreas
  • NOTE: Fat should be stored under the skin, but it can go to the wrong place when we have exceeded our visceral adiposity limits

Describe how the insulin responses vary between sufferers of Type 2 diabetes and those with normal glucose tolerance.

  • In type 2 diabetes mellitus, the first-phase insulin release is often significantly impaired or completely absent
  • This means that there is frequent hyperglycaemia as the body struggles to lower the blood glucose adequately

Tolerance And T2Dm.

What is the First Phase Insulin Release?

The first-phase insulin release is the initial burst of insulin released by the pancreas in response to an increase in blood glucose, such as

after eating. Describe what happens to the First Phase Insulin Release in Type 2 diabetes mellitus.

In type 2 diabetes mellitus, the first-phase insulin release is often significantly impaired or completely absent. — This loss leads to higher post-meal blood glucose levels, increased metabolic strain on the pancreas, and the progression of hyperglycaemia.

First Phase Insulin Release.

Describe what happens to glucose uptake into skeletal muscle in Type 2 diabetes mellitus.

In type 2 diabetes, reduced insulin action (due to insulin resistance) causes less uptake of glucose into skeletal muscle

Explain what happens to hepatic glucose output in Type 2 diabetes mellitus.

Hepatic glucose production is also increased due to both a reduction in insulin action and increase in glucagon action

Describe the relationship between glucagon and insulin.

  • Insulin lowers blood glucose after meals, while glucagon raises it during fasting or low-glucose situations
  • Insulin also lowers glucagon levels

Describe the two mechanisms, other than dietary glucose intake, that hyperglycaemia can occur through in Type 2 diabetes.

  • Impaired insulin-mediated glucose disposal
  • Excessive glucagon-mediated glucose output — i.e hepatic glucose output (HGO)

Non-Dietary Induced Hyperglycaemia.

Describe the relationship between insulin resistance and insulin secretion.

  • Physiologically, insulin resistance rises with insulin secretion
  • NOTE: Sensitivity is inversely proportional to resistance. As resistance rises, sensitivity decreases

Relationship Between Insulin Resistance And Insulin Secretion.

Describe the consequences of insulin resistance in the liver.

In the liver, there would be increased hepatic glucose output (HGO) due to the lack of signalling from insulin

Describe the consequences of insulin resistance in the skeletal muscle.

In the skeletal muscle, there would be impaired glucose uptake as well as increased risk of muscle wasting as the body isn’t getting adequate energy from glucose

Describe the consequences of insulin resistance in the adipocytes.

In the adipocytes, there would be impaired glucose uptake as well as lipolysis

Describe the difference between monogenic and polygenic Type 2 diabetes.

  • Monogenic diabetes results from mutations in a single gene that directly affects glucose metabolism, leading to diabetes — ‘Born with it, always going to develop diabetes’
  • Polygenic diabetes arises from the combined effects of multiple genetic variants (i.e polymorphisms), each contributing a small amount to the overall risk of developing the disease — ‘Not born with it but high risk and may develop later depending on other factors’

Describe the role that polygenic (genetic) risk plays in one’s chances of being diagnosed with Type 2 diabetes.

High genetic risk increases one’s chances of being diagnosed with Type 2 diabetes and reduces the impact of the environment on your expressed phenotype

Polygenic Risk And Type 2 Diabetes.

Describe how GWAS can be useful to Type 2 diabetes treatment.

  • Nucleotide changes (SNPs) are assessed in control groups compared with those that have Type 2 diabetes mellitus
  • Specific nucleotide changes are often present in type 2 diabetes group but not controls

Describe how SNPs can affect one’s risk of developing Type 2 diabetes.

  • Each individual SNP has only a mild effect on risk
  • The cumulative effect of all SNPs have a bigger effect

Describe the role of obesity in the development of Type 2 diabetes.

Obesity is a major risk factor for Type 2 diabetes mellitus — Fatty acids and adipocytokines are important

What is an adipocytokine?

  • Adipocytokines are signalling molecules that are secreted by adipose (fat) tissue
  • Dysregulation of adipocytokines, particularly decreased adiponectin and increased pro-inflammatory cytokines, is associated with the development of insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes.
  • NOTE: Adiponectin is an anti-inflammatory adipocytokine, protective against T2DM.

What percentage of T2DM patients are obese?

80% of all Type 2 diabetes patients are obese

Recall two other major factors that can play large roles in the development of Type 2 diabetes.

  • Perturbations in gut microbiota
  • Intra-uterine growth retardation

Describe how perturbations in gut microbiota can play a role in the development of Type 2 diabetes.

  • Dysbiotic gut microbiota can cause bacterial lipopolysaccharides to ferment microbiota to short chain fatty acids and bacterial modulation of bile acids
  • This leads to inflammation, signalling metabolic pathways

Describe how intra-uterine growth retardation can play a role in the development of Type 2 diabetes.

  • The Barker-Hale’s hypothesis suggests that if you've experienced intrauterine growth retardation, then through methylation changes, you are at higher risk of developing T2DM
  • NOTE: Intrauterine growth retardation, or IUGR, is when a baby in the womb (a fetus) does not grow as expected.

Recall three clinical signs of Type 2 diabetes.

  • Hyperglycaemia
  • Overweight
  • Dyslipidaemia
  • Fewer osmotic symptoms
  • Insulin resistance
  • Later insulin deficiency

What is dyslipidaemia?

Dyslipidemia is a metabolic disorder characterised by abnormally high or low amounts of any or all lipids or lipoproteins in the blood

Recall three risk factors for Type 2 diabetes.

  • Age
  • PCOS
  • High BMI
  • Family History
  • Ethnicity
  • Inactivity

Describe how Type 2 diabetes is diagnosed.

  • 1x HbA1c ›48 mmol/L with symptoms — OR
  • 2x HbA1c ›48 mmol/L without symptoms (to make sure there is no mistake)
  • This tends to be the most reliable way of monitoring hyperglycaemia

Recall an acute complication of Type 2 diabetes.

Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state

Recall two chronic complications of Type 2 diabetes.

  • Ischaemic heart disease
  • Retinopathy
  • NOTE: Retinopathy is often confused as an acute complication, but it is chronic as it often takes a few years to develop

Describe what is meant by a hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS).

  • The hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state refers to a life-threatening condition resulting from severe hyperglycaemia and dehydration, primarily affecting individuals with Type 2 diabetes
  • There is often an identifiable precipitating event — i.e myocardial infarction, infection

Describe two complications of a hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS).

The severe hyperglycaemia seen in hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic

state (HHS) can lead to increased osmotic diuresis which causes extreme dehydration in those with Type 2 diabetes

Due to this increased osmotic diuresis, patients with HHS are also at great risk of thrombosis as their blood becomes incredibly hyperviscous, due to increased fluid loss

Describe why a hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS) is more commonly observed in Type 2 diabetes mellitus.

  • Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS) is driven by relative insulin deficiency, insufficient to prevent glucose levels from rising uncontrollably
  • We often do not see this in Type 1 diabetes mellitus because although there is absolute insulin deficiency which would → hyperglycaemia, the absolute insulin deficiency leads to lipolysis → ketone formation (via NEFA oxidation) → diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) — Hence, the patients in Type 1 diabetes with hyperglycaemia will typically present with DKA before they progress to HHS

Recall a condition that commonly presents with hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS).

Renal failure

Describe three ways we can differentiate between a hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS) and diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA).

  • In HHS, there is hypovolaemia but in DKA there is just dehydration — i.e due to osmotic diuresis secondary to hyperglycaemia
  • In HHS the blood glucose is usually ›30 mmol/L but in DKA it is ›11 mmol/L
  • In HHS the urine ketones are ++ or less, but in DKA the urine ketones are +++ or more
  • In HHS the blood pH is ›7.3, but in DKA the blood pH is ‹7.3 — i.e due to acidity of ketones
  • In HHS the bicarbonate is ›15, but in DKA the bicarbonate is ‹15 — i.e due to bicarbonates being depleted by use in the buffer system to balance out acidic blood pH in DKA — H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ → H₂CO₃ → CO₂+H₂O - HCO3- is depleted in high H+ states
  • In HHS the osmolarity is ›320 mosmol/kg, but in DKA the osmolarity is variable

How do we manage diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS)?

Intravenous (IV) fluids immediately

Ketoacidosis (Dka).

Describe the management of Type 1 diabetes.

  • Exogenous insulin (basal-bolus regime)
  • Self-monitoring of glucose
  • Structured education
  • Technology

Describe the management of Type 2 diabetes.

  • Diet
  • Oral medication
  • Structured education
  • May need insulin later
  • Remission / reversal

Describe the differences in management between Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes.

T2DM management is more focused on diet/lifestyle changes, whereas T1DM is centred around insulin therapy — There is also effort made to remit T2DM which isn’t the case for T1DM as it is an autoimmune condition

Recall three dietary recommendations made to patients with Type 2 diabetes.

  • Total calories control — to avoid weight gain
  • Reduce calories as fat — to avoid weight gain
  • Reduce calories as refined carbohydrate — to avoid uncontrolled spikes/surges in blood glucose
  • Increase calories as complex carbohydrate
  • Increase soluble fibre
  • Decrease sodium

Recall three things that are routinely monitored during a Type 2 diabetes consultation.

  • Glycaemia — HbA1c, glucose monitoring if on insulin, medication review
  • Weight assessment
  • Blood pressure
  • Dyslipidaemia — cholesterol profile
  • Screening for complications — foot check (peripheral neuropathy leading to reduced sensation in the foot), retinal screening

Describe three major facets of the pathophysiology of Type 2 diabetes.

  • Excess hepatic glucose production
  • Resistance to action of circulating insulin
  • Inadequate insulin production for extent of insulin resistance — i.e due to beta cell dysfunction
  • Excess glucose in circulation (hyperglycaemia)

What one thing can address all of these problems?

Weight loss

Recall a drug that can reduce hepatic glucose output.

Metformin

Recall a drug that can improve insulin senstivity.

  • Metformin
  • Thiozolidinediones

Recall a drug that can increase insulin production to overcome resistance.

  • Sulphonylureas
  • DPP4-inhibitors
  • GLP-1 Agonists
  • NOTE: DPP-4 does not break down insulin ; it breaks down incretin hormones (i.e GLP-1, GIP), which in turn signal the pancreas to release insulin

Recall a drug that can address hyperglycaemia.

  • Alpha-glucosidase inhibitor
  • SGLT-2 inhibitor

What is alpha-glucosidase?

Alpha-glucosidase is an enzyme in the small intestine that breaks down complex carbohydrates into glucose

What is Metformin and when is it used?

Metformin is an oral medication commonly used as a first-line treatment for Type 2 diabetes mellitus if dietary/lifestyle adjustments have had no impact

Describe the major effects of Metformin.

  • Metformin reduces insulin resistance (increases sensitivity)
  • Metformin reduces hepatic glucose output (HGO)
  • Metformin increases peripheral glucose disposal

Recall two contraindications of Metformin.

Metformin is contraindicated in severe liver, severe cardiac or moderate renal failure

Describe the side effects of Metformin.

Metformin has GI side effects

What are sulphonylureas?

Sulphonylureas are a class of oral medications used to treat Type 2 diabetes mellitus.

Describe the major effects of sulphonylureas.

Sulphonylureas boost insulin secretion

Describe the mechanism behind how sulphonylureas work.

  • Physiologically, the ATP-sensitive potassium channel on beta-cells needs to close in order for insulin to be produced
  • Sulphonylureas work by closing this ATP-sensitive potassium channel independent of ATP to induce extra production of insulin

Sulphonylureas.

What is Pioglitazone?

  • Pioglitazone is an oral medication used to treat Type 2 diabetes mellitus
  • It belongs to a class of drugs known as thiazolidinediones (TZDs)

Describe the major effects of Pioglitazone.

  • Pioglitazone is an insulin-sensitiser used to tackle the effects of insulin resistance
  • NOTE: It can be used in those with insulin resistance who are quite lean.

Effect Of Glucose Lowering Therapies On Weight.

What is Glucagon-like peptide (GLP-1)?

  • Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) is a hormone that plays a critical role in glucose metabolism and appetite regulation
  • It can be used to treat diabetes mellitus

When is GLP-1 physiologically secreted?

GLP-1 is a hormone naturally released in response to nutrients in the gut

Describe the major effects of Glucagon-like peptide (GLP-1).

  • They stimulate insulin, suppressing glucagon — incretin effect
  • They increase satiety (feeling of fullness)

Describe the half-life of Glucagon-like peptide (GLP-1).

Short half life due to rapid degradation from enzyme dipeptidyl peptidase-4

How can we address the short half-life of incretins such as dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4)?

DPP-4 inhibitors

What is an incretin?

Incretins are hormones that are primarily responsible for the "incretin effect," where insulin secretion is significantly increased when glucose is ingested orally compared to intravenously
