Overview

Which part of the pituitary gland is responsible for the secretion of arginine vasopressin?

Posterior pituitary

What is the role of a magnocellular neuron?

  • To transport regulatory hormones from the hypothalamus to the posterior pituitary gland for storage.
  • They are longer than parvocellular neurons and they allow the posterior pituitary to be anatomically continuous with hypothalamus

Which two hormones do the hypothalamic magnocellular neurons secrete?

  • Arginine vasopressin (AVP)
  • Oxytocin

What are the names of the two types of cell bodies present in the hypothalamus that act on the posterior pituitary and from which magnocellular neurons originate?

  • Supraoptic
  • Paraventricular
  • Magnocellular neurons originate from these nuclei and carry signals to the posterior pituitary

What is the difference between the supraoptic and paraventricular cell bodies (nuclei) of the hypothalamus?

  • Supraoptic nuclei store AVP (ADH)
  • Paraventricular nuclei store oxytocin

Describe the path of ADH from the hypothalamus to the posterior pituitary.

Hypothalamus → Magnocellular neurons → Posterior pituitary

What is the name of the receptor that ADH works on?

V2 receptor

Where is this receptor located?

Basolateral membrane of the kidney’s collecting duct cells

What is the physiological effect of arginine vasopressin (ADH)?

  • To concentrate urine — To increase reabsorption of water from the urine back into the blood
  • Anti-diuresis

Recall two other physiological effects of arginine vasopressin (ADH).

  • Vasoconstriction — via V1 receptor
  • To stimulate ACTH release from the anterior pituitary

Describe the mechanism through which vasopressin concentrates urine.

  • AVP binds to the V2 receptors present on the basolateral membrane (closer to blood capillary) of the cells in the kidney’s collecting duct and this leads to a signalling cascade of reactions — cAMP and protein kinase A are involved in this cascade
  • This subsequently results in the channels aquaporin-2 (into cell) and aquaporin-3 (out of cell) being opened on the surfaces of the collecting duct cells
  • Water flows out of the collecting duct of the kidney of the and into the capillaries down the concentration gradient to maintain osmolarity
  • This ultimately means that the concentration of urine has increased, and the volume of the urine has decreased

What is the best radiological way to view the pituitary gland?

  • MRI scans
  • CT scans are not very clear for visualising the pituitary gland

Describe how AVP release is induced by the physiological mechanisms of the body.

AVP release is induced by an increase in plasma osmolality — i.e a decrease in water content in the body

What detects the increase in plasma osmolality?

This increase in osmolality is detected by osmoreceptors

Where are osmoreceptors located?

  • Osmoreceptors are specialised sensory receptors located in the hypothalamus
  • NOTE: Osmoreceptors are cells

Describe the difference between osmolarity and osmolality.

The main difference between osmolarity and osmolality is the unit of measurement and how the concentration is expressed: — Osmolarity is the number of milliosmoles of solute per litre of solution (mmol/L). Osmolarity is dependent on the volume of the solution, and changes with temperature — Osmolality is the number of milliosmoles of solute per kilogram of solution (mOsm/kg H2O). Osmolality is dependent on the mass of the solvent, and is thermodynamically accurate because it's temperature-independent

What do osmoreceptors detect?

  • Osmoreceptors are sensitive to changes in plasma osmolality, which is the concentration of solutes in the blood
  • NOTE: Osmoreceptors are cells within the hypothalamus of the brain

Describe how osmoreceptors regulate AVP release.

  • There is an increase in plasma osmolality
  • This increase is detected by the osmoreceptor cells which causes them to release water into the plasma
  • The osmoreceptors shrink and there is hence an increased osmoreceptor firing rate — This increased firing rate is a result of the conformational change made by the osmoreceptor
  • This resulting increased osmoreceptor firing rate leads to AVP release

Describe how the shrinkage of osmoreceptor cells leads to the release of ADH. (8.2, Appetite)

  • Osmoreceptor cells shrink when the plasma is more concentrated (increased osmolality) — water goes from cells to plasma to balance concentration
  • The proportion of cation channels on cells increases → membrane depolarisation
  • Depolarisation sends signals to the ADH producing cells to increase ADH production
  • Fluid retention is induced by ADH production as well as a physiological response of drinking

And Vice Versa

Describe how osmoreceptors are able to detect changes in blood plasma osmolality despite being located within the brain. (8.2, Appetite)

  • The majority of the brain is not fully exposed to blood plasma due to the presence of the highly selective blood brain barrier (BBB)
  • However, the two regions of the hypothalamus that are involved in osmoregulation (OVLT and subfornical gland) are not protected by the blood-brain barrier (BBB)
  • This means they can come into contact with the blood plasma and

can be sensitive to changes in osmolality Recall the two main physiological impacts of increased osmoreceptor firing rate.

  • AVP release via the hypothalamo-pituitary axis
  • Thirst

Recall the two main vasopressin-related conditions.

  • AVP deficiency (AVP-D)
  • AVP resistance (AVP-R)

What happens in AVP deficiency (AVP-D)?

Cannot produce sufficient AVP, collecting duct of kidney able to respond to it

What happens in AVP resistance (AVP-R)?

(see lecture diagram / notes)

Can produce AVP, collecting duct of kidney unable to respond to it What was the old name for AVP deficiency?

  • Cranial diabetes insipidus
  • This is because there is a problem within the hypothalamo-pituitary axis which causes problems with production of AVP
  • NOTE: “Insipidus” means “not tasty/lacking flavour” which explains

why the name was given to this condition in which patients produce very dilute urine Which is more common; diabetes mellitus or AVP deficiency/resistance?

Diabetes mellitus —

When you see symptoms such as dilute urine, polyuria etc., think diabetes and not AVP resistance/deficiency

(see lecture diagram / notes)

Describe how AVP deficiency/resistance can lead to death.

  • AVP deficiency/resistance → Low AVP levels → Large volumes of dilute urine being produced
  • This remains the case even when there is increased plasma osmolality, as the AVP is not able to intervene and concentrate the urine
  • In cases where there is no access to water, the patient cannot compensate for this excess loss in fluid and increased osmolality,

which leads to dehydration and death Recall three clinical symptoms of AVP deficiency/resistance.

  • Polyuria
  • Nocturia
  • Thirst – often extreme
  • Polydipsia

Describe the difference in the aetiology of polyuria, nocturia & polydipsia between diabetes mellitus and AVP deficiency/resistance.

  • In diabetes mellitus (hyperglycaemia), these symptoms are due to osmotic diuresis
  • However, in AVP deficiency/resistance, these symptoms are due to an inability of AVP to concentrate the urine
  • REMEMBER: The most common cause of polyuria, nocturia & polydipsia is diabetes mellitus, NOT due to a problem with vasopressin

What is osmotic diuresis?

Osmotic diuresis is the increased production of urine due to the presence of certain substances in the renal filtrate (in diabetes, usually glucose) that cause water to be drawn into the urine

Recall three causes of AVP deficiency.

  • Acquired — Traumatic brain injury — Pituitary surgery — Pituitary tumours — Inflammation of pituitary stalk — Autoimmune
  • Congenital (rare)

Recall two causes of AVP resistance.

  • Congenital (rare) — Mutation in V2 receptor gene or aquaporin-2 channel
  • Lithium drugs

Which is more common, AVP deficiency or AVP resistance?

AVP deficiency — AVP resistance is very rare

Recall two characteristics of the urine in those with AVP deficiency/resistance.

  • Very dilute (hypo - osmolar)
  • Large volumes

Recall two characteristics of the plasma in those with AVP deficiency/resistance.

  • Increased concentration ( hyper-osmolar ) as patient becomes dehydrated
  • Increased sodium (hypernatraemia)
  • Glucose normal (make sure you ALWAYS check this in a patient with these symptoms)

What is another condition that mimics the side effects of AVP resistance?

Psychogenic polydipsia

What is psychogenic polydipsia?

  • Polydipsia is excessive or abnormal thirst, accompanied by intake of excessive quantities of water or fluid
  • Psychogenic polydipsia is characterised by the excessive volitional water intake that lacks a medical cause — It is often seen in patients with psychiatric disorders and/or neurodevelopmental disorders
  • There may be no physical effects, but hyponatraemia can occur

In which type of patients is psychogenic polydipsia most common?

Psychogenic polydipsia is commonly encountered in patients with psychiatric disorders, particularly in those with schizophrenia

How can psychogenic polydipsia be diagnosed?

Water deprivation test

What is the water deprivation test?

A water deprivation test is a medical examination that measures how well a patient's AVP is functioning and helps diagnose AVP-D or psychogenic polydipsia

What happens during the water deprivation test?

During the test, a patient is not allowed to drink water for a set period of time, and the: — urine volumes — urine concentrations (osmolality) — plasma concentrations are monitored (osmolality)

Describe how the water deprivation test can be used to distinguish between psychogenic polydipsia and AVP deficiency/resistance.

  • Patients with psychogenic polydipsia have no problems with producing AVP or their hypothalamo-pituitary axis — The only problem they have is that they drink too much water — This means that their urine osmolality should naturally increase over time
  • Patients with AVP deficiency/resistance are unable to concentrate their urine, so their urine osmolarity remains low even in the absence of water
  • This is because unlike patients with psychogenic polydipsia, patients with AVP deficiency/resistance don’t have any AVP to increase their urine osmolarity

Describe how the water deprivation test can be used to distinguish between AVP deficiency and AVP resistance.

  • Desmopressin (AVP-mimic) is administered to patients after eight hours
  • Patients with AVP deficiency are able to concentrate their urine now as their kidneys have no problems with responding to desmopressin
  • Patients with AVP resistance are unable to concentrate their urine as the collecting duct cells of the kidney are unable to respond to the desmopressin

Recall another thing that needs to be done during the water deprivation test and why.

Patients need to be weighed regularly and instructions are to stop the test if they lose ›3% of their body weight
