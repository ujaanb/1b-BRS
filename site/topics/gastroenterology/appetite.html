Overview

What is appetite?

Appetite is the natural desire or craving to eat food, often influenced by physical, psychological, and environmental factors

What is hydration?

Hydration refers to the process of providing or maintaining an adequate amount of water in the body

Recall three things that the body does when thirsty.

  • Body fluid osmolality increases
  • Blood volume is reduced
  • Blood pressure is reduced

Which of these is the most potent stimulus?

  • Plasma osmolality
  • An increase in plasma osmolality of 2-3% induces a strong desire to drink — In contrast, a decrease of 10-15% in blood volume or arterial pressure is required to produce the same response (Stage 1 Shock)

What is osmolality?

Osmolality is the number of milliosmoles of solute per kilogram of solution (mOsm/kg H2O) — If osmolality of a solute increases, its concentration has increased

Describe the difference between osmolarity and osmolality.

The main difference between osmolarity and osmolality is the unit of measurement and how the concentration is expressed: — Osmolarity is the number of milliosmoles of solute per litre of solution (mmol/L). Osmolarity is dependent on the volume of the solution, and changes with temperature. — Osmolality is the number of milliosmoles of solute per kilogram of solution (mOsm/kg H2O). Osmolality is dependent on the mass of the solvent, and is thermodynamically accurate because it's temperature-independent

Describe how osmolality is regulated.

Plasma osmolality is regulated using the hormone anti-diuretic hormone (ADH) or vasopressin

Describe the stimulus that induces AVP release.

  • AVP release is induced by an increase in plasma osmolality
  • This increase is detected by osmoreceptors — Osmoreceptors are special sensory receptors in the hypothalamus

What is the name of the receptor that ADH works on?

V2 receptor

Where is this receptor located?

Basolateral membrane of the kidney’s collecting duct cells

What is the physiological effect of arginine vasopressin (ADH)?

  • To concentrate urine — To increase reabsorption of water from the urine back into the blood
  • Anti-diuresis

What is another minor physiological effect of arginine vasopressin?

To stimulate ACTH release from the anterior pituitary

Describe the mechanism through which vasopressin concentrates urine.

  • AVP binds to the V2 receptors present on the basolateral membrane (closer to blood capillary) of the cells in the kidney’s collecting duct and this leads to a signalling cascade of reactions — cAMP and protein kinase A are involved in this cascade
  • This subsequently results in the channels aquaporin-2 (in) and aquaporin-3 (out) being opened on the surfaces of the collecting duct cells
  • Water flows out of the collecting duct of the kidney of the and into the capillaries down the concentration gradient to maintain osmolarity.
  • This ultimately means that the concentration of urine has increased, and the volume of the urine has decreased.

Where is ADH stored?

Posterior pituitary

Which sensory receptors are involved in the control of plasma osmolality?

  • Osmoreceptors
  • NOTE: Osmoreceptors are cells

Where are osmoreceptors found?

Hypothalamus

Recall the two regions of the hypothalamus where osmoreceptors are found.

  • Organum vasculosum of the lamina terminalis (OVLT)
  • Subfornical Organ (SFO)

Hypothalamus.

Describe how osmoreceptors regulate the release of ADH.

  • Osmoreceptor cells shrink when the plasma is more concentrated (increased osmolality) — water goes from cells to plasma to balance concentration
  • The proportion of cation channels on cells increases → membrane depolarisation
  • Depolarisation sends signals to the ADH producing cells to increase ADH production
  • Fluid retention is induced by ADH production as well as a physiological response of drinking
  • AND VICE VERSA

Describe how osmoreceptors are able to detect changes in blood plasma despite being located within the brain.

  • The majority of the brain is not fully exposed to blood plasma due to the presence of the highly selective blood brain barrier (BBB)
  • However, the two regions of the hypothalamus that are involved in osmoregulation (OVLT and subfornical gland) are not protected by the blood-brain barrier (BBB)
  • This means they can come into contact with the blood plasma and

can be sensitive to changes in osmolality What is the push-activation model?

  • The “push” is the low level of baseline activity which exists by osmoreceptors under physiological conditions — It leads to a steady, controlled production of ADH
  • The “activation” describes the increase in blood osmolality and osmoreceptor firing rate which leads to increased ADH production — i.e a stimulus-driven response

Push-Activation Model.

What is thirst?

Thirst is a sensation that is best described as the desire to drink

Describe an alternative way thirst is regulated other than osmolality.

  • There is obviously a delay between the consumption of water and the correction of plasma osmolality — i.e GI tract has to absorb the water, and process it appropriately to correct things
  • Hence there are sensory receptors which detect water when we are drinking to decrease thirst — This is even before sufficient water has been absorbed by the GI tract to correct plasma osmolality
  • NOTE: Relief of thirst sensation via these receptors is short lived and this mechanism also helps to prevent excessive fluid intake

Recall three areas where the receptors involved in this pathway are located.

  • Mouth
  • Pharynx
  • Oesophagus

When is thirst completely satisfied?

Thirst is only completely satisfied once plasma osmolality has decreased or blood volume or arterial pressure corrected

What is renin?

Renin is an enzyme produced by the kidneys in response to a reduction in renal blood flow (i.e low blood pressure)

What is the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS)?

The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) is a homeostatic mechanism of the body used to respond to changes in blood pressure by altering fluid characteristics within the body

Describe how the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) works.

  • Aldosterone production is regulated by renin
  • Renin is secreted by juxtaglomerular apparatus (JGA) cells when systolic blood pressure decreases
  • Renin production leads to angiotensinogen (kidneys) →angiotensin I
  • Angiotensin II is then produced from angiotensin I via ACE (angiotensin conversion enzyme) — ACE is produced in the lungs
  • Angiotensin II can then activate the enzymes: hydroxlase 21, hydroxylase 11, hydroxylase 18, side chain cleavage enzyme and 3 hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase in the adrenal gland which leads to aldosterone production from the zona glomerulosa of the adrenal cortex
  • Angiotensin II also causes vasoconstriction

Summarise how the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system is involved in blood pressure regulation.

Decrease in systolic blood pressure → Angiotensinogen production → Angiotensin I production → Angiotensin II production → Aldosterone production → Blood pressure increases

Recall four functions of angiotensin II.

  • Aldosterone production
  • Thirst
  • ADH secretion
  • Vasoconstriction, increased sympathetic activity
  • NOTE: Generally, angiotensin II works to increase fluid retention and counteract the decreased blood pressure

Describe the role of aldosterone in the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS).

  • H2O retention via Na+ and Cl- absorption as well as K+ excretion
  • This works to increase blood pressure

Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (Raas).

Examples of which drug class are ramipril and lisinopril?

  • ACE inhibitors
  • These hence work to decrease blood pressure

Describe how adipose tissue and fat mass are involved in body weight homeostasis.

  • A reduction in fat mass increases food intake and reduces energy expenditure
  • Adipose tissue expansion reduces food intake and increases energy expenditure
  • NOTE: Because of this, most adults maintain a relatively stable weight over long periods of time

Describe how weight homeostasis is managed in the overfed state.

  • Increased sympathetic nervous system activity
  • Increased energy expenditure
  • Decreased hunger/food intake
  • Weight loss

Describe how weight homeostasis is managed in the underfed state.

  • Decreased sympathetic nervous system activity
  • Decreased energy expenditure
  • Increased hunger/food intake
  • Weight regain

Weight Homeostasis.

What does the “Biggest loser” competition teach us?

  • A group of individuals were set on a diet for 30 weeks, and significant weight was lost
  • However, after going back to their regular diets, most individuals regained most of the weight they lost
  • This goes back to the idea that weight homeostasis is wired to enable us to gain some of the weight we lost back and lose some of the weight we gain — This can make it very difficult for individuals to lose weight

What is resting metabolic rate (RMR)?

The resting metabolic rate (RMR) is the amount of energy (calories) your body needs to maintain basic physiological functions at rest

Describe what is meant by the metabolic adaptation to dieting.

  • In reference to the biggest loser competition specifically, the metabolic adaptation to dieting refers to how the dietary period saw a substantial decrease in the resting metabolic rates of the participating individuals that had lost weight — i.e 600-700 calories
  • This would mean that each individual has to eat around 700 calories less to maintain their healthy weight
  • This is difficult due to the homeostatic mechanisms described above

Describe how appetite is hormonally regulated.

  • Leptin sends signals to the hypothalamus
  • Ghrelin, PYY, GLP-1 and other gut hormones also signal to the hypothalamus

What is leptin?

Leptin is a hormone produced primarily by fat cells (adipocytes) and plays a key role in regulating energy balance, appetite, and body weight.

Describe the main physiological effect of leptin.

  • Leptin acts on the hypothalamus in the brain to reduce hunger
  • When fat stores are sufficient, leptin levels rise, signalling to the brain that the body has enough energy, which reduces the desire to eat
  • NOTE: As body fat increases, leptin also increases

Describe three other physiological effects of leptin.

  • Regulates synthesis of thyroid hormones
  • Increases blood pressure
  • Activation of immune cells
  • Regulation of menstrual cycle
  • Regulates bone mass
  • Increased heart rate
  • Decreases glucose-stimulated insulin secretion

Recall three effects of leptin deficiency.

  • Infertility
  • Stunted linear growth
  • Decreased body temperature
  • Decreased energy expenditure
  • Decreased immune function

What does ghrelin do?

Also known as the “hunger hormone”, ghrelin levels increase when our stomachs are empty and when it is time to eat

What does leptin do?

Leptin is released by the subcutaneous fat to regulate (suppress) hunger and energy metabolism — It acts on the hypothalamus

What effect does peptide YY have?

  • Reduces intestinal motility, gallbladder contraction and pancreatic exocrine secretion
  • This makes sense as it is released in the distal portion of the gut - the terminal ileum, colon and rectum

What is glucagon-like-peptide (GLP-1)?

A hormone that is used to induce satiety

What is the main physiological effect of GLP-1 and how does it do this?

It induces satiety by increasing the sensitivity of the beta-cells of the pancreas to glucose

Appetite Regulation.

What does orexigenic mean?

Appetite stimulant

What does anorectic mean?

Appetite suppressive

Recall four regions of the hypothalamus involved in appetite control.

  • Arcuate nucleus
  • Lateral hypothalamus
  • Ventromedial hypothalamus
  • Paraventricular nucleus

What is the arcuate nucleus?

The arcuate nucleus is a region within the medial-basal hypothalamus involved in regulating energy balance, appetite control, hormone regulation, and metabolism

Hypothalamic Regions.

Describe how the lateral hypothalamus is involved in appetite control.

Also known as the feeding centre, the lateral hypothalamus is involved in the production of orexigenic signals

Describe how the ventromedial hypothalamus is involved in appetite control.

  • Also known as the satiety centre, the lateral hypothalamus is involved in the production of anorectic signals
  • NOTE: Lesions of the ventromedial hypothalamus can lead to obesity

Describe how the paraventricular nucleus is involved in appetite control.

The paraventricular nucleus is the area where the neurons of the arcuate nucleus terminate

Describe how the arcuate nucleus is involved in appetite control.

The arcuate nucleus produces both anorectic and orexigenic signals

Describe a physical property of the arcuate nucleus.

The arcuate nucleus has an incomplete blood brain barrier (BBB)

which allows access to peripheral hormones

This allows it to produce its anorectic or orexigenic signals

Describe the neuronal populations within the arcuate nucleus.

  • Stimulatory — Neuropeptide Y (NPY) and Agouti-related peptide (AGRP) neurons
  • Inhibitory — Pro-Opio Melanocortin (POMC) neuron
  • NOTE: These neurons are named because they produce the specific molecule they are named after. For example, the neuropeptide Y (NPY) neuron produces neuropeptide Y (NPY)

Describe how the stimulatory neuronal population of the arcuate nucleus works to regulate appetite.

  • The stimulatory neuronal population of the arcuate nucleus are the neuropeptide Y and Agouti related peptide (AGRP) neurons
  • They make peptides that stimulate food intake and also reduce melanocortin signalling via the release of AGRP

Describe how this NPY/AGRP system is regulated.

  • It contains receptors for leptin, so it can respond to changes in leptin levels — i.e increased leptin leads to downregulation of stimulatory pathway, decreased leptin leads to upregulation of stimulatory pathway
  • There are also receptors for insulin — increased insulin leads to downregulation, decreased insulin leads to upregulation

Where do NPY and AGRP act, once produced by their neurons?

They act on the paraventricular nucleus (PVN)

Describe how the inhibitory neuronal population of the arcuate nucleus works to regulate appetite.

  • The inhibitory neuronal population of the arcuate nucleus are the POMC neurons
  • They make peptides that inhibit food intake

What is the melanocortin system?

  • This is the main system within the hypothalamus involved in the downregulation of appetite — i.e inhibition
  • Melanocortins are products of the POMC gene

Recall the name of the receptor on which the products of the melanocortin system work on to induce its inhibitory effects.

MC4 receptor within the paraventricular nucleus (PVN)

Describe how the melanocortin system is involved in the regulation of appetite.

  • Pro-opio melanocortin (POMC) is the main precursor molecule produced by the POMC neurons within the arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus
  • POMC is converted using enzymes into other molecules which can act on the paraventricular nucleus (PVN) to inhibit appetite and decrease feeding

Npy/Agrp And Pomc Systems.

Recall three other ways signals are received for appetite.

  • Higher centres — i.e amygdala
  • Other parts of the hypothalamus — i.e lateral hypothalamus
  • Vagus to brainstem to hypothalamus

What is the adipostat mechanism?

The adipostat mechanism is a hypothesis presented to explain how fat can release hormones and this controls our appetite and hunger

Describe the adipostat mechanism.

  • Circulating hormones are produced by fat — i.e leptin
  • Hypothalamus senses the concentration of the hormone
  • Hypothalamus then alters neuropeptides to increase or decrease food intake

Describe how the adipostat mechanism explains the control of food intake.

It is thought that problems with the adipostat mechanism can potentially be responsible for obesity

What is the ob/ob mouse?

  • This is a mouse that lacked the gene that codes for leptin
  • This means it had no adipostat regulation of hunger that usually comes from leptin, meaning it became polyphagic and obese
  • NOTE: Another mouse (the db/db mouse) lacks the receptor for leptin and so has a similar phenotype

Describe the role of leptin.

Leptin is released to regulate (suppress) hunger and energy metabolism

Describe how leptin works to induce its effects on the body.

Leptin acts on the hypothalamus regulating appetite (intake) and thermogenesis (expenditure)

Recall the two areas of the hypothalamus that leptin acts on.

Cell receptors in the — Arcuate nucleus — Ventromedial nuclei

Where is leptin secreted from?

  • White adipose tissue
  • Gastric mucosa

Describe what is meant by white adipose tissue (WAT).

  • There are two types of adipose tissue, white adipose tissue (WAT) and brown adipose tissue (BAT)
  • White adipose tissue (WAT) stores energy in the form of triglycerides and releases it in times of energy demand
  • Brown adipose tissue (BAT) generates heat and releases it in cold environments via non-shivering thermogenesis — i.e mediated by UCP-1

When is leptin secreted?

  • Leptin levels generally reflect energy reserves — Leptin is low when body fat is low — Leptin is high when body fat is high
  • Leptin also increases post-prandially

Describe the effects of leptin.

Leptin generally decreases food intake and increases thermogenesis

What is congenital leptin deficiency?

  • This is a condition in which individuals have low to no levels of leptin from birth
  • It is very rare

Describe the effects of leptin therapy in individuals with congenital leptin deficiency.

In these individuals, leptin has been effective in reducing body weight

Describe the relationship between leptin levels and weight.

  • As people gain weight, leptin levels increase exponentially
  • As people lose weight, leptin levels fall

What is leptin resistance?

Leptin resistance is a condition where the brain doesn't respond properly to leptin, a hormone that regulates hunger and energy balance

Recall three mechanisms through which leptin resistance can arise.

  • Leptin absence/Reduced leptin expression
  • Leptin receptor fault
  • Reduced leptin sensitivity

Describe the effects of leptin resistance.

  • Leptin resistance generally leads to obesity
  • When the hormone is present but doesn’t signal effectively, this causes weight gain → obesity Is leptin an effective weight loss/control drug?
  • No.
  • This is because as individuals become obese/gain weight, they become more resistant to leptin

Why do we feel less hungry after a meal?

  • Bulk (mass) within the stomach helps us to feel fuller
  • Nutrients in circulation help us to feel less hungry
  • Hormonal signals from the gut help us to feel less hungry

Describe how gastrointestinal hormones are secreted.

Gastrointestinal hormones are secreted by enteroendocrine cells in the stomach, pancreas & small bowel

Recall two gastrointestinal hormones that regulate appetite.

  • Ghrelin
  • Peptide YY (PYY)
  • Glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1)

Describe the role of ghrelin.

To stimulate appetite — Also known as the “hunger hormone”, ghrelin levels increase

when our stomachs are empty and when it is time to eat Describe the role of peptide YY (PYY) and glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1).

To inhibit food intake

Gut Hormones.

Describe how ghrelin works to induce its effects.

Directly modulates neurons in the arcuate nucleus — Stimulates NPY/Agrp neurons — Inhibits POMC neurons

Describe the effects of ghrelin on the gut.

Ghrelin helps prepare for food intake by increasing gastric motility and acid secretion

Describe how the concentration of ghrelin fluctuates throughout the day.

  • Blood levels of ghrelin are highest before meals before rapidly falling post-prandially
  • There is also diurnal variation of ghrelin levels

Ghrelin Concentration.

Describe the effects of ghrelin on feeding in rats.

Administration of ghrelin in rats increases cumulative food intake

Ghrelin In Rats.

Describe the effects of ghrelin on feeding in humans.

Administration of ghrelin in humans also increases cumulative food intake
