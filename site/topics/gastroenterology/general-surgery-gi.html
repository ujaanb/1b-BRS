Overview

What is meant by the term acute abdomen?

  • “Acute abdomen” is an umbrella term that describes symptoms of a rapidly developing abdominal pathology — i.e the sudden onset of severe abdominal pain, often indicating a serious or life-threatening condition requiring urgent medical attention
  • It is NOT A DIAGNOSIS

What is the difference between referred and radiating pain?

  • Both referred and radiating pain are experienced in areas that differ to the source. They differ in how the pain is relayed across nerves
  • Referred pain describes pain that is caused by the merging of sensory neurons onto a common nerve (usually second-order neuron), causing the brain to misinterpret the source of the pain within the somatosensory cortex — i.e myocardial infarction (heart) → left arm pain. It is commonly

when visceral sensation presents as somatic pain.

Radiating pain happens when a nerve root or peripheral nerve is irritated or compressed, causing pain to follow the sensory distribution (dermatome) and course of that nerve — from the spine outward — i.e sciatica, compression of sciatic nerve

Sensory Nerve Course.

What causes referred pain?

Take the example of the heart and the left arm. Commonly, a myocardial infarction (heart) will present as left arm pain, even though these are two distinct sites in the body and it is the myocardial infarction that is causing the pain, with the left arm being physiologically okay ◦ i.e the pain has been referred from one area to another • With referred pain, pain from the affected area (usually an organ) merges onto a sensory neuron that carries sensation from another part of the body (second-order neuron, usually for the skin or muscle), however the brain interprets it as coming from wrong place • In the context of the left arm and the heart, the sensory nerve that carries pain from the heart merges onto that for the left arm, causing the brain to interpret the signal within the somatosensory cortex as left arm pain, rather than heart pain • This is common with visceral pain as it is uncommon and often diffuse (vague), so the brain struggles to interpret it from the correct area

Recall three other components of an acute abdomen history.

Describe the range of investigations generally done for an acute abdomen assessment.

  • Bloods: VBG (for lactate) FBC, CRP, U&Es (renal profile), LFTs + amylase

  • Imaging: Erect Chest X-Ray, Abdominal X-Ray, CTAP, CT angiogram, USS

What is the significance of lactate levels in a routine blood test?

High lactate levels are a marker of increased levels of anaerobic respiration

Describe the management of the acute abdomen.

  • ABCDE approach

  • NOTE: Most patients are managed conservatively

What is the ABCDE approach?

The ABCDE approach is a systematic, priority-based method for assessing and treating critically ill or injured patients

Describe the components of the ABCDE approach.

  • Airways — Assess the trachea’s position - should be midline
  • Breathing — Assess the lungs and the pleural spaces — Examine for asymmetry, opacities, or hyperlucency.
  • Circulation — Assess the mediastinum, heart and great vessels
  • Diaphragm — Focus on the diaphragm - right hemidiaphragm is normally higher than the left due to the liver.
  • Everything Else — Focus on bones, soft tissue, lines and tubes

What is bowel ischaemia?

Bowel ischaemia is when you have inadequate blood supply to the bowel

Recall three features within the clinical presentation of bowel ischaemia.

  • Sudden onset crampy abdominal pain
  • Severity of pain depends on the length and thickness of colon affected
  • Bloody, loose stool (currant jelly stools)
  • Fever, signs of septic shock

Describe why bleeding is commonly associated with bowel ischaemia.

  • With bowel ischaemia, there is shedding of the intestinal mucosa (inner layer)
  • This causes damage to blood vessels (including veins), which can cause blood to leak in the bowel and blood in the stool

Recall three risk factors for bowel ischaemia.

  • Age ›65 yr
  • Cardiac arrhythmias (mainly AF), atherosclerosis
  • Hypercoagulation/thrombophilia
  • Vasculitis
  • Sickle cell disease
  • Profound shock causing hypotension

Describe why atrial fibrillation is a risk factor for bowel ischaemia.

This is because atrial fibrillation causes blood flow within the arteries to not be laminar • Non-laminar blood flow increases the risk of clot formation, which

can in turn lead to bowel ischaemia Recall two types of bowel ischaemia.

  • Acute mesenteric ischaemia
  • Ischaemic colitis

What is acute mesenteric ischaemia?

This is a form of bowel ischaemia that typically affects the small bowel — The acute presentation may arise in an atherosclerotic nature, triggered by food, or a thrombotic nature where the blood vessels are clogged

What is ischaemic colitis?

This is a form of bowel ischaemia that typically affects the large bowel

Describe three differences between acute mesenteric ischaemia and ischaemic colitis.

  • Acute mesenteric ischaemia typically affects the small bowel, ischaemic colitis typically affects the large bowel
  • Acute mesenteric ischaemia is typically occlusive due to thromboemboli but ischaemic colitis is usually due to non-occlusive low flow states, or atherosclerosis
  • Acute mesenteric ischaemia typically has sudden onset (but presentation and severity varies) but ischaemic colitis is more mild and gradual (80-85% of the cases)
  • In acute mesenteric ischaemia, the abdominal pain is out of proportion of clinical signs but in ischaemic colitis, there is often moderate pain and tenderness

Recall the three investigations performed for bowel ischaemia.

  • Bloods
  • Imaging
  • Endoscopy

Describe the signs we expect to see when bloods are used in the investigation of bowel ischaemia.

  • Full Blood Count (FBC) should show neutrophilic leukocytosis
  • Venous Blood Gas should show lactic acidosis

Describe the signs we expect to see when imaging is used in the investigation of bowel ischaemia.

  • Disrupted flow
  • Vascular stenosis
  • Pneumatosis intestinalis
  • Thumbprint sign

What is pneumatosis intestinalis?

Pneumatosis intestinalis is the presence of gas within the wall of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, rather than in the lumen itself

What is thumbprint sign?

This is a sign on X-rays that represents thickening of the colonic haustral folds and are a sign of severe submucosal oedema

Thumbprint Sign.

Describe how endoscopy is used in the investigation of bowel ischaemia.

For mild or moderate cases of ischaemic colitis (oedema, cyanosis, ulceration of mucosa)

Describe the conservative management of mild to moderate cases of ischaemic colitis.

  • IV fluid resuscitation
  • Bowel rest
  • Broad-spectrum Antibiotics — colonic ischaemia can result in bacterial translocation & sepsis
  • NasoGastric (NG) tube for decompression - in concurrent ileus
  • Anticoagulation
  • Treat/manage underlying cause
  • Serial abdominal examination and repeat imaging

Recall three indications for surgical management of bowel ischaemia.

  • Small bowel ischaemia
  • Signs of peritonitis or sepsis
  • Haemodynamic instability
  • Massive bleeding
  • Fulminant (sudden) colitis with toxic megacolon

What is toxic megacolon?

  • Toxic megacolon is a complication of severe colon disease or infection
  • It results in a non-obstructive, dilated colon with systemic toxicity

Recall two surgical procedures that can be used to manage bowel ischaemia.

  • Exploratory laparotomy
  • Endovascular revascularisation

Describe how exploratory laparotomy is used in the management of bowel ischaemia.

Resection of necrotic bowel +/- open surgical embolectomy or mesenteric arterial bypass

Describe how endovascular revascularisation is used in the management of bowel ischaemia.

  • Balloon angioplasty/thrombectomy
  • Used in patients without signs of ischaemia — i.e non-acute settings
  • NOTE: Angioplasty = widening of narrowed blood vessels

What is acute appendicitis?

Acute appendicitis is sudden inflammation of the appendix

Describe the clinical presentation of acute appendicitis.

  • Initially periumbilical pain that migrates to Right Lower Quadrant (within 24hours)
  • Anorexia, nausea +/- vomiting, low grade fever, change in bowel habit

Recall five clinical signs of acute appendicitis.

  • McBurney’s point
  • Blumberg sign
  • Rovsing sign
  • Psoas sign
  • Obturator sign

What is McBurney’s sign?

This is tenderness in the Right Lower Quadrant (RLQ) (lateral 1/3 of a hypothetical line drawn from the right ASIS to the umbilicus)

What is Blumberg sign?

This is rebound tenderness especially in the Right Iliac Fossa

What is Rovsing sign?

This is Right Lower Quadrant (RLQ) pain elicited on deep palpation of the Lower Left Quadrant

What is Psoas sign?

This is Right Lower Quadrant (RLQ) pain elicited on flexion of right hip against resistance when laying supine OR extension of right hip

when laying on left side What is Obturator sign?

This is Right Lower Quadrant (RLQ) pain on passive internal rotation of the hip with hip & knee flexion

Describe the signs we expect to see when bloods are used in the investigation of acute appendicitis.

  • FBC: neutrophilic leukocytosis
  • ↑ed CRP
  • Urinalysis: possible mild pyuria/haematuria — appendix irritating bladder
  • Electrolyte imbalances in profound vomiting

Recall the gold standard of imaging for acute appendicitis in adults.

CT scans — especially if age›50

Recall the gold standard of imaging for acute appendicitis in children.

Ultrasound scans (USS)

When is an ultrasound scan used to investigate acute appendicitis?

Used in children/pregnancy/breastfeeding

When is an MRI scan used to investigate acute appendicitis?

MRI scans are used in pregnancy if ultrasound scan (USS) is inconclusive

When is diagnostic laparoscopy used to investigate acute appendicitis?

In cases of persistent pain & inconclusive imaging

What is the Alvarado score?

The Alvarado score is a scoring system used to predict the likelihood of acute appendicitis in patients presenting with abdominal pain

Describe how the Alvarado score is used in the management of acute appendicitis.

  • Points are attributed on the basis of many clinical signs. RLQ tenderness 2 Fever ( › 37.3 °C) 1 Rebound tenderness 1 Pain migration 1 Anorexia 1 Nausea+/- vomiting 1 WCC › 10.000 2 Neutrophilia (Left shift 75%) 1
  • A score ≤4 = Unlikely, 5-6 = Possible, ≥7 = Likely

What is the gold standard of management for acute appendicitis?

Surgical management - laparascopic appendicectomy

Describe the conservative management of acute appendicitis.

  • Intravenous (IV) Fluids, Analgesia, Intravenous (IV) or PO (oral) Antibiotics
  • In abscess, phlegmon or sealed perforation → Resuscitation + IV Antibiotics +/- percutaneous drainage

Describe the indications for conservative management of acute appendicitis.

(see lecture diagram / notes)

After negative imaging in selected patients with clinically uncomplicated appendicitis

In delayed presentation with abscess/phlegmon formation — in these cases, CT-guided drainage is used

What is interval appendicectomy and when should it be considered?

  • An interval appendicectomy is a delayed surgical removal of the appendix, typically performed after an initial period of successful non-operative treatment, like antibiotics
  • Rate of recurrence after conservative management of abscess/perforation is 12-24%, so interval appendicectomy should be considered following conservative management

Recall the technique used in the surgical management of acute appendicitis.

Laparoscopic appendicetomy

What is laparascopic appendicectomy?

Laparoscopic appendectomy is a surgical procedure to remove the appendix through several small incisions, rather than one large one

What is open appendicectomy?

Open appendectomy is a surgical procedure to remove the appendix

through one large incision in the right lower abdomen, rather than several smaller ones Describe three advantages of laparascopic over open appendicectomy.

  • Less pain
  • Lower incidence of surgical site infection
  • ↓ed length of hospital stay
  • Earlier return to work
  • Overall costs
  • Better quality of life scores

Describe the steps involved in laparascopic appendicectomy.

  • Trocar placement (usually 3)
  • Exploration of RIF & identification of appendix
  • Elevation of appendix + division of mesoappendix (containing artery)
  • Base secured with endoloops and appendix is divided
  • Retrieval of appendix with a plastic retrieval bag
  • Careful inspection of the rest of the pelvic organs/intestines
  • Pelvic irrigation (wash out) + Haemostasis
  • Removal of trocars + wound closure

What is bowel obstruction?

This is when there is restriction of normal passage of intestinal contents

Recall the four cardinal signs of bowel obstruction.

  • Pain
  • Abdominal distension
  • Constipation
  • Vomiting

Recall the two main groups of bowel obstruction.

  • Paralytic (Adynamic) ileus — systemic or neural dysfunction → impaired bowel function
  • Mechanical obstruction — i.e blockage of bowel by contents from inside or outside wall

Recall the four components to categorising bowel obstruction.

  • Speed of onset
  • Site
  • Nature
  • Aetiology

Describe how speed of onset is used to categorise bowel obstruction.

Acute, chronic, acute-on-chronic

Describe how site is used to categorise bowel obstruction.

  • High or low — roughly synonymous with small or large bowel obstruction respectively
  • NOTE: High obstruction more typically leads to vomiting and severe pain

Describe how nature is used to categorise bowel obstruction.

Simple vs strangulating — Simple = bowel is occluded without damage to blood supply — Strangulating = blood supply of involved segment of intestine is cut off (e.g. in strangulated hernia, volvulus, intussusception)

What is intussusception?

This is a serious condition in which part of the intestine slides into another part, much like a telescope

Intussusception.

Describe how aetiology is used to categorise bowel obstruction.

  • Causes in the lumen - faecal impaction, gallstone ‘ileus’
  • Causes in the wall - Crohn’s disease, tumours, diverticulitis of colon
  • Causes outside the wall – — Strangulated hernia (external or internal) — Volvulus — Obstruction due to adhesions or bands.

Recall five causes of small bowel obstruction.

  • Adhesions (60%) — common in those with history of previous abdominal surgery
  • Neoplasia (20%) — primary, metastatic, extraintestinal
  • Incarcerated hernia (10%) — hernia trapped within hernial sac
  • Crohn's Disease (5%)
  • Other (5%)

Bowel Adhesions.

Recall five causes of large bowel obstruction.

  • Colorectal carcinoma
  • Volvulus — sigmoid, caecal
  • Diverticulitis — inflammation, strictures
  • Faecal impaction — immobile faeces in bowels due to constipation
  • Hirschsprung disease — commonly found in infants/children

Describe the nature of the abdominal pain in small bowel obstruction.

Colicky, central

Describe the nature of the abdominal pain in large bowel obstruction.

Colicky or constant

Describe the nature of the vomiting in small bowel obstruction.

  • Early onset
  • Large amount
  • Bilious

Describe the nature of the vomiting in large bowel obstruction.

  • Late onset
  • Initially bilious
  • Progresses to faecal vomiting

Describe the nature of the absolute constipation in small bowel obstruction.

Late sign

Describe the nature of the absolute constipation in large bowel obstruction.

Early sign

Describe the nature of the abdominal distension in small bowel obstruction.

Less significant

Describe the nature of the abdominal distension in large bowel obstruction.

Early sign and significant

Describe the other signs present in small and large bowel obstruction.

  • Dehydration
  • Increased high pitched tinkling bowel sounds (early sign), or absent bowel sounds (late sign)
  • Diffuse abdominal tenderness

What should always be done in the examination of a large bowel obstruction?

Examination should always include a search for hernias & abdominal scars, including laparoscopic portholes

What is strangulation?

This is when the blood supply of an involved segment of intestine is cut off — e.g. in strangulated hernia, volvulus, intussusception

Recall three clinical features that suggest strangulation.

  • Change in character of pain from colicky to continuous
  • Tachycardia
  • Pyrexia
  • Peritonism
  • Bowel sounds absent or reduced
  • Leukocytosis
  • ↑ed C-reactive protein (CRP)

Describe the clinical significance of strangulating obstruction with peritonitis.

Strangulating obstruction with peritonitis has a mortality of up to 15%

What is a hernia?

A hernia is the abnormal exit of tissue or an organ, such as the bowel,

through the wall of the cavity in which it normally resides Recall five common sites of abdominal hernia.

  • Epigastric
  • Umbilical
  • Incisional
  • Inguinal
  • Femoral

Recall three types of hernia.

  • Neck of sac
  • Strangulated hernia
  • Richter’s hernia

What is a strangulated hernia?

This is when a hernia becomes trapped and its blood supply is cut off, potentially leading to tissue death

What is Richter’s hernia?

This is when is only a portion of the bowel's circumference protrudes through a small defect in the abdominal wall — This region can become ischaemic and perforate

Types Of Hernia.

Describe the signs we expect to see when bloods are used in the investigation of bowel obstruction.

  • WCC/CRP usually normal (if raised suspicion of strangulation/perforation)
  • U&E: electrolyte imbalance

Describe the signs we expect to see on bloods when there is vomiting and strangulation in bowel obstruction.

  • VBG if vomiting: HypoCl-,HypoK+, metabolic alkalosis
  • VBG if strangulation: Metabolic Acidosis (lactate)

Describe the findings made when imaging is used in the investigation of small and large bowel obstruction respectively.

  • Erect CXR/AXR
  • SBO: Dilated small bowel loops ›3cm proximal to the obstruction (central)
  • LBO: Dilated large bowel ›6cm (if caecum ›9cm ) predominantly peripheral
  • CT abdo/pelvis → Transition point, dilatation of proximal loops – IV +/- oral contrast if possible

Describe the findings made when an abdominal X-ray is used in the investigation of small bowel obstruction.

  • Ladder pattern of dilated loops & their central position
  • Striations that pass completely across the width of the distended loop produced by the circular mucosal folds

Describe the findings made when an abdominal X-ray is used in the investigation of large bowel obstruction.

  • Distended large bowel tends to lie peripherally
  • Show haustrations of taenia coli - do not extend across whole width of the bowel

Abdominal X-Ray.

Describe the important of CT scans when used in the investigation of bowel obstruction.

  • Can localise site of obstruction
  • Detect obstructing lesions & colonic tumours
  • May diagnose unusual hernias (e.g. obturator hernias)

Ct Scan - Bowel Obstruction.

When is supportive/conservative management of bowel obstruction suggested?

In patients with no signs of ischaemia/no signs of clinical deterioration

What is the risk of operating for bowel obstruction?

The more we operate, the more there is risk of adhesions within the abdomen

Describe the supportive management of bowel obstruction.

  • Nil By Mouth, IV peripheral access with large bore cannula → IV Fluid resuscitation
  • IV analgesia, IV antiemetics, correction of electrolyte imbalances
  • NG tube for decompression, urinary catheter for monitoring output
  • Introduce gradual food intake if abdominal pain and distension improve

Describe the conservative management of faecal impaction.

Faecal impaction → stool evacuation (manual, enemas, endoscopic)

Describe the conservative management of sigmoid volvulus.

Sigmoid volvulus → rigid sigmoidoscopic decompression

Describe the conservative management of small bowel obstruction.

Small Bowel Obstruction → oral gastrograffin (highly osmolar iodinated contrast agent) can be used to resolve adhesional small bowel obstruction

Recall three indications for surgical management of bowel obstruction.

  • Haemodynamic instability or signs of sepsis
  • Complete bowel obstruction with signs of ischaemia
  • Closed loop obstruction
  • Persistent bowel obstruction ›2 days despite conservative management

Describe the role/aims of the operation for bowel obstruction.

  • Exploratory Laparotomy/Laparoscopy
  • Restoration of intestinal transit (depending on intra-operational findings)
  • Bowel resection with primary anastomosis or temporary/permanent stoma formation
  • Endoscopic stenting

What is GI perforation?

A gastrointestinal (GI) perforation is a hole or rupture in the wall of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract

Describe the clinical presentation of GI perforation.

  • Sudden onset severe abdominal pain associated with distention
  • Diffuse abdominal guarding, rigidity, rebound tenderness
  • Pain aggravated by movement
  • Nausea, vomiting, absolute constipation
  • Fever, Tachycardia, Tachypnoea, Hypotension
  • Decreased or absent bowel sounds

Recall four common GI perforations.

  • Perforated peptic ulcer
  • Perforated diverticulum
  • Perforated appendix
  • Perforated malignancy

Describe the clinical features of a perforated peptic ulcer.

  • Sudden epigastric or diffuse pain
  • Referred shoulder pain
  • History of NSAIDs, steroids, recurrent epigastric pain

Describe the clinical features of a perforated diverticulum.

  • Lower Left Quadrant pain
  • Constipation

Describe the clinical features of a perforated appendix.

  • Migratory pain
  • Anorexia
  • Gradual worsening RLQ pain

Describe the clinical features of a perforated malignancy.

  • Change in bowel habit
  • Weight loss
  • Anorexia
  • Per Rectal Bleeding

Describe the findings made when bloods are used to investigate GI perforation.

  • FBC: neutrophilic leukocytosis
  • Possible elevation of Urea, Creatinine
  • VBG: Lactic acidosis

Describe the findings made when imaging is used to investigate GI perforation.

  • Erect CXR → subdiaphragmatic free air (pneumoperitoneum)
  • CT abdo/pelvis → Pneumoperitoneum, free GI content, localised mesenteric fat stranding — can exclude common differential diagnoses such as pancreatitis

Recall three differential diagnoses related to GI perforation.

  • Acute cholecystitis
  • Appendicitis
  • Myocardial infarction
  • Acute pancreatitis

Describe the conservative management of GI perforation.

  • Nil By Mouth & NG tube
  • IV peripheral access with large bore cannula → IV Fluid resuscitation
  • Broad spectrum Antibiotics
  • Intravenous (IV) Proton Pump Inhibitor (PPI)
  • Parenteral analgesia & antiemetics
  • Urinary catheter
