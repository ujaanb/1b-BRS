Overview

What is cancer?

A disease caused by an uncontrolled division of abnormal cells in a part of the body

What is a primary cancer?

A cancer arising directly from the cells in an organ

What is a secondary/metastatic cancer?

A cancer that has spread from another organ, directly or by other means (blood or lymph)

Describe the difference between a primary and a secondary/metastatic cancer.

  • A primary cancer arises directly from the organ in question
  • A secondary/metastatic cancer has spread from another organ of the body

Recall two types of epithelial cell cancers.

  • Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC)
  • Adenocarcinoma

What is a squamous cell carcinoma?

A cancer of the squamous epithelial cells — squamous cells are found in the oesophagus

What is an adenocarcinoma?

A cancer of the glandular epithelial cells

What are neuroendocrine cells?

Neuroendocrine cells are specialised cells that receive signals from the nervous system but, unlike neurons, they also produce and release hormones (endocrine)

Recall two types of neuroendocrine cell cancers.

  • Neuroendocrine Tumours (NETs)
  • Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumours (GISTs)

What is a neuroendocrine tumour (NET)?

A cancer of the enteroendocrine cells of the GI tract

What is a gastrointestinal stromal tumour (GIST)?

A cancer of the Interstitial cells of Cajal within the GI tract

What are the interstitial cells of Cajal?

Interstitial cells of Cajal are specialised, non-neural cells in the gastrointestinal tract that act as pacemakers — They generate electrical slow waves that drive smooth muscle contractions and coordinate gastrointestinal motility

Recall two types of connective tissue cancers.

  • Leiomyoma/leiomyosarcomas
  • Liposarcomas

What is a leiomyoma/leiomyosarcoma?

A cancer of smooth muscle

What is a liposarcoma?

A cancer of adipose (fatty) tissue

Location Of Gi Cancers.

Describe the commonness of colorectal cancer among gastrointestinal (GI) cancers.

Colorectal cancer is the most common GI cancer in Western Societies

Describe the lethalness of colorectal cancer among all cancers.

It is the third most common cause of cancer death in men & women

Recall which gender is at greater risk of developing colorectal cancer.

Men

Recall the lifetime risk for developing colorectal cancer as a man.

1 in 10

Recall the lifetime risk for developing colorectal cancer as a woman.

1 in 14

Describe the age group that is most commonly affected by colorectal cancer.

Colorectal cancer generally affects patients › 50 years — ›90% of cases

Recall the three main forms of colorectal cancer.

  • Sporadic
  • Familial
  • Hereditary syndrome

Describe what is meant by sporadic colorectal cancer.

This is when the cancer arises with absence of family history, generally within the older population, and it is an isolated lesion

Describe what is meant by familial colorectal cancer.

This refers to cases where family history of cancer is present

Describe two factors that can increase risk of developing familial colorectal cancer given that family history of cancer is present.

  • Higher risk if index case is young — ‹50 years
  • If the relative is close (1st degree)

What is the index case in terms of family history?

The index case is the first identified case of a particular condition within a family

What is a first-degree relative?

This is a relative that has an immediate relationship with the subject in question — i.e parent, sibling, child

Describe what is meant by hereditary syndrome.

This refers to cases of colorectal cancer where there is a family history of cancer, younger age of onset and specific gene defects that have been inherited

Recall two examples of hereditary colorectal cancer syndromes.

  • Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP)
  • Hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer (HNPCC or Lynch syndrome)
  • NOTE: These both have autosomal dominant inheritance patterns

What is Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP)?

Familial adenomatous polyposis is a condition that can significantly increase your risk of developing certain cancers such as colorectal cancers, gastric cancer, duodenal cancer

What is Lynch syndrome?

Lynch syndrome is an inherited condition that significantly increases the risk of developing certain cancers, particularly colorectal and endometrial cancers, often at a younger age than in the general population

Describe the difference between hereditary syndrome and familial colorectal cancer.

  • While both subtypes involve family history, hereditary colorectal cancer syndrome follows a clear Mendelian pattern, with the gene being passed down from generation to generation — It has a younger age of onset, as well as specific gene defects
  • Familial colorectal cancer is not directly caused by genetics, however it describes how family history of cancer can simply increase one’s risk of developing cancer

What is the histopathological classification of most colorectal cancers?

Adenocarcinoma

Recall three mutations/defects involved in the pathogenesis of colorectal cancer.

  • APC mutation — APC normally suppresses tumour growth
  • COX-2 overexpression — COX-2 is an enzyme that promotes inflammation and is implicated in cancers
  • K-ras mutation — oncogene
  • p53 mutation — p53 is a tumour suppressor
  • Loss of 18q — long arm of chromosome 18 is lost → increased risk of cancer

Stages Of Development For Colorectal Cancer.

Recall three groups of risk factors for colorectal cancer.

  • Past history
  • Family history
  • Diet/Environmental

Recall three past history risk factors for colorectal cancer.

  • Past history of colorectal cancer
  • Past history of adenoma
  • Past history of ulcerative colitis
  • Past history of radiotherapy

Recall two family history risk factors for colorectal cancer.

  • 1st degree relative ‹ 55 yrs
  • Relatives with identified genetic predisposition — (e.g. FAP, HNPCC, Peutz-Jegher’s syndrome)

What is Peutz-Jeghers syndrome?

Peutz-Jeghers syndrome is a condition characterised by the development of non-cancerous growths (hamartomatous polyps) in the gastrointestinal tract and dark spots (mucocutaneous pigmentation) on the skin and inside the mouth, with a significantly increased risk of developing various cancers

Recall three diet/environmental risk factors for colorectal cancer.

  • Carcinogenic foods
  • Smoking
  • Obesity
  • Socioeconomic status

Recall the factor which causes variety in the clinical presentation of colorectal cancer.

Location of the cancer

Recall the most common location for colorectal cancer.

  • 2/3 of colorectal cancers present in the descending colon and rectum
  • Of these, 1/2 present in the sigmoid colon and rectum — this is within reach of flexible sigmoidoscopy

Describe the clinical presentation of caecal and right-sided cancer.

  • Iron deficiency anaemia (most common)
  • Change of bowel habit (diarrhoea)
  • Distal ileum obstruction (late)
  • Palpable mass (late)

Describe the clinical presentation of left-sided and sigmoid cancer.

  • PR bleeding, mucus — per rectum bleeding
  • Thin stool (late)

Describe the clinical presentation of a rectal carcinoma.

  • PR bleeding, mucus
  • Tenesmus
  • Anal, perineal, sacral pain (late)
  • NOTE: Bowel obstruction is also possible in presentation of colorectal cancer

What is tenesmus?

  • Tenesmus is the persistent, often painful, feeling of needing to have a bowel movement even when your bowels are empty
  • It is often caused by the presence of a tumour within the GI tract, often imitating the effects of stool

Recall two signs of local invasion by a colorectal cancer.

  • Bladder symptoms
  • Female genital tract symptoms

Recall two signs of metastasis by a colorectal cancer.

  • Liver — hepatic pain, jaundice
  • Lung — cough
  • Regional lymph nodes
  • Peritoneum
  • Sister Marie Joseph nodule
  • NOTE: Metastases are difficult to pick up clinically because many patients will present without symptoms

What is a Sister Marie Joseph nodule?

A Sister Marie Joseph nodule is a metastatic umbilical nodule often presenting as a firm, palpable mass in the belly button, often indicating advanced metastatic cancer with poor prognosis

Sister Marie Joseph Nodule.

Describe the signs observed on examination in primary colorectal cancer.

  • Abdominal mass
  • Upon Digital Rectal Examination (DRE), most tumours are ‹12cm within the dentate line and are reached by the examining finger
  • Rigid sigmoidoscopy
  • Abdominal tenderness and distension — large bowel obstruction

What is the dentate line?

The dentate line, also known as the pectinate line, is an anatomical landmark in the anal canal, marking the boundary between the upper two-thirds and lower one-third

Dentate Line.

Recall three signs of metastasis observed on examination of primary colorectal cancer.

  • Hepatomegaly
  • Monophonic wheeze
  • Bone pain

Recall the main investigation done for primary colorectal cancer.

  • Faecal Immunochemical Test (FIT) — detects minute amounts of blood in faeces (faecal occult blood).
  • Blood tests also done
  • NOTE: The Guaiac test was also used, lecturer mentioned that it is no longer used

What is the Guaiac test?

A guaiac test is a test used to detect hidden (occult) blood in stool, potentially indicating colorectal cancer or other gastrointestinal problems

Describe the advantages and disadvantages of the Guaiac test.

  • Dietary restrictions are placed on the patient — avoid red meat, melons, horse-radish, vitamin C & NSAIDs for 3 days before test
  • Sensitivity of 40-80%; Specificity of 98% — i.e it rarely returns false positives, however likelihood is false negative is variable

Recall the blood tests done for primary colorectal cancer.

  • FBC: anaemia, haematinics – low ferritin
  • Tumour markers: Carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) which is useful for monitoring — some tumours secrete CEA
  • NOTE: Tumour markers are NOT a diagnostic tool, but they are useful for following patients up after they have tumours removed to see if the tumour has come back

Describe how colonoscopy is used in the management of primary colorectal cancer.

  • Colonoscopy can visualise lesions ‹ 5mm
  • Small polyps can be removed to reduce cancer incidence
  • Colonoscopy is usually performed under sedation

Colonoscopy.

Recall a similar alternative to colonoscopy used to treat primary colorectal cancer.

CT colonoscopy/colonography

Describe the benefits of CT colonoscopy/colonography.

(see lecture diagram / notes)

Can visualise lesions › 5mm

  • No need for sedation
  • Less invasive, better tolerated
  • It is generally good for frail patients

Describe a disadvantage of CT colonoscopy/colonography.

If lesions identified, patient still needs colonoscopy for diagnosis

Ct Colonoscopy/Colonography.

Recall the two other imaging tests used in the management of primary colorectal cancer.

  • MRI pelvis – Rectal Cancer
  • CT Chest/Abdo/Pelvis

Describe how an MRI is used in the management of primary colorectal cancer.

  • Depth of invasion, mesorectal lymph node involvement
  • No bowel prep or sedation required
  • Help choose between preoperative chemoradiotherapy or straight to surgery
  • Is the CRM threatened? — Circumferential Resection Margin, i.e is the area beyond which resection is infeasible close to being invaded?

Describe how CT scans are used in the management of primary colorectal cancer.

CT Chest/Abdo/Pelvis is used for staging prior to treatment — i.e to check there are no metastases

Describe how colorectal cancer is primarily managed.

Colon cancer is primarily managed by surgery

Describe how obstructive right-sided and transverse colon cancer is managed.

  • Resection and primary anastomosis — i.e reconnecting the separated sections of bowel
  • This is because the blood supply to the right side of the colon is good, so there is less risk of leakage from bowel — anastomotic healing requires good blood supply

Describe how obstructive left-sided colon cancer is managed.

  • Hartmann’s procedure
  • Primary anastomosis
  • Palliative stent — placing a small tube (stent) in a narrowed or blocked area of the digestive tract to relieve symptoms, used in those ineligible for surgery

What is Hartmann’s procedure?

  • Hartmann's procedure involves removing a portion of the colon (sigmoid colon and sometimes rectum) and creating a colostomy, with the remaining rectum sealed off
  • It is often used in emergencies or when primary anastomosis is not feasible

Describe why we usually avoid surgical intervention on the left side of the colon.

There is up to ~10% chance of leaks after anastomosis

Rectal Cancer.

Recall four cancers that can present in the liver.

  • Colorectal cancer — spreads to the liver
  • Gallbladder cancer — picked up earlier and more frequently due to increasing prevalence of cholecystectomy
  • Hepatocellular carcinoma
  • Cholangiocarcinoma

What is a hepatocellular carcinoma?

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common type of primary liver cancer

Describe the causes of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).

  • ~70-90% have underlying cirrhosis
  • Aflatoxin

What is aflatoxin?

  • Aflatoxins are a group of highly toxic, carcinogenic mycotoxins (mold toxins) produced by fungi that contaminate food and feed, posing health risks to humans and animals
  • The main fungi that produce aflatoxins are Aspergillus flavus and Aspergillus parasiticus

Describe the prognosis of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).

  • Median survival without resection is 4-6 months
  • 5 year survival rate is ‹5%

Describe how effective chemotherapy is against hepatocellular carcinomas (HCCs).

Systemic chemotherapy ineffective — recovery rate ‹20%

Describe the surgical treatment options for hepatocellular carcinomas (HCCs).

  • Orthotopic Liver Transplantation (OLTx)
  • Transarterial chemoembolisation (TACE)
  • Radiofrequency ablation (RFA)

What is OLTx?

Orthotopic liver transplantation (OLTx) is a surgical procedure

where a diseased liver is replaced with a healthy liver from a deceased or living donor

It works because of the aetiology of liver cancer, which is that liver cancer originates from problems with the liver

What is TACE?

Transarterial chemoembolisation (TACE) is a minimally invasive treatment for liver cancer that combines chemotherapy and embolisation (blocking blood vessels) to target and shrink or destroy tumours • NOTE: Embolisation is done to block blood flow to the tumour

Describe the effectiveness of TACE.

TACE doesn’t cure liver cancer, but it can keep people alive for longer

What is RFA?

Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) is a treatment for liver cancer that uses heat from a needle electrode to destroy cancer cells

Describe when RFA is typically used.

Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) is often used when surgery isn't an option, particularly for smaller tumours

What is the gold standard treatment for hepatocellular carcinoma?

  • The optimal treatment is surgical excision with curative intent
  • However, not everyone is in a fit state to have surgery, hence why there are so many treatment options

Describe how effective surgical resection of hepatocellular carcinomas (HCCs) are.

Surgical resection has a 5yr survival of ›30%

Recall what percentage of patients are eligible for surgery for hepatocellular carcinomas (HCCs).

~5-15% are suitable for surgery

What is gallbladder cancer?

Gallbladder cancer is a relatively uncommon cancer that begins within the cells of the gallbladder

Describe the causes of gallbladder cancer.

Aetiology unknown, although gallbladder cancer is associated with: — gallstones — porcelain gallbladder — chronic typhoid infection

What is porcelain gallbladder?

Porcelain gallbladder is the calcification (hardening) of the gallbladder wall, often appearing as a bluish, brittle consistency — Gallstones are present in ›90% of cases of porcelain gallbladder

Describe the prognosis of gallbladder cancer.

  • Median survival without resection is 5-8 months
  • 5 year survival rate is ‹5%

Describe the treatment options for gallbladder cancer.

  • Systemic chemotherapy ineffective
  • Optimal treatment is surgical excision with curative intent

What is Stage 2 gallbladder cancer?

In Stage 2 gallbladder cancer, the cancer has grown through the muscle layer of the gallbladder wall and into the connective tissue underneath, but has not spread outside the gallbladder

Recall the 5 year survival rate for someone who has had excision of Stage 2 gallbladder cancer.

64%

What is Stage 3 gallbladder cancer? Stage 3 is divided into 3A and 3B.

  • Stage 3A means the cancer has grown through the gallbladder wall but has not spread to the lymph nodes
  • Stage 3B means the cancer is within the gallbladder wall or has broken through the outer lining and spread to nearby lymph nodes

Recall the 5 year survival rate for someone who has had excision of Stage 3 gallbladder cancer.

44%

What is Stage 4 gallbladder cancer?

Stage 4 gallbladder cancer, the most advanced form, signifies the cancer has spread beyond the gallbladder, potentially to lymph nodes, nearby organs, or distant parts of the body, making it difficult to treat and with a poor prognosis

Recall the 5 year survival rate for someone who has had excision of Stage 4 gallbladder cancer.

8%

Recall what percentage of patients are eligible for surgery for gallbladder cancer.

‹15% of patients are suitable for surgery

What is cholangiocarcinoma (ChCA)?

  • Cholangiocarcinoma (ChCA) is a type of cancer that forms in the bile duct
  • NOTE: The location of these tumours often make them difficult to deal with

Describe the causes of cholangiocarcinoma (ChCA).

  • Primary sclerosing cholangitis
  • Ulcerative colitis
  • Liver fluke (clonorchis sinesis)
  • Choledochal cyst

Describe the prognosis of cholangiocarcinoma (ChCA).

  • Median survival (depends on site) without resection is ‹6 months
  • 5 year survival rate is ‹5%

Describe how effective chemotherapy is against cholangiocarcinoma (ChCA).

Systemic chemotherapy ineffective — GEMCIS - median overall survival 11.7 months*

Describe the treatment options for cholangiocarcinoma (ChCA).

Optimal treatment is surgical excision with curative intent

Recall the 5 year survival rate for someone who has had excision of cholangiocarcinoma (ChCA).

5 year survival rate is 20-40%

Recall what percentage of patients are eligible for surgery for cholangiocarcinoma (ChCA).

~20-30% of cholangiocarcinoma (ChCA) patients are suitable for surgery

What is secondary liver metastasis (CRC)?

Secondary liver metastasis (CRC) refers to cancer that has spread to the liver from the colon or rectum, rather than originating in liver

Describe the difference between synchronous and metachronous secondary liver metastasis (CRC).

  • Synchronous colorectal liver metastases (CRLM) occur when liver metastases are detected at or before the diagnosis of the primary colorectal cancer
  • Metachronous colorectal liver metastases (CRLM) occur when liver metastases from colorectal cance r are detected after the primary colorectal cancer has been treated

Recall what proportion of secondary liver metastasis is synchronous.

15-20%

Recall what proportion of secondary liver metastasis is metachronous.

25%

Describe the causes of secondary liver metastasis (CRC).

Metastasis from the colon or rectum

Describe the prognosis of secondary liver metastasis (CRC).

  • Median survival without resection is ‹1 year
  • 5 year survival is 0%

Describe how effective chemotherapy is against secondary liver metastasis (CRC).

Systemic chemotherapy improving

Improving Chemotherapy Against Secondary Liver Metastasis

Describe the treatment options for secondary liver metastasis (CRC).

Optimal treatment is surgical excision with curative intent

Recall the 5 year survival rate for someone who has had excision of secondary liver metastasis (CRC).

5 year survival rates are ~25-50%

Recall what percentage of patients are eligible for surgery for secondary liver metastasis (CRC).

~25% suitable for surgery

Surgical Resection Of Gallbladder Cancer (Gb Ca).

Describe what makes pancreatic cancer particularly dangerous.

By the time patients present to the doctor with pancreatic cancer, it has usually metastasised

Recall the most common form of pancreatic cancer.

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDA)

What is pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDA)?

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDA) is the most common type of pancreatic cancer, originating in the cells lining the pancreas's ducts, which transport digestive enzymes

Recall the two main presentations of pancreatic cancer and their prevalences.

  • Late presentation (80%)
  • Resectable disease (15-20%)

Describe the prognosis of late presenting pancreatic cancer.

  • Overall median survival is ‹6 months
  • 5-year survival rate is 0.4 - 5%

Describe the prognosis of resectable pancreatic cancer.

  • Median survival 11-20 months
  • 5-year survival 20–25%
  • Virtually all patients dead within 7 years of surgery

Describe the epidemiology of pancreatic cancer.

  • Incidence higher in Western/industrialised countries
  • Rare before 45 years, 80% occur between 60 & 80 years of age
  • Incidence & mortality roughly equivalent – UK in 2015 — 9,921 new cases of PDA — 9263 deaths from PDA
  • Projected to be 2nd commonest cause of cancer death – in USA 2030 — 48,000 deaths

Describe the lethalness of pancreatic cancer among all cancers.

4th commonest cause of cancer death

Recall which gender is affected more by pancreatic cancer.

Men › women (1.5-2:1)

why it ranks highly amongst other cancers for mortality but not so highly for incidence

Recall five risk factors for pancreatic cancer.

  • Chronic pancreatitis — → 18-fold higher risk, persistent inflammation
  • Type II diabetes mellitus — → relative risk 1.8
  • Cholelithiasis, previous gastric surgery & pernicious anaemia — WEAK
  • Diet — ↑fat & protein, ↓fruit & veg, coffee & Ethanol — WEAK
  • Occupation — insecticides, aluminium, nickel & acrylamide
  • Cigarette smoking — → causes 25-30% PDAs
  • 7-10% have a family history — Relative risk of PDA increased by: 2, 6 & 30-fold with: 1, 2 & 3 affected first degree relatives

Recall three genes associated with causing hereditary pancreatitis.

  • PRSS1 - Cationic trypsinogen
  • SPINK1 - Pancreatic secretory trypsin inhibitor
  • CFTR - CFTR protein
  • NOTE: Mutations in other genes typically tend to inhibit the action of tumour suppressors and can lead to conditions such as FAP and Peutz-Jeghers syndrome

Recall three genes associated with causing Lynch syndrome.

  • MLH1
  • MSH2
  • MSH6
  • PMS2

Recall a gene associated with causing familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP).

APC

Describe the pathogenesis of pancreatic cancer.

  • Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAs) evolve from non-invasive neoplastic precursor lesions known as Pancreatic Intraepithelial Neoplasias (PanIN)
  • PanINs are microscopic (‹5 mm diameter) & not visible by pancreatic imaging
  • They acquire clonally selected genetic and epigenetic alterations along the way

What is a neoplasm?

  • The autonomous growth of tissue that have escaped normal constraints on cell proliferation — i.e any tissue that has grown too big without external stimulation, but with cell cycle defects
  • Cancers are malignant neoplasms

Panin Progression Model, With Genetic Alterations.

Recall the most common location for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinomas (PDAs) to arise from.

At least two-thirds of PDAs arise in the head of the pancreas

Describe three features of the clinical presentation of pancreatic cancer (head of pancreas).

  • Jaundice — ›90% due to either invasion or compression of common bile duct — often painless — palpable gallbladder
  • Weight loss — anorexia — malabsorption (secondary to exocrine insufficiency) — diabetes
  • Pain (70% at the time of diagnosis) — epigastrium — radiates to back in 25%
  • 5% of patients experience an atypical attack of acute pancreatitis
  • In advanced cases, duodenal obstruction results in persistent vomiting
  • Gastrointestinal bleeding — duodenal invasion or varices secondary to portal or splenic vein occlusion

What is Courvoisier’s sign?

This is the name given to the presence of a palpable gallbladder

Describe the significance of back pain in the presentation of pancreatic cancer.

  • Back pain usually indicates posterior capsule invasion and irresectability
  • NOTE: The posterior capsule is the connective tissue layer between the pancreas and the structures closely behind it — i.e aorta, IVC, kidneys, adrenal glands

The Pancreas In The Abdomen.

Describe three features of the clinical presentation of pancreatic cancer (body/tail of pancreas)

  • Develop insidiously and are asymptomatic in early stages
  • At diagnosis they are often more advanced than lesions located in the head
  • There is marked weight loss with back pain in 60% of patients — in contrast with the head where there is back pain in ~25% of patients
  • Jaundice is uncommon
  • Vomiting sometimes occurs at a late stage from invasion of the Duodenal-jejunal flexure
  • Most unresectable at the time of diagnosis

Recall seven investigations that can be conducted for pancreatic cancer.

  • Tumour marker CA19-9
  • Ultrasonography (ultrasound)
  • Dual-phase CT — it accurately predicts resectability in 80–90% of cases
  • MRI
  • Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) — specialised MRI
  • Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) — combination of endoscopies and X-rays
  • Endoscopic ultrasound
  • Laparoscopy & laparoscopic ultrasound
  • PET

What is the best imaging technique for visualising pancreatic cancer?

CT scans

Describe how tumour marker CA19-9 is used to investigate pancreatic cancer.

  • Tumour marker CA19-9 is a helpful, but not diagnostic tool for pancreatic cancer
  • It is falsely elevated in pancreatitis, hepatic dysfunction and obstructive jaundice — concentrations › 200 U/ml confer 90% sensitivity — concentrations in the thousands associated with high specificity
  • Similar to CEA, CA19-9 is a useful follow-up marker, not diagnostic tool

Describe what is meant by the specificity and the sensitivity of a medical test.

  • Sensitivity tells you how good the test is at detecting people who actually have the disease — a very sensitive test will pick up nearly all positive cases
  • Specificity tells you how good the test is at identifying people who do not have the disease — a very specific test will reject nearly all negative cases, as it is specifically reaching a certain quota

Describe how ultrasound sonography is used to investigate pancreatic cancer.

Can identify pancreatic tumours, dilated bile ducts and liver metastases

Describe how dual-phase CT is used to investigate pancreatic cancer.

  • Contiguous organ invasion
  • Vascular invasion (coeliac axis & SMA)
  • Distant metastases
  • NOTE: Dual-phase CT accurately predicts resectability in 80–90% of cases

Dual-Phase Ct Of Pancreas.

Describe how an MRI is used in the management of pancreatic cancer.

MRI imaging detects and predicts resectability with accuracies similar to CT

Describe how MRCP is used in the management of pancreatic cancer.

MRCP provides ductal images without complications of ERCP

Describe how ERCP is used in the management of pancreatic cancer.

ERCP — confirms the typical ‘double duct’ sign — aspiration/brushing of the bile-duct system for biopsies — ERCP is also used as a therapeutic modality → biliary stenting to relieve jaundice

What is the double duct sign?

  • The double duct sign refers to simultaneous dilation of the common bile duct and pancreatic duct
  • It indicates an obstruction in the distal bile duct and pancreatic duct, frequently caused by pancreatic or periampullary tumors

Biliary Tree.

Describe how endoscopic ultrasound is used in the management of pancreatic cancer.

  • Highly sensitive in the detection of small tumours
  • Assessing vascular invasion
  • Fine Needle Aspiration (FNA)

Describe how laparoscopy and laparoscopic ultrasound is used in the management of pancreatic cancer.

It can detect radiologically occult metastatic lesions of liver & peritoneal cavity

Describe how PET scans are used in the management of pancreatic cancer.

PET mainly used for demonstrating occult metastases

Tail Of Pancreas Resection.

Where do neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) arise from?

  • Neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) arise from the gastroenteropancreatic (GEP) tract or bronchopulmonary system
  • They are regarded as common entity as arise from secretory cells of the neuroendocrine system

Describe the cause of neuroendocrine tumours (NETs).

  • Sporadic tumours in 75%
  • Associated with a genetic syndrome in 25%. This is called Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia Type 1 (MEN1) and includes: — Parathyroid tumours — Pancreatic tumours — Pituitary tumours

Describe the clinical presentation of neuroendocrine tumours (NETs).

  • Most NETs are asymptomatic and are incidental findings
  • ‹10% of NETs produce symptoms

Describe how neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) behave.

Secretion of hormones & their metabolites in 40% of cases — serotonin, tachykinins (substance P) & other vasoactive peptides

Describe the sequelae of neuroendocrine tumours (NETs).

Neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) can result in a variety of debilitating effects, namely carcinoid syndrome

Recall three symptoms of carcinoid syndrome.

  • Vasodilatation
  • Bronchoconstriction
  • ↑ed intestinal motility → diarrhoea
  • Endocardial fibrosis (PR & TR) — pulmonary/tricuspid regurgitation

Recall the four major gastrointestinal sites for neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) to develop.

  • Pancreas
  • Pancreatic/duodenal
  • Entire GIT
  • Midgut
  • Hindgut

Recall the two neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) of the pancreas.

  • Insulinoma
  • Glucagonoma

Describe the clinical features of an insulinoma.

  • Hypoglycaemia
  • Whipple’s triad

Recall the components of Whipple’s triad.

  • Hypoglycemic symptoms
  • Low blood glucose level
  • Relief of symptoms after blood glucose is raised

Describe the clinical features of a glucagonoma.

  • Diabetes mellitus
  • Necrolytic migratory erythema — a red, blistering rash that spreads across the skin

Recall a neuroendocrine tumour (NETs) of the pancreas/duodenum.

Gastrinoma

Describe the clinical features of a gastrinoma.

Zollinger-Ellison syndrome

What is Zollinger-Ellison syndrome?

  • This is a tumour of the gastric (G) cells causing an excess of gastric acid
  • It can lead to increased activity within the GI tract, while causing intestinal ulceration

How can Zollinger Ellison syndrome be treated?

  • Proton pump inhibitor to reduce acid secretion
  • Somatostatin analogues that work to inhibit function

Recall two neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) of the entire GIT.

  • VIPoma
  • Somatostatinoma

Describe the clinical features of a VIPoma.

  • Verner-Morrison syndrome
  • Watery diarrhoea

What effect does vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) have?

It causes relaxation of the gut smooth muscle

What is Verner-Morrison syndrome?

  • Verner-Morrison syndrome (VIPoma syndrome) is a rare condition that secretes vasoactive intestinal polypeptide (VIP), leading to symptoms like watery diarrhoea, hypokalemia, and achlorhydria
  • NOTE: Achlorhydria is a condition where the stomach doesn't produce hydrochloric acid (HCl)
