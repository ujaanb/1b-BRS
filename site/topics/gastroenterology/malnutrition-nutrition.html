Overview

What is malnutrition?

A state in which deficiency, excess or imbalance, of energy, protein or other nutrients, results in a measurable adverse effect on body composition, function and clinical outcome

Which gender is at a higher risk of malnutrition?

Women

Which age groups are a higher risk of malnutrition on wards?

  • Very young people
  • Very old people

Which groups are a higher risk of malnutrition on wards?

  • Care of the elderly patients
  • Oncology patients

Recall the prevalence of malnutrition in acute admissions.

  • 1 in 3 patients are malnourished at acute admission
  • NOTE: Hospitalisation exacerbates risk of malnutrition

Describe what happens to the weight of the majority of patients after a hospital stay.

~70% of patients have lost weight at discharge – mainly muscle

Describe the financial cost of malnutrition.

Malnutrition uses ~15% of total public expenditure on health and social care

Recall the three main mechanisms of malnutrition in hospital.

  • Reduced intake
  • Maldigestion/Malabsorption
  • Altered metabolism

Recall five causes of reduced intake.

  • Contraindicated
  • Disease related anorexia
  • Taste changes
  • Nil by mouth
  • Food options
  • Depression
  • Inactivity
  • Oral health
  • Fatigue

Recall three causes of maldigestion/malabsorption.

  • Function — of the gut
  • Length
  • Losses — by the gut
  • Drug-nutrient interactions

Describe how altered metabolism post-injury can lead to malnutrition.

  • Post injury there are three phases which require altered nutrition goals due to altered metabolism — Ebb phase - Body activity decreases, shock — Early flow phase - Body metabolism increases significantly, more calories required to meet metabolic needs — Late flow phase - Body metabolism still increased, done to build back muscle
  • Failure to adjust to the hypercalorific requirements of the early and late flow phases can easily lead to malnutrition

Altered Metabolism Post-Trauma.

Describe how the clinical impact of malnutrition was discovered.

  • In 1936, Surgeon Hiram Studley was performing surgeries for perforated duodenal ulcers
  • Postoperative mortality was 10x greater in those who had lost ≥20% bodyweight preoperatively, compared with those who had lost less

Recall how many deaths were caused by malnutrition in England & Wales, 2022.

77 deaths

Recall how many deaths had malnutrition as a contributory factor in England & Wales, 2022.

436 hospital deaths

Describe the direct impact of malnutrition on clinical outcomes. Increased

  • Mortality
  • Septic and post surgical complications
  • Length of hospital-stay
  • Pressure sores
  • Re-admissions
  • Dependency Decreased
  • Wound healing
  • Response to treatment
  • Rehabilitation potential
  • Quality of life

Survival In Well-Nourished And Malnourished Patients.

Describe the steps involved in the diagnosis of malnutrition.

  • Screening — Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool (MUST)
  • Assessment — A systematic process of collecting and interpreting information to determine the nature and cause of the nutrient imbalance
  • Diagnose

Describe how the Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool (MUST) is used.

  • The Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool (MUST) is a screening tool used to identify risk of a patient falling into malnutrition — It is not an assessment or diagnosis
  • Guidelines are to use within 6 hours of admission and weekly thereafter

What is meant by assessment in the context of diagnosing malnutrition?

  • A systematic process of collecting and interpreting information to determine the nature and cause of the nutrient imbalance
  • It is usually conducted by a dietitian

Describe the components involved in the dietitian’s assessment of a patient for malnutrition.

  • Anthropometry — measurements of body
  • Body composition
  • Function
  • Biochemistry
  • Clinical
  • Dietary
  • Social
  • Physical
  • Requirements

Recall the three criteria used to categorise a patient as malnourished.

  • BMI ‹ 18.5 kg/m2 OR
  • Unintentional weight loss ›10 % past 3 - 6 months OR
  • BMI ‹ 20 kg/m2 AND unintentional weight loss › 5 % past 3 – 6 months

Describe the two criteria used to categorise a patient as being at risk of malnutrition.

  • Have eaten little or nothing for › 5 days and / or are likely to eat little or nothing for the next 5 days or longer OR
  • Have a poor absorptive capacity, and / or have high nutrient losses and/or have increased nutritional needs from causes such as catabolism

Algorithm For The Treatment Of Malnutrition.

What is oral nutrition support?

Oral nutritional support is a method used to provide extra nutrients via the oral route to people who can't get enough from their diet

Recall three ways to provide oral nutrition support.

  • Fortification of meals and snacks — i.e with relevant nutrients
  • Altered meal patterns
  • Practical support
  • Oral nutritional supplements (ONS)
  • Tailored dietary counselling

Describe when a patient is considered for oral nutrition support.

Oral nutrition support is considered for any patient with inadequate food and fluid intake to meet requirements, unless they cannot swallow safely, have inadequate gastrointestinal function or if no benefit is anticipated

What is artificial nutrition support?

  • Artificial nutrition support is when nutrition and hydration are provided to people who are unable to eat normally
  • It includes enteral and parenteral nutrition

What is enteral nutrition?

Enteral nutrition refers to a method of providing nutrition by delivering liquid food directly into a person's gastrointestinal (GI) tract

through a tube

(see lecture diagram / notes)

Which is superior, enteral or parenteral nutrition?

  • Enteral nutrition (EN) is superior to parenteral nutrition (PN)
  • This is because use of the GI tract is beneficial for immune function and general clinical outcomes, and not using the GI tract will lead to its atrophy

Describe the aim when using parenteral nutrition.

Where parenteral nutrition is used, the aim is to return to enteral → oral feeding as soon as clinically possible

Describe the different types of enteral feeding.

  • Naso-gastric tube (NGT)
  • Naso-duodenal (NDT) / naso-jejunal tube (NJT)

Nasogastric Tube.

Describe how enteral feeding is provided to patients who need it long-term.

  • For patients who require long term enteral feeding (› 3 months), gastrostomy / jejunostomy is required
  • NOTE: A nasogastric (NG) tube is not used long-term because prolonged use can lead to complications like nasal irritation, pressure sores in the nose, stomach irritation, increased risk of infection, and aspiration pneumonia

What is a gastrostomy/jejunostomy?

  • This is a surgical procedure where a percutaneous incision is created into the stomach or the jejunum of the small intestine
  • It is different to a regular nasogastric tube (NGT)/naso-duodenal tube (NDT) because it requires surgical intervention and doesn’t go

through the nose

(see lecture diagram / notes)

Gastrostomy (2).

Describe how enteral nutrition can be provided in patients that can receive gastric feeding.

Naso-gastric tube (NGT)

Describe when we may choose a naso-jejunal tube (NJT) over a naso- gastric tube (NGT).

  • A naso-jejunal tube (NJT) may be chosen over a naso-gastric tube (NGT) in cases where there is gastric outlet obstruction, preventing proper absorption of nutrients from food
  • The NJT will help to bypass this obstruction and negate its adverse effects

Examples Of Enteral Access.

What is a gastric outlet obstruction?

A gastric outlet obstruction is a blockage that prevents food from passing from the stomach to the small intestine

Describe the nutritional feeds of enteral feeding.

  • Renal
  • Low sodium
  • Respiratory
  • Immune
  • Elemental
  • Peptide
  • High energy
  • High protein

What are the complications associated with enteral feeding?

Tube being placed in the wrong place — i.e into lungs/stomach

What is parenteral nutrition?

Parenteral nutrition is the delivery of nutrients, electrolytes and fluid directly into venous blood

Describe two indications for the administration of parenteral nutrition.

  • An inadequate or unsafe oral and/or enteral nutritional intake
  • A non-functioning, inaccessible or perforated gastrointestinal (GI) tract
  • NOTE: Parenteral nutrition is a bit of a high-risk procedure, so it is usually ordered and reviewed by a multidisciplinary team (MDT), before being administered

Describe the composition of parenteral nutrition.

Ready made “scratch” bags containing fluid and electrolytes

Describe how we provide access for parenteral nutrition to be administered.

Central venous catheter (CVC): tip at superior vena cava and right atrium — Different central venous catheters (CVCs) can be used for short/long term use

Parenteral Nutrition.

What are the complications associated with parenteral nutrition?

  • Impaired blood glucose control (hyperglycaemia)
  • Delivering nutrients to the wrong locations
  • Hypertriglyceridaemia
  • Increased risk of gut atrophy — dependent on length of use

Does nutrition support benefit malnourished patients?

  • Yes.
  • Clinical trials have shown that clinical outcomes are significantly worsened in malnourished patients

What is albumin?

The most abundant (55%) protein in the plasma used to transport lipids, hormones and ions as well as maintaining osmotic pressure of the plasma

Where is albumin synthesised?

In the liver

Describe the impact of hypoalbuminaemia.

Hypoalbuminaemia has a poor prognosis — It can lead to oedema (reduced plasma oncotic pressure) and weakness

What is meant when albumin is described as a negative acute phase protein?

This means that plasma albumin levels decrease in the presence of inflammation

What is the acute phase response?

The acute phase response is a complex set of physiological changes that occur in response to infection, inflammation, or tissue injury

Describe how the acute phase response alters levels of albumin.

  • Inflammatory stimulus → activation of monocytes & macrophages → release cytokines.
  • Cytokines act on liver to stimulate synthesis of some proteins e.g. c- reactive protein (CRP), whilst down-regulating production of others, such as albumin Is albumin a valid marker of malnutrition in the acute hospital setting?
  • Albumin is a not a valid marker of nutritional status in the acute setting

Molecular Analysis During Acute Phase Response.

What is refeeding syndrome (RFS)?

A group of biochemical shifts and clinical symptoms that can occur in the malnourished or starved individual upon the reintroduction of oral, enteral or parenteral nutrition — It's caused by shifts in electrolytes and fluids

Describe the consequences of refeeding syndrome (RFS).

  • Arrhythmia, tachycardia, CHF → Cardiac arrest, sudden death
  • Respiratory depression
  • Encephalopathy, coma, seizures, rhabdomyolysis
  • Wernicke’s encephalopathy

Describe the pathogenesis of refeeding syndrome (RFS).

In the state of starvation/malnourishment, the body switches from glucose metabolism being the primary source of energy provision to fat and muscle metabolism

When feeding is reintroduced, insulin levels surge rapidly and this has adverse effects on the body

  • These include increased cellular glucose uptake, increased cellular thiamine consumption, increased cellular K+, Mg2+ and PO4 2- uptake
  • This combination of symptoms leads to what is more commonly referred to as refeeding syndrome (RFS)

Recall the five main effects of the rapid insulin surge in refeeding syndrome (RFS).

  • Increased cellular glucose uptake → hypoglycaemia
  • Increased cellular thiamine consumption
  • Increased cellular K+
  • Increased cellular Mg2+
  • Increased cellular PO4 2- uptake
  • Salt and water retention → oedema

Explain the thiamine deficiency seen in refeeding syndrome (RFS).

  • Thiamine is rapidly used in glucose metabolism
  • This is because enzymes such as Pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH), used to convert pyruvate → acetyl CoA are vital in the metabolism of glucose

Explain the hypokalaemia seen in refeeding syndrome.

  • Potassium levels decrease in response to insulin
  • This is because insulin increases the activity of the Na+/K+ ATPase pump
