Overview

What is the upper gastrointestinal tract?

  • The upper gastrointestinal (GI) tract refers to the portion of the digestive system that includes the organs involved in the early stages of digestion
  • It comprises the mouth, pharynx, oesophagus, stomach and the duodenum
  • It is approximately 45cm in length Between which spinal vertebrae does the oesophagus run between?
  • C5 and T10

What are the oesophageal sphincters?

  • These are the muscles that guard the opening and closing of the oesophageal tract
  • The upper oesophageal sphincter lies between the pharynx and the top of the oesophagus
  • The lower oesophageal sphincter lies between the stomach and the bottom of the oesophagus

Describe the structural differences between the upper and lower oesophageal sphincters/regions.

  • Upper oesophageal sphincter has striated (skeletal) muscle cells that are more voluntary
  • Middle third is striated and smooth muscle
  • Lower oesophageal sphincter has smooth muscle cells that are involuntary

What is the main function of the oesophagus?

To act as a conduit for food, drink and swallowed secretions between the pharynx and the stomach

What type of epithelium is in the oesophagus?

Non-keratinising stratified squamous epithelium

What is meant by the oesophageal epithelium being a “wear and tear” lining?

It means it can be damaged upon extremes of temperature and texture

What role do the muscles of the oesophagus play?

  • Peristalsis
  • Sequential contractions of the circular and longitudinal muscles of the oesophagus move food downwards to the stomach, independent of gravity

Upper Gi Tract.

Describe the relationship between the oesophagus and the trachea.

  • The oesophagus lies posterior to the trachea
  • NOTE: The oesophagus is a fairly posterior located organ, as it sits just in front of the erector spinae muscles

Oesophagus And Trachea.

Describe the relationship between the oesophagus and the aorta.

The aorta lies posterior to the oesophagus and slightly to the left for most of its course

Describe the relationship between the oesophagus and the diaphragm.

The oesophagus runs through the diaphragm at the oesophageal hiatus (T10 level)

Structures Relating To The Upper Gi Tract.

What is the lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS)?

  • The lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS) is a ring of smooth muscle located at the junction between the oesophagus and the stomach
  • It lies in the distal 3-4 cm of the oesophagus and is within the abdomen

Recall two structures related to the lower oesophageal sphincter.

  • Phrenoesophageal ligament
  • Left crus and right crus

Crura Of Diaphragm.

What is the phrenoesophageal ligament?

  • The phrenoesophageal ligament is a thin, elastic structure that plays a crucial role in anchoring the oesophagus to the diaphragm
  • It plays a role in generating the angle of His which is between the oesophagus and the stomach

Diaphragm.

What is the angle of His?

  • The angle of His is the acute angle formed between the oesophagus and the fundus of the stomach at the gastroesophageal junction
  • It is an important anatomical feature that contributes to the prevention of gastroesophageal reflux
  • NOTE: The angle of His is not fully developed in children, hence why reflux is more common in children

Angle Of His.

Is the upper oesophageal sphincter open or closed at rest and why?

  • Closed
  • We do not want things entering the oesophagus unless we want to.

Recall the four phases of swallowing.

  • Stage 0 - Oral phase
  • Stage 1 - Pharyngeal phase
  • Stage 2 - Upper oesophageal phase
  • Stage 3 - Lower oesophageal phase

What happens in the oral phase?

Chewing and saliva prepare the bolus of food

What happens in the pharyngeal phase?

  • The pharyngeal musculature guides the bolus into the oesophagus
  • Both oesophageal sphincters open

What happens in the upper oesophageal phase?

  • Upper sphincter closes after swallowing
  • Superior circular muscle rings contract and inferior rings dilate. — To allow movement of food down the oesophageal tract
  • There is sequential contraction of longitudinal muscles to move the bolus down

What happens in the lower oesophageal phase?

Lower sphincter closes after food passes through it into the stomach

Regions Of Upper Gi Tract.

What is manometry?

  • Manometry is a diagnostic test used to measure and evaluate the pressure, movement, and functionality of muscles and sphincters in the body
  • It can specifically help us to measure oesophageal motility

Recall the pressure within the oesophagus when contracting.

~40 mmHg

Recall the resting pressure of the lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS).

~20 mmHg

What happens to the pressure of the lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS) during swallowing?

↓‹5 mmHg during receptive relaxation

When does the lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS) contract and relax?

  • The lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS) is typically contracted at rest to maintain a high-pressure zone that will prevent the reflux of contents from the stomach
  • It relaxes during swallowing and reduces its pressure to allow the bolus to pass through to the stomach
  • NOTE: A sphincter is a ring-shaped muscle

Describe how the action of the lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS) is mediated.

  • The relaxation of the lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS) is mediated by inhibitory non-cholinergic non-adrenergic (NCNA) neurons of the myenteric (Auerbach’s) plexus — it is NCNA because the neurons rely on molecules such as vasoactive intestinal peptide and nitric oxide rather than acetylcholine/norepinephrine
  • The absence of this plexus leads to incomplete relaxation (relaxation = opening) of the LOS during swallowing

Neuronal Control Of Lower Oesophageal Sphincter (Los).

What is stricture?

Stricture in a medical context refers to abnormal narrowing or restrictions placed on a structure by any means

Describe the significance of stricture absence in the oesophagus.

Absence of oesophageal stricture means there is no abnormal narrowing or restriction of the oesophagus, which is a perfectly physiological scenario

Recall two disorders of the oesophagus that have absence of a stricture.

  • Abnormal contraction of the oesophagus — Hypermotility — Hypomotility — Disordered coordination
  • Failure of protective mechanisms for reflux (i.e sphincters, angle of His) — GastroOesophageal Reflux Disease

What is GastroOesophageal Reflux Disease (GORD)?

GastroOesophageal Reflux Disease (GORD) is a chronic digestive condition where stomach acid or bile irritates the lining of the oesophagus

What is dysphagia?

Dysphagia is difficulty in swallowing

What is the cricopharyngeal sphincter?

The cricopharyngeal sphincter is an alternative name for the upper oesophageal sphincter (UOS)

Describe the descriptions of dysphagia that can aid our diagnoses.

  • Localisation — is it in the throat (UOS) or in the chest (LOS)?
  • Caused by solids or fluids?
  • Intermittent or progressive?
  • Precise or vague in appreciation? — i.e can patient locate it well?

What is odynophagia?

Odynophagia is pain on swallowing

What is regurgitation?

Regurgitation refers to return of oesophageal contents above an obstruction

What is reflux?

Reflux is passive return of gastroduodenal contents to the oesophagus (silent reflux) or even mouth

Describe the difference between regurgitation and reflux.

  • Regurgitation refers to backflow of oesophageal contents past a barrier/obstruction — i.e to the mouth
  • Reflux refers to backflow of gastroduodenal contents to the mouth

What is achalasia?

  • Achalasia is a rare, chronic disorder of the oesophagus, where the lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS) fails to relax properly and the oesophagus does not contract effectively
  • It is characterised by hypermotility of the oesophagus

Describe the cause of achalasia.

  • Achalasia is due to the loss of ganglion cells in Auerbach’s myenteric plexus in the LOS wall
  • This leads to decreased activity of inhibitory NCNA neurones which prevents the LOS from relaxing normally during swallowing
  • NOTE: Inhibitory NCNA neurones usually cause relaxation of the LOS

which enables swallowing Recall the two main types of achalasia.

  • Primary
  • Secondary

Describe the difference between primary and secondary achalasia.

  • Primary achalasia is an idiopathic disorder — i.e its exact cause is unknown
  • Secondary achalasia is directly caused by an underlying disease or external factors

Recall three diseases that can cause secondary achalasia.

  • Chagas’ Disease — parasitic disease caused by Trypanosoma cruzi, develops over time so take travel history
  • Protozoa infection
  • Amyloid/Sarcoma/Eosinophilic Oesophagitis

Recall a clinical sign of achalasia.

  • “Bird-beak” oesophagus
  • This is caused by an enlarged oesophageal body in comparison to a narrowed (contracted) oesophageal sphincter region

Achalasia Pathophysiology (Proposed).

Describe how achalasia affects the resting pressure of the lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS).

Due to the lack of inhibitory NCNA neuronal control, the lower oesophageal sphincter is less relaxed at rest, leading to a higher resting LOS pressure

Describe the physiological pressure changes that occur within the lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS) during swallowing.

  • At rest, the lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS) maintains a pressure of 10–30 mmHg which is higher than the surrounding oesophagus and stomach — This is to maintain the barrier between the oesophagus and the stomach, and prevent reflux. — NOTE: A higher pressure from the oesophagus/stomach is required to overcome the barrier
  • During swallowing, the lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS) relaxes and its pressure drops below the pressure of the surrounding oesophagus/stomach — This is to remove the barrier between the oesophagus and the stomach and allow the passage of its contents

Describe the effect of increased lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS) pressure on the upper GI tract.

  • Increased lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS) pressure leads to weak and late relaxation of the LOS during swallowing
  • The pressure in the LOS is higher than the stomach during swallowing which is not normal, and so swallowed food collects in the oesophagus, instead of its normal passage into the stomach
  • This causes dilation of the oesophagus (due to increased pressure caused by food) and propagation of peristaltic waves cease

Describe how hypermotility comes across in achalasia.

Achalasia is characterised by increased lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS) pressure • The oesophagus may attempt to overcome the obstruction at the LOS through increased muscular activity, particularly in the early stages of the disease • This results in an absence in normal peristalsis which becomes replaced by a series of uncoordinated, spasm-like contractions within the oesophagus

Achalasia.

Describe the prognosis of achalasia.

  • Achalasia has an insidious course
  • Without treatment, it can lead to progressive oesophageal dilatation

Describe how achalasia alters one’s risk of oesophageal cancer.

The risk of oesophageal cancer in individuals with achalasia increases 28-fold — The annual incidence of achalasia is only 0.34%

Recall a treatment option for achalasia.

Pneumatic dilatation

What is pneumatic dilatation (PD)?

Pneumatic dilatation (PD) is a treatment option for achalasia which involves the circumferential stretching and in some cases, tearing of LOS muscle fibres — This is to reduce the high pressure found within it

Describe the efficacy of pneumatic dilatation.

71 - 90% of patients respond initially but many patients subsequently relapse

Pneumatic Dilatation (Pd).

Recall two surgical treatments for achalasia.

  • Heller’s Myotomy
  • Dor fundoplication

What is Heller’s Myotomy?

  • A Heller myotomy is a surgical procedure that cuts the muscles of the lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS) to treat achalasia
  • NOTE: Myotomy is the process of cutting the serosa and muscularis layers of an organ to relieve abnormal muscle contractions or obstructions HELLER’S MYOTOMY.

Recall a technique that can be paired with Heller’s Myotomy.

Dor fundoplication

What is Dor fundoplication and when is it used?

  • Dor fundoplication is when the anterior fundus is folded over the oesophagus and sutured to the right side of myotomy
  • It is done in combination with Heller’s myotomy to protect against perforation and slightly re-establish the reflux mechanism — i.e to create a slight constriction and allow for slight reflux prevention

Recall two risks for oesophageal surgery.

  • Oesophageal & gastric perforation (10 – 16%)
  • Division of vagus nerve – rare
  • Splenic injury – 1 – 5%

Vagus Nerve And The Oesophagus.

What is scleroderma?

Scleroderma (systemic sclerosis, SSc), is a chronic autoimmune disease characterised by the hardening and thickening of the skin and connective tissues

Describe how scleroderma affects the upper gastrointestinal tract.

  • Scleroderma is an autoimmune condition that also causes neuronal defects
  • Due to the hypomotility induced by neuronal defects, there is atrophy of the smooth muscle in the oesophagus
  • Peristalsis in the distal portion of the oesophagus ultimately ceases altogether

Describe how scleroderma affects the lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS).

Scleroderma also leads to a decreased resting lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS) pressure — i.e the LOS becomes weakened

Describe a secondary GI complication of scleroderma.

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GORD) develops — this is often associated with CREST syndrome

What is CREST syndrome?

  • CREST syndrome is a form of limited cutaneous scleroderma that is characterised by the combination of a set of symptoms
  • These include: — Calcinosis — Raynaud’s Phenomenon — Esophageal Dysmotility — Sclerodactyly — Telangiectasia

Calcinosis.

Describe the treatment/prognosis for scleroderma.

  • Exclude organic obstruction — i.e obstruction of organs
  • Improve force of peristalsis with prokinetics (cisapride)
  • Once peristaltic failure occurs → usually irreversible

What is Corkscrew oesophagus?

Corkscrew oesophagus is a pathology characterised by completely uncoordinated peristaltic movements within the oesophagus

Describe how Corkscrew oesophagus affects individuals.

  • Uncoordinated contractions lead to dysphagia and chest pain
  • Pressures of up to 400-500 mmHg are often generated within the oesophagus

Describe the treatment for Corkscrew oesophagus.

May respond to forceful pneumatic dilatation (PD) of cardia — Results not as predictable as achalasia

Corkscrew Oesophagus.

Describe the anatomical perforations (points of constriction) of the oesophagus.

There are three areas of anatomical constriction — Cricopharyngeal constriction — i.e UOS — Aortic and bronchial constriction — Diaphragmatic and sphincter constriction

Anatomical Constrictions Of Oesophagus.

Recall three causes of pathological oesophageal narrowing.

  • Cancer
  • Foreign body
  • Physiological dysfunction

What is oesophageal perforation?

This is a tear or hole in the oesophagus

What is iatrogenic oesophageal perforation?

This is oesophageal perforation caused by medical examination or treatment

Recall a procedure that can cause iatrogenic oesophageal perforation.

Oesophago-Gastro-Duodenoscopy (OGD)

Where is iatrogenic oesophageal perforation most common?

Iatrogenic oesophageal perforation is most common in the presence of diverticula or cancer

Recall the incidence of iatrogenic oesophageal perforations associated with OGD.

OGD = 0.03%

Recall the incidence of iatrogenic oesophageal perforations associated with stricture dilatation.

Stricture dilatation = 0.1-2%

Recall the incidence of iatrogenic oesophageal perforations associated with sclerotherapy.

Sclerotherapy = 1-5%

Recall the incidence of iatrogenic oesophageal perforations associated with achalasia dilatation.

Achalasia dilatation = 2-6%

What is Boerhaave’s oesophageal perforation?

Boerhaave’s oesophageal perforation is a spontaneous rupture of the oesophagus, characterised by a sudden increase in intra- oesophageal pressure

Describe the incidence of Boerhaave’s oesophageal perforation.

3.1 per 1,000,000 — rare

Recall a symptom of Boerhaave’s oesophageal perforation.

Vomiting against a closed glottis

Which part of the oesophagus is most commonly affected in Boerhaave’s oesophageal perforation?

Left posterolateral aspect of the distal oesophagus is perforated (narrowed)

Glottis.

Recall four causes of foreign body oesophageal perforation.

  • Disk batteries growing problem — cause electrical burns if embeds in mucosa — i.e in children
  • Magnets
  • Sharp objects
  • Dishwasher tablets
  • Acid/Alkali
  • NOTE: Unfortunately, these are often associated with suicide attempts

Recall two ways trauma can cause oesophageal perforation.

  • Neck — penetrating force
  • Thorax — blunt force

Recall three clinical features of oesophageal perforation caused by trauma.

  • Dysphagia
  • Blood in saliva
  • Haematemesis — vomiting blood
  • Surgical emphysema — the presence of air or gas within the subcutaneous tissues, the layer under the skin. it happens when air escapes from somewhere else in the body

Describe the presentation of oesophageal perforation.

  • Pain (95%)
  • Fever (80%)
  • Dysphagia (70 %)
  • Emphysema (35%)

Describe the investigations done for oesophageal perforation.

  • CXR
  • CT
  • Swallow (gastrograffin)
  • OesophagoGastroDuodenoscopy (OGD)

Describe how much of an emergency oesophageal perforation is.

  • Oesophageal perforation is a surgical emergency and should be treated seriously
  • There is 2x increased mortality if there is a 24 hour delay in diagnosis

Describe the initial management of oesophageal perforation.

  • NBM — nil by mouth
  • IV fluids
  • Broad spectrum antibiotics & antifungals
  • ITU/HDU level care
  • Bloods (including Group&Screen to assess transfusion eligibility )
  • Tertiary referral centre

What is the default management of oesophageal perforation?

Operative management

Describe how oesophageal perforation can be conservatively managed.

Conservative management of oesophageal perforation is performed with a covered metal stent

Describe how oesophageal perforation can be operatively managed.

  • Operative management of oesophageal perforation involves routine repair of the perforation or oesophagectomy (definitive solution) if necessary
  • NOTE: Operative management should be default

What is oesophagectomy?

An oesophagectomy is a surgical procedure to remove part or all of the oesophagus

Primary Repair Vs. Oesophagectomy.

What is sporadic reflux?

  • Sporadic reflux refers to occasional episodes of gastroesophageal reflux that occur infrequently and do not cause major chronic problems, as seen in GORD
  • NOTE: Sporadic reflux is normal

Recall three causes of sporadic reflux.

  • Pressure on full stomach
  • Swallowing
  • Transient sphincter opening

Recall three ways how lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS) pressure is increased.

  • Acetylcholine
  • Beta-adrenergic agonists
  • Protein rich food
  • High intra-abdominal pressure

Recall three ways how lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS) pressure is decreased.

  • Fats
  • Smoking
  • Acidic gastric juice
  • Chocolate
  • Hormones
  • Dopamine

Describe the role of the lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS) in preventing reflux.

  • Lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS) is usually closed as a barrier against reflux of harmful gastric juice (pepsin & HCl)
  • NOTE: High LOS pressure inhibits reflux, low LOS pressure causes reflux

Recall three protective mechanisms of the stomach against reflux after it has occurred.

  • Volume clearance of stomach contents - oesophageal peristalsis reflex
  • pH clearance - saliva (which is fairly neutral) — i.e neutralisation
  • Epithelium - barrier properties

What is a hernia?

A hernia is a condition in which an organ, tissue, or part of the body protrudes through a weak spot or opening in the muscle or connective tissue that normally contains it

What is a sliding hiatus hernia?

  • This describes a complete anatomical disruption of the lower part of the oesophagus
  • This leads to a removal of the protective mechanism which is usually provided by anatomical constriction in this area, allowing the contents of the stomach to go back up into the herniated oesophagus
  • NOTE: This is the most common oesophageal hernia and many that have it may even be asymptomatic

Sliding Hiatus Hernia.

What is a rolling/paraoesophageal hiatus hernia?

  • This is a more complex and serious type of hernia where a part of the stomach and/or other abdominal organs herniate upwards through the oesophageal hiatus
  • This usually occurs because the chest typically generates negative pressures, whereas the abdomen generates positive pressures
  • Also, disruption of the phrenoesophageal ligament’s integrity can facilitate herniation

Rolling/Paraoesophageal Hiatus Hernia.

Recall three investigations made to detect GORD.

  • OGD — To exclude cancer — Oesophagitis, peptic stricture & Barretts oesophagus confirm ∆
  • Oesophageal manometry
  • 24-hr oesophageal pH recording

Recall two recommendations made for patients with GORD.

  • Lifestyle changes — weight loss, smoking, EtOH
  • PPIs

Recall the physiological pH of the oesophagus.

pH 4.0 - 7.0

Recall two surgical treatments for GORD.

  • Dilatation peptic strictures
  • Laparoscopic Nissen’s fundoplication

Fundoplication.

Describe three functions of the stomach.

  • Breaks food into smaller particles — HCl & pepsin
  • Holds food, releasing it in controlled, steady rate into duodenum
  • Kills parasites & certain bacteria

Describe the regions of the stomach from proximal to distal.

  • Cardia
  • Fundus
  • Body
