Overview

Recall the three functions of the spine.

  • Locomotor
  • Bony armour
  • Neurological

Describe the locomotor function of the spine.

The spine is capable of being both rigid & mobile

Describe the bony armour function of the spine.

Protects the spinal cord

Describe the neurological function of the spine.

Neurological spinal cord transmission of signals between brain & periphery

What is kyphosis?

Kyphosis is excessive outward (posterior) curvature of the spine, causing hunching of the back

What is lordosis?

Lordosis is excessive inward (anterior) curvature of the spine

Describe the curvature of the spine from cervical to lumbar.

There is lordosis in the cervical spine which changes to a kyphosis (outward curvature) through the majority of the thoracic spine before a lordosis which is seen in the lumbar spine

Spinal Column (2).

Recall the two main musculoskeletal structures.

  • Spinal column
  • Muscles

Recall the three major components of the spinal column.

  • Vertebrae
  • Intervertebral discs
  • Facet joints

Recall how many cervical vertebra there are.

7

Recall how many thoracic vertebra there are.

12

Recall how many lumbar vertebra there are.

5

Describe the role of the intervertebral discs.

These are shock absorbers and they allow for segmentation & multi- directional movement

Describe the role of the facet joints.

These are small synovial joints at posterior spinal column linking each vertebra

Facet Joints.

Describe the role of muscles in relation to the spinal column.

To move the spine

Describe the role of the spinal cord.

The spinal cord is involved in the transmission of signals to/from brain

Describe the course of the spinal cord.

The spinal cord runs from the base of the brainstem and ends at L2 vertebra

Describe the relationship between the nerve roots and the spinal cord.

  • Nerve roots exit the spinal cord bilaterally
  • NOTE: A nerve root is the initial segment of a nerve leaving the central nervous system, specifically the spinal cord

What is the cauda equina, and why does it exist?

  • The cauda equina describes a group of nerves which curve their way down the spinal column to reach their respective intervertebral foramina
  • It arises due to the spinal cord being significantly shorter than the spinal column and these nerves (specifically those near the bottom of the spinal cord) having to travel long distances to reach the foramina
  • This leads to the accumulation of these nerves into a tail-looking structure (hence the name “equina”, horse, horse tail)

Describe the clinical importance of knowing where the spinal cord ends.

This is because lumbar puncture is performed at L3/4 space to avoid the spinal cord

Describe the three main movements of the spine.

  • Flexion/Extension
  • Lateral flexion (side bend)
  • Rotation (twist)

Movements Of The Spine.

Recall the prevalence of back pain.

Very common: ›50% of people will experience an episode

Describe the general prognosis of back pain.

  • Acute back pain is usually self-limiting — i.e resolves on its own
  • Most patients are better in a few days, 96% are better in six weeks
  • Chronic back pain (›12 weeks duration) is also common — due to sedentary lifestyle

Describe the importance of diagnosis when back pain is presented.

There is a need to distinguish mechanical back pain from serious pathology

What is mechanical back pain?

Mechanical back pain is the most common type of back pain, arising from issues within the spine, its joints, or surrounding soft tissues

Describe the clinical presentation of mechanical back pain.

  • Reproduced or worse with movement
  • Better or not present at rest

Recall three causes of mechanical back pain.

  • Muscular tension — e.g. chronic poor posture, weak muscles
  • Acute muscle sprain/spasm
  • Degenerative disc disease
  • Osteoarthritis of facet joints

Recall a common symptom that may accompany mechanical back pain.

Sciatica

What is sciatica?

Sciatica describes pain radiating down one leg

Describe the typical cause of sciatica.

Sciatica typically due to a intervertebral disc herniation (“slipped disc”) contacting the exiting lumbar nerve root

Sciatica.

Recall the two regions within an intervertebral disc.

  • Annulus fibrosus
  • Nucleus pulposus

Describe how the two regions of the intervertebral disc are involved to cause sciatica.

A tear in the annulus fibrosus can cause the jelly-like material within the nucleus pulposus to leak out and compress the spinal nerve roots

Describe what determines the location of pain experienced in sciatica.

Location of the pain is determined by the level of the herniated disc

Intervertebral Disc.

Recall four serious causes of back pain.

  • Tumour — metastatic cancer or myeloma
  • Infection — Discitis — Vertebral osteomyelitis — Paraspinal abcess — Microbiology : Staphylococcus, streptococcus, tuberculosis (TB)
  • Inflammatory spondyloarthropathy — ankylosing spondylitis, psoriatic arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)-associated — NOTE: spondylo = vertebra, spine
  • Fracture (traumatic or atraumatic)
  • Large disc prolapse causing neurological compromise

Describe how a tumour can cause back pain.

Spinal tumours, both benign and malignant, can cause back pain by pressing on the spinal cord or nerve roots

What is spondyloarthropathy?

Spondyloarthropathy describes a group of inflammatory rheumatic diseases that primarily affect the spine and joints, often causing pain, stiffness, and inflammation

Describe how a fracture can cause back pain.

Nerve irritation or compression

Recall two sources of back pain that has been referred from other sources.

  • Pancreas
  • Kidneys
  • Aortic aneurysm

Describe how we can identify serious causes of back pain.

Serious causes of back pain can be identified using the “red flag” symptoms

Recall five “red flag” symptoms for serious causes of back pain.

  • Pain at night or increased pain when supine — pressure is taken off the back when supine, so this indicates alternative causes
  • Constant or progressive pain
  • Thoracic pain
  • Weight loss
  • Previous malignancy
  • Fever/night sweats
  • Immunosuppressed
  • Bladder or bowel disturbance (Sphincter dysfunction)
  • Leg weakness or sensory loss
  • Age ‹20 or ›55 yrs

What is cauda equina syndrome (CES)?

Cauda equina syndrome (CES) is a condition that occurs when the nerves at the bottom of the spinal cord are compressed

Cauda Equina Syndrome (Ces).

Describe the clinical urgency of cauda equina syndrome.

Cauda equina is a neurosurgical emergency

Describe the consequence of untreated cauda equina syndrome.

If untreated, cauda equina syndrome may lead to permanent lower limb paralysis and incontinence

Recall five symptoms/signs of cauda equina syndrome.

  • Saddle anaesthesia
  • Bladder/bowel incontinence
  • Loss of anal tone on PR
  • Radicular leg pain
  • Ankle jerks may be absent — controlled by S1 nerve

Describe the investigation done to investigate cauda equina syndrome.

Urgent MRI of the lumbar spine

Describe the causes of cauda equina syndrome.

  • Large disc herniation
  • Bony metastases
  • Myeloma
  • Tuberculosis
  • Paraspinal abscess

Describe the treatment for cauda equina syndrome.

Treatment of cauda equina is conducted according to cause: — may require urgent surgery

Cauda Equina Syndrome.

Describe the components of history-taking for cauda equina syndrome.

  • Site & pattern
  • Character — e.g. aching, throbbing, burning, electricity
  • Onset
  • Prolonged morning stiffness
  • Exacerbating/relieving factors
  • Effect of movement vs. inactivity
  • Radiation — e.g. sciatica
  • Buttock pain
  • Leg weakness
  • Sensory loss/paraesthesia
  • Lower limb claudication

What is claudication?

Claudication is a medical term usually referring to impairment in walking, or pain, discomfort, numbness, or tiredness in the legs that occurs during walking or standing and is relieved by rest

Conus Medullaris.

Describe the components of an examination of the spine following back pain presentation.

  • Look
  • Feel
  • Move
  • Straight leg raise (SLR)
  • Lower limb neurological exam
  • General exam — signs of malignancy, AAA

Examination Of The Spine.

Describe how back pain is investigated in the absence of red flag symptoms.

In the absence of red flags, investigation usually not required

Describe the directions given to GPs for presented back pain in the absence of red flag symptoms.

  • Do not routinely arrange a spinal X-ray or other imaging to diagnose non-specific lower back pain in primary care
  • Arrange review if symptoms persist or worsen after 3–4 weeks and reassess for an underlying cause

Describe the management of back pain in the absence of red flag symptoms.

  • Time — i.e give the patient time to recover
  • Analgesia (NSAIDs e.g. ibuprofen, paracetamol, codeine)
  • AVOID bed rest — i.e keep moving
  • Physiotherapy — Soft tissue work — Corrective exercises — esp. core such as side plank and bird dog

Bird Dog.

Recall five investigations done for back pain without red flags.

  • Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR)
  • C-reactive protein (CRP)
  • Full blood count (FBC)
  • Alkaline phosphatase (ALP)
  • Calcium
  • PSA (prostate specific antigen)
  • NOTE: Most of these are non-specific, however provide clues that may point us in the right diagnostic direction

Describe the significance of erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) levels in back pain.

↑ in myeloma, chronic inflammation, TB

Describe the significance of C-reactive protein (CRP) levels in back pain.

↑ in infection or inflammation

Describe the significance of full blood count (FBC) measurements in back pain.

  • Anaemia in myeloma, chronic disease
  • ↑ WCC in infection

Describe the significance of alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels in back pain.

  • ↑ with bony metastases (mets)
  • NOTE: ALP is naturally found in the bone, liver and other tissues, so increased activity in the bone caused by bony metastases can increase ALP levels

Describe the significance of calcium levels in back pain.

May be ↑ in myeloma, bony metastases (mets)

Describe the significance of prostate specific antigen (PSA) levels in back pain.

Prostate Specific Antigen (PSA) is indicative of prostate cancer, and it is one of the cancers with a tendency to cause bony metastases

What are bony metastases?

Bony metastases are when cancer cells from a primary tumour spread to the bones

Recall the three imaging techniques that can be used to investigate back pain.

  • X-rays
  • CT scans
  • MRI scans

Describe the pros and cons of X-rays when used to investigate back pain. Pros

  • Cheap, widely available Cons
  • Poor sensitivity
  • Radiation

Describe the pros and cons of CT scans when used to investigate back pain. Pros

  • Good for bony pathology Cons
  • Larger radiation dose (than X-ray and MRI)

Describe the pros and cons of MRI scans when used to investigate back pain. Pros

  • Best visualisation of soft tissue structures like tendons and ligaments
  • Best for spinal imaging: can see spinal cord and exiting nerve roots Cons
  • Expensive and time-consuming Of these, which is considered the gold-standard for imaging for back pain?
  • MRI scans

Describe the demographic most commonly affected by herniated discs.

Most prevalent in individuals aged 30-50

Describe the prognosis of herniated discs.

  • Good outlook
  • Normally spontaneous improvement, although typically slower than for lower back pain alone

Describe the treatment options for herniated discs.

  • Conservative as for lower back pain without sciatica — Analgesia, especially NSAIDs — Physiotherapy to improve core strength and treat associated muscle spasm
  • Nerve root injection (local anaesthetic and glucocorticoid)
  • Surgery if neurological compromise or symptoms persist
  • NOTE: Trials of surgery vs conservative recommendations show no long term advance for surgery

Spontaneous Resolution Of Prolapsed Lumbar Disc.

What is inflammatory spondyloarthritis?

This is a group of immune-mediated inflammatory diseases that primarily affect the spine and joints, causing pain, stiffness, and potentially leading to spinal fusion

What is meant by inflammatory spondyloarthritis being sero-negative?

  • We don't find the rheumatoid factor autoantibodies or the CCP autoantibodies that we see in rheumatoid arthritis
  • NOTE: spondylo = vertebra, spine

Recall three diseases of the inflammatory spondyloarthritis family.

  • Ankylosing spondylitis (AS)
  • Psoriatic arthritis
  • Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)

Describe how inflammatory spondyloarthritis affects the body.

  • Primarily inflammation of the spine (spondylitis) and sacro-iliac joints (sacro-iliitis)
  • Peripheral joints, esp. tendon insertions (entheses), can also be affected

Recall four extra-articular manifestations of inflammatory spondyloarthritis.

  • A nterior uveitis (iritis)
  • A pical lung fibrosis
  • A ortitis → aortic regurgitation
  • A myloidosis – due to chronically serum amyloid A (SAA) depositing in organs

What is anterior uveitis (iritis)?

Uveitis is inflammation of the uvea → ocular inflammation

Anterior Uveitis.

What is amyloidosis?

Amyloidosis is a rare disease that occurs when a protein called amyloid builds up in organs

Psoriasis.

What is ankylosing spondylitis?

Ankylosing spondylitis is the major inflammatory spondyloarthritis disease characterised by long-term inflammation of the spine

Describe a major clinical manifestation of ankylosing spondylitis.

Loss of spinal movements

Loss Of Spinal Movements.

What are entheses?

Entheses are the sites where tendons, ligaments, and joint capsules attach to bone, acting as crucial interfaces for transmitting forces from muscles to bone

Entheses.

Describe the characteristic feature of ankylosing spondylitis.

Ankylosing spondylitis is characterised by enthesitis

What is enthesitis?

Inflammation of the entheses - sites where tendon and ligaments join to bone

Describe the cause of ankylosing spondylitis.

  • Large genetic component
  • Many genetic variants associated with the disease ( polygenic )

Recall the gene that is the strongest risk factor for ankylosing spondylitis.

  • HLA-B27 is the strongest genetic risk factor — HLA-B27 +ve in 90% of AS patients versus ~10% in general population
  • It is used as a diagnostic biomarker but HLA-B27 +ve alone does not equal ankylosing spondylitis

Background On Hla.

Describe the role of cytokines in the pathogenesis of ankylosing spondylitis.

Cytokines play important roles in pathogenesis

Recall three cytokines that play an important role in the pathogenesis of ankylosing spondylitis.

  • Tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNF-alpha)
  • Interleukin-17 (IL-17)
  • Interleukin-23 (IL23)

Describe how the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is implicated in the pathogenesis of ankylosing spondylitis.

Aberrant peptide processing pathways (aminopeptidases) in the endoplasmic reticulum is known to contribute to the pathogenesis of ankylosing spondylitis

Recall a characteristic sign of ankylosing spondylitis on imaging.

Radiographic sacro-iliitis

Radiographic Sacro-Iliitis (Mri) (2).

Describe the prognosis of untreated ankylosing spondylitis.

  • Spinal enthesitis
  • Bridging syndesmophytes — (new bone growth between adjacent vertebra)
