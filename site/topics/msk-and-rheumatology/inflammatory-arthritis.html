Overview

What is arthritis?

  • A disease that causes inflammation and swelling of the joints
  • It can be very painful for sufferers, and quality of life may be severely impacted

What are the two main groups of arthritis?

  • Degenerative (osteoarthritis)
  • Inflammatory

What is the major pathological change that occurs in osteoarthritis?

  • Loss of articular cartilage
  • Bony remodelling also occurs

Articular Cartilage.

Recall three clinical signs of inflammatory joint disease.

  • Red
  • Hot/warm
  • Swelling/fluid

Recall the three main groups of inflammatory joint arthritis.

  • Infection (septic, tuberculosis)
  • Crystal arthritis (gout, pseudogout)
  • Immune-mediated arthritis (i.e rheumatoid, reactive)
  • NOTE: Infection and crystal arthritis are secondary inflammation to a noxious insult, but autoimmune arthritis is primary inflammation

Which of these are sterile/non-sterile?

  • Infection (NON-STERILE)
  • Crystal arthritis (STERILE)
  • Immune-mediated arthritis (STERILE)

What usually causes septic arthritis of the joints?

Bacterial infection at the joints which has spread via the bloodstream

What are the two types of crystal arthritis?

  • Gout
  • Pseudogout

Describe whether inflammation is present in osteoarthritis, immune- mediated arthritis, crystal arthritis and septic arthritis.

  • Osteoarthritis - None or little
  • Immune-mediated arthritis - Yes (autoimmune)
  • Crystal arthritis - Yes (secondary to crystals)
  • Infection - Yes

Describe the speed of onset in osteoarthritis, immune-mediated arthritis, crystal arthritis and septic arthritis.

  • Osteoarthritis - Slow
  • Immune-mediated arthritis - Subacute
  • Crystal arthritis - Rapid
  • Infection - Rapid

Describe the findings made on synovial fluid analysis in osteoarthritis, immune-mediated arthritis, crystal arthritis and septic arthritis.

  • Osteoarthritis - Sterile, no inflammatory cells present
  • Immune-mediated arthritis - Inflammatory cells, sterile
  • Crystal arthritis - Inflammatory cells, sterile, crystals
  • Infection - Inflammatory cells, bacteria
  • NOTE: Inflammatory cells = neutrophils, lymphocytes, and monocytes/macrophages

Describe the CRP levels in osteoarthritis, immune-mediated arthritis, crystal arthritis and septic arthritis.

  • Osteoarthritis - Normal
  • Immune-mediated arthritis - High
  • Crystal arthritis - High or Very high
  • Infection - Very high

Describe the white cell count (WCC) in osteoarthritis, immune-mediated arthritis, crystal arthritis and septic arthritis.

  • Osteoarthritis - Normal
  • Immune-mediated arthritis - Normal
  • Crystal arthritis - Normal
  • Infection - High

Describe a characteristic feature of septic arthritis.

Acute hot, swollen joint = SEPTIC ARTHRITIS until proven otherwise

Recall the primary investigation undertaken for septic arthritis.

  • Joint aspiration
  • Send fluid for Gram stain and culture

Describe the management of septic arthritis.

Joint washout (lavage) & intravenous (IV) antibiotics

Recall the three main immune-mediated inflammatory joint diseases.

  • Rheumatoid arthritis
  • Psoriatic/reactive arthritis (seronegative inflammatory arthritis)
  • Systemic lupus erythematous (SLE)

What is rheumatoid arthritis?

Rheumatoid arthritis is a chronic autoimmune disease that causes inflammation of the joints, leading to pain, swelling, and stiffness

What is the primary site of pathology for rheumatoid arthritis?

Synovium (synovial membrane) of the joints

What is synovitis?

Inflammation of the synovial membrane

Recall the three locations where the synovium is found and commonly affected in rheumatoid arthritis.

  • Synovial (diarthrodial) joints — i.e proximal interphalangeal joints (PIPJs)
  • Tenosynovium — synovium that protects and is wrapped around tendons
  • Bursa

What is tenosynovium?

The tenosynovium is the thin, fluid-filled sheath that surrounds and protects tendons, allowing them to glide smoothly through the body

What is a bursa?

  • A fluid-filled sac that acts as a cushion and lubricant, reducing friction between bones, tendons, muscles, and skin around joints
  • It is lined by a synovial membrane BURSA.

Recall the prevalence of rheumatoid arthritis.

  • Common: prevalence ~1%
  • NOTE: RA is the most common autoimmune disease

Recall which gender is more affected by rheumatoid arthritis.

Sex bias Females : Male ~2:1 — Typically, the female sex is affected more by inflammatory conditions

Recall the age of onset of rheumatoid arthritis.

Usually 30-50s

Describe the key features of rheumatoid arthritis.

  • Chronic arthritis
  • Polyarthritis
  • Pain, swelling and early morning stiffness in and around joints
  • May lead to joint damage and destruction - ‘joint erosions’ on radiographs
  • Gets better with joint use

Describe how rheumatoid arthritis could be identified clinically.

Auto-antibodies usually detected in blood

Describe the aetiology of rheumatoid arthritis.

Rheumatoid arthritis can have both genetic and environmental elements to it, which both contribute to its onset — This is the truth for most diseases

Describe how twin studies can be used to help identify genetic and environmental influences on phenotypes. Assess monozygotic and dizygotic twins

  • Monozygotic (“identical”) twins: have the same DNA
  • Dizygotic (“non-identical”) twins: share 50% DNA (like a typical sibling) Look at concordance of a trait in monozygotic vs dizygotic twins
  • Look at pairs of twins — Ask: if one twin has the disease, how often does the other?
  • If the concordance rate for monozygotic twins › dizygotic twins, this indicates a genetic component
  • NOTE: If a disease was purely genetic, the concordance rate would be 100% for monozygotic twins

Describe the concordance seen in rheumatoid arthritis and its significance.

  • RA concordance in twins:
  • Monozygotic ~15%
  • Dizygotic ~4%
  • This means there is an element of genetics in RA, but ultimately a mixture of genetic and environmental factors lead to its aetiology

Recall three environmental causes of rheumatoid arthritis.

  • Smoking
  • Microbiome — Porphyromonas gingivalis (present in gums)
  • Poor oral health

Acpa Recap.

Recall the two types of “auto-antibodies” found in those with rheumatoid arthritis.

  • Rheumatoid factor
  • Antibodies to citrullinated protein antigens (ACPA)

Describe the action of rheumatoid factor.

Rheumatoid factor is a type of IgM, but can bind to the Fc region of IgG antibodies — i.e IgM anti-IgG antibodies

Describe the importance of antibodies to citrullinated protein antigens (ACPAs) to rheumatoid arthritis diagnosis.

  • Anti-CCP antibodies are more specific for rheumatoid arthritis than rheumatoid factor
  • It can also be an indicator of more serious disease

Describe the action of antibodies to citrullinated protein antigens.

  • Citrullination is a post-transcriptional modification made to proteins that is mediated by enzymes
  • Antibodies to citrullinated protein antigens (ACPAs) work by binding onto these enzymes and preventing citrullination

Describe how ACPAs can have adverse effects throughout the body.

  • Smoking → citrullination of proteins in lung epithelium
  • P. gingivalis can also cause citrullination
  • ACPAs bind to these citrullinated proteins and induce an inflammatory reaction at the site in question

Recall the strongest genetic risk factor for rheumatoid arthritis.

  • HLA-DR gene — this gene codes for MHC II molecules
  • This is like HLA-B27, implicated in ankylosing spondylitis

Describe how HLA-DR interferes with the phenotypic expression of rheumatoid arthritis.

  • On the HLA-DRbeta chain, there is a specific sequence of amino acids 70-74 called the ‘shared epitope’
  • Smoking alongside this shared epitope synergistically increase risk of rheumatoid arthritis (RA)

What is an epitope?

The region on an antigen where a B or T cell receptor binds to

What are genome-wide association studies (GWAS)?

  • It is a hypothesis-free study used to identify as many SNPs as possible within a wider section of the genome
  • i.e there is no hypothesis (X causes Y), but we are simply trying to find all the different SNPs in the genome which may suggest causation
  • NOTE: SNP = mutation

Describe the findings that have been made from GWAS on rheumatoid arthritis.

  • There are ~100 other genetic loci that contribute to RA risk (polygenic) — E.g. PTPN22, IL6R
  • Effect of a risk allele at any given locus typically modest
  • Cumulative genetic burden rather than any one variant determines risk

Gwas - Rheumatoid Arthritis.

Describe the implications of HLA class in different autoimmune conditions. (ADDITIONAL)

  • MHC class 1 (coded by HLA A, B and C genes) is expressed on all cells — Affected cells present antigen in association with HLA MHC class 1 to CD8 (‘killer’) T cells
  • MHC class 2 (coded by HLA D) is only expressed on professional antigen presenting cells (APCs), including dendritic cells, macrophages, B cells — APCs present antigen in association with MHC class 2 to CD4 (‘helper’) T cells — CD4 T cells then provide help to B cells → antibody production
  • HLA class 1 association with a disease (eg HLA-B27 allele in Ankylosing spondylitis) implicates CD8 T cells in pathogenesis, and so no antibodies are produced — this is a seronegative autoimmune disorder
  • HLA class 2 association with a disease ( HLA-DR4 in RA) implicates CD4 T cells and B cells → antibodies
  • This fits with autoantibodies (made by B cells) in RA but not in Ankylosing Spondylitis — AS = HLA B27, RA = HLA DR

Is rheumatoid arthritis typically symmetrical or asymmetrical?

Symmetrical

Is rheumatoid arthritis typically a mono or polyarthritis?

  • Polyarthritis — Affects multiple joints
  • This distinguishes it from osteoarthritis which is typically a monoarthritis

Recall five of the commonest affected joints in rheumatoid arthritis.

  • Metacarpophalangeal joints (MCPJs)
  • Proximal interphalangeal joints (PIPJs)
  • Wrists
  • Knees
  • Ankles
  • Metatarsophalangeal joints (MTPJs)

  • PIPJs, MCPJs, wrist, DIPJs spared Osteoarthritis
  • Pain worse with activity
  • DIPJs, PIPJs, thumb CMCJ, MCPJs spared

Recall three extra-articular features of rheumatoid arthritis. Systemic inflammation

  • Fatigue (v common)
  • Fever
  • Weight loss Organ-specific
  • Subcutaneous nodules
  • Lung disease – nodules, interstitial lung disease (ILD)/ fibrosis, pleuritis
  • Ocular inflammation e.g. episcleritis
  • Vasculitis
  • Neuropathies
  • Felty’s syndrome – triad of splenomegaly, leukopenia and rheumatoid arthritis
  • Amyloidosis

Extra-Articular Features Of Rheumatoid Arthritis.

Describe how subcutaneous nodules in rheumatoid arthritis present.

  • Central area of fibrinoid necrosis surrounded by histiocytes (immune cell in tissue) and peripheral layer of connective tissue
  • Occur in ~30% of patients
  • Associated with — Severe disease — Extra-articular manifestations — Rheumatoid factor

Rheumatoid Arthritis Subcutaneous Nodules.

Describe the histiopathological changes that occur to the synovial membrane in rheumatoid arthritis.

  • In a normal individual, the synovium is 1-3 cells thick with few cells such as type A, type B syniviocytes and Type I Collagen
  • In rheumatoid arthritis, inflammation of the synovium occurs and the synovium becomes a pannus (proliferated mass of tissue)

Explain the cause of these histiopathological changes observed in rheumatoid arthritis.

  • In rheumatoid arthritis, inflammation of the synovium occurs when TNF-a and other infiltrates get to action — The synovium becomes a pannus (proliferated mass of tissue)
  • This inflammation is mediated by an imbalance of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines — Neovascularisation, lymphangiogenesis and inflammatory cells such as activated B T cells and macrophages, plasma cells and mast cells are all responsible for the synovium’s inflammation and progression to pannus

Recall three cellular/molecular players in the pathogenesis of rheumatoid arthritis.

  • Autoreactive B cells — ( treatment: rituximab )
  • Autoreactive T cells — ( treatment: abatacept )
  • Cytokines — TNF-alpha — Interleukin-6 — Interleukin-1

Recall the most dominant pro-inflammatory cytokine in rheumatoid arthritis.

TNF-alpha

Describe three major effects of TNF-alpha in rheumatoid arthritis.

  • Inflammatory cell recruitment, angiogenesis, lymphangiogenesis -› Pannus formation
  • Matrix metalloproteases (MMPs) -› Cartilage loss
  • Osteoclast activation -› Bone loss (erosions, osteopenia)

Describe the role of TNF-alpha in the pathogenesis of rheumatoid arthritis.

  • It is the major inflammatory cytokine involved in the pathogenesis of rheumatoid arthritis
  • Its action is detrimental in rheumatoid arthritis and leads to the inflammation seen in the synovium of these joints
  • Therapeutic treatment can be centred around TNF-a inhibition

Recall three blood tests done for rheumatoid arthritis.

  • ↑ ESR
  • ↑ CRP
  • sometimes normocytic anaemia, ↑platelet (PLT)
  • Autoantibodies* — Rheumatoid factor (RF) = antibodies that bind IgG — Anti-CCP antibodies

Which is more specific for rheumatoid arthritis, rheumatoid factor or anti- CCP antibodies?

  • Anti-CCP antibodies (ACPAs) — CCP antibodies are the most specific for rheumatoid arthritis and associated with more aggressive/erosive disease
  • NOTE: Rheumatoid Factor (RF) can be positive in other autoimmune and infective conditions, and in individuals without disease. Therefore, RF positive in the absence of clinical features does not necessarily indicate rheumatoid arthritis

Recall the treatment goal for rheumatoid arthritis.

To prevent joint damage

Describe when autoantibodies appear in rheumatoid arthritis.

  • Rheumatoid Factor and Anti-Cyclic Citrullinated Protein Antibodies (ACPAs) precede symptom onset
  • ACPAs predate first clinical symptoms of RA by a median of 4.5 years

Autoantibodies In Rheumatoid Arthritis.

Recall the mainstay imaging modality for rheumatoid arthritis.

Ultrasound (US)

Describe the findings we may see on an X-ray for rheumatoid arthritis.

  • Soft tissue swelling
  • Ulnar deviation at the metacarpophalangeal joints (MCPJs)
  • Periarticular osteopenia (bone looks darker near the joint)
  • Bony erosions
  • NOTE: Erosions occur only in established disease. The aim of modern therapy is to treat EARLY before erosions (permanent damage) has occurred

Describe a limitation of X-rays when imaging for rheumatoid arthritis.

Information from X-rays is largely limited to bony structures

X-Ray For Rheumatoid Arthritis.

Describe the findings we may see on an ultrasound scan for rheumatoid arthritis.

  • Synovial thickening (synovial hypertrophy)
  • Increased blood flow (seen as doppler signal)
  • May detect erosions not seen on plain X-ray
  • NOTE: US (usually of hands and wrists) can be performed alongside clinical assessment in a dedicated early arthritis clinic

Describe an alternative imaging modality for rheumatoid arthritis.

MRI can also be used but expensive and time-consuming

Ultrasound Scan - Rheumatoid Arthritis.

Describe the principles of management for rheumatoid arthritis.

  • Early recognition of symptoms → referral and diagnosis
  • Prompt initiation of treatment: — GENERAL RULE: joint destruction = extent of inflammation x time
  • Aggressive pharmacological treatment to suppress inflammation
  • Multidisciplinary input where needed — e.g. physiotherapy, occupational therapy, surgery

Describe the first-line pharmacological treatment for rheumatoid arthritis.

  • Combination DMARD therapy: — DMARDs ’ = disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs
  • Methotrexate + hydroxychloroquine &/or sulfasalazine (immunosuppressants)
  • PLUS
  • Immunomodulators or short course of oral steroids

Describe the second-line pharmacological treatment for rheumatoid arthritis.

  • This is after first-line treatments have been tried and the disease is still active
  • Biological therapies (usually therapeutic monoclonal antibodies) targeting specific molecules: — e.g. anti-TNF-alpha blockade

Describe how steroids (glucocorticoids) can be used for the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis.

Glucocorticoid therapy (‘steroids’) useful acutely but avoid long- term use because of side-effects

Describe how NSAIDs can be used for the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis.

  • NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs) — e.g. ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac
  • Historically used but increasingly less relevant
  • Can provide partial symptom relief but do not prevent disease progression
  • Unfavourable long-term side-effect profile

Describe how glucocorticoids work to reduce inflammation.

  • Glucocorticoids bind the glucocorticoid receptor (GR) — GR resides in cytoplasm
  • On binding by glucocorticoids, steroid-GR complex translocates to the nucleus and binds DNA response elements, affecting transcription

Recall four methods of steroid administration.

  • Oral prednisolone
  • Intramuscular (IM) methyl prednisolone
  • Intravenous (IV)
  • Intra-articular (IA)

Recall the side effects of steroid use.

  • Many and bad!
  • Cushing’s syndrome — Cataracts — Mood disturbance — Weight gain → Sleep apnoea — Increased blood pressure — Increased cardiovascular risk — Type II Diabetes mellitus — Central obesity — Osteoporosis — Skin thinning — Myopathy — Infection risk

Steroids (Glucocorticoids) Side Effects.

Describe what is meant by the concept of “treat to target” for rheumatoid arthritis.

This is the concept of suppressing disease activity to improve outcome — This is achieved by regular objective measurement of disease activity e.g. DAS28 score

Describe the components of the DAS28 score and how it is used in the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis.

  • DAS28 = composite of tender joints, of swollen joints, patient visual analogue score (VAS), ESR (or CRP)
  • If DAS28 not suppressed → escalate treatment

What are biological therapies?

Biological therapies are proteins (usually antibodies) that specifically target a protein

Recall the two types of biological therapies used to treat rheumatoid arthritis (RA).

  • Biologics targeting cytokines
  • Biologics targeting lymphocytes

Recall the two mechanisms of biological therapies that target cytokines.

  • Inhibition of tumour necrosis factor-alpha (‘anti-TNF’) — antibodies i.e (infliximab, adalimumab, golimumab, certolizumab) — fusion proteins (etanercept)
  • Inhibition of interleukin-6 (IL-6) signalling — Antibodies against IL-6 receptor: — Tocilizumab (RoActemra) — Sarilumab (Kevzara)

Recall the two mechanisms of biological therapies that target lymphocytes.

  • B cell depletion
  • Blocking T cell co-stimulation

Recall a biological therapy that causes B cell depletion.

Rituximab — Given as two iv infusions, 2 weeks apart — Results in rapid depletion of peripheral B cells — Usually repopulate after ~6-9 months — Side effects: infusion reactions, infection, hypogammaglobulinaemia

Recall how Rituximab induces B cell depletion.

It is an antibody against the B cell antigen, CD20

Recall a biological therapy that blocks T cell co-stimulation.

Abatacept - fusion protein - extracellular domain of CTLA-4 linked to modified Fc portion of human immunoglobulin G1

Describe how Abatacept works.

  • T cells require 2 signals to activate: — MHC + peptide on APC binding to TCR on T cell — CD80/CD86 on APC binding to CD28 on T cell
  • Abatacept blocks signal 2

Abatacept.

What is seronegative inflammatory arthritis?

  • These are a family of conditions with overlapping clinical features and pathogenesis
  • Unlike rheumatoid arthritis, RF and CCP antibodies not present in blood (“seronegative”)
  • BUT they are immune-mediated conditions

Describe the distinct feature of seronegative inflammatory arthritis.

RF and CCP antibodies not present in blood (“seronegative”) but they are immune-mediated conditions

Recall four seronegative inflammatory arthritis conditions.

  • Psoriatic arthritis
  • Ankylosing spondylitis
  • Reactive arthritis
  • IBD-associated arthritis — i.e Crohn’s disease or ulcerative colitis

What is psoriatic arthritis?

Psoriatic arthritis is a long-term inflammatory arthritis that may occur in some people affected by psoriasis

Describe the appearance of psoriatic arthritis.

  • Psoratic arthritis may often appear as scaly red plaques on extensor surfaces — i.e elbows and knees
  • NOTE: The same as psoriasis

Recall the proportion of psoriasis patients that also have joint inflammation.

~10% of psoriasis patients also have joint inflammation (psoriatic arthritis)

Describe the molecules involved in the pathogenesis of psoriatic arthritis.

  • Dominant pathogenic pathway is interleukin-17/interleukin-23 (IL17- IL23)
  • NOTE: Unlike rheumatoid arthritis, rheumatoid factors are not present (“seronegative”)

Describe the relationship between the skin disease and joint disease in psoriatic arthritis.

  • Skin disease severity not correlated to joint manifestations
  • Examine skin carefully for small areas of psoriasis (NB scalp, umbilicus)
  • Sometimes nail changes may be the only skin manifestation
