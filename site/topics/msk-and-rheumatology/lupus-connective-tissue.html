Overview

Recall four autoimmune “connective tissue” disorders.

  • Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)
  • Autoimmune inflammatory muscle disease
  • Systemic sclerosis (scleroderma)
  • Sjogren’s syndrome

Describe why the term “connective tissue disorder” may not be entirely accurate for the above disorders.

  • The term “connective tissue disorder” not entirely accurate as there is often involvement of other organs
  • ‘Multisystem autoimmune rheumatic diseases’ might be better

Describe what is meant by an overlap syndrome.

Overlap syndrome is when more than one of the above autoimmune connective tissue disorders coexist or seem to coexist — this can occur especially in children

What is rheumatoid arthritis?

Rheumatoid arthritis is a chronic autoimmune disease that causes inflammation of the joints, leading to pain, swelling, and stiffness

What is the primary site of inflammation for rheumatoid arthritis?

Synovium (synovial membrane) of the joints

Describe the distinctive feature that can help us to identify rheumatoid arthritis clinically.

Associated with autoantibodies: — Rheumatoid factor — Anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (CCP) antibodies

Rheumatoid Arthritis.

What is ankylosing spondylitis?

Ankylosing spondylitis is the major inflammatory spondyloarthritis disease characterised by long-term inflammation of the spine that

can lead to spinal fusion and deformity Describe the major physical manifestation of ankylosing spondylitis.

Loss of spinal movements — as a result of spinal deformity

Spinal Deformity.

Describe the characteristic feature of ankylosing spondylitis.

Ankylosing spondylitis is characterised by enthesitis

What are entheses?

Entheses are the sites where tendons, ligaments, and joint capsules attach to bone, acting as crucial interfaces for transmitting forces from muscles to bone

Entheses.

Recall four seronegative inflammatory arthritis disorders.

  • Ankylosing spondylitis
  • Reactive Arthritis
  • Arthritis associated with psoriasis (psoriatic arthritis)
  • Arthritis associated with gastrointestinal inflammation (enteropathic arthritis)

What is systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)?

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is an autoimmune disease involving disturbance of both innate and adaptive immune systems

Describe the clinical features of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

  • Autoantibodies (antibodies directed against self antigens) — antibodies to nuclear components
  • NOTE: Unlike rheumatoid arthritis which has designated antibodies like rheumatoid factor and anti-CCPs, SLE has these anti-nuclear antibodies (ANAs) which target self-antigens

What is an immune complex?

  • An immune complex is a molecule formed when an antibody binds to a specific antigen
  • Antibody-antigen = immune complex

Describe the nature of inflammation observed in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

Multi-site inflammation but particularly the joints, skin and kidney

Describe how this inflammation arises.

Immune complexes and other mechanisms lead to this chronic tissue inflammation

What is arthralgia?

Joint pain

Malar Rash.

Describe three features of autoimmune “connective tissue” disorders.

  • Arthralgia and arthritis is typically non-erosive ( unlike rheumatoid)
  • Serum autoantibodies are characteristic and… — Useful diagnostically — Some correlate with disease activity — May be directly pathogenic
  • Raynaud’s phenomenon is common in these conditions

What is Raynaud’s phenomenon?

Raynaud's phenomenon is a condition where blood vessels in the extremities, particularly the fingers and toes, react excessively to cold or stress, causing intermittent vasospasms in the digit that restrict blood flow and leading to colour changes and numbness

Describe what Raynaud’s phenomenon looks like and its progression.

Typical triphasic colour changes — WHITE → Arterial vasospasm (constriction) leads to blanching of digit — BLUE → Cyanosis as static venous blood deoxygenates — RED → Reactive hyperaemia, blood rushes back in as vessels reopen

Recall the trigger for Raynaud’s phenomenon.

Usually triggered by cold exposure

Describe the prognosis of Raynaud’s phenomenon.

Severe Raynaud’s → tissue ischaemia, ulcers and necrosis

Describe the typical age of onset for systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

Typical onset between 15 – 45 years

Describe which gender is affected more by systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

Female : Male ~9:1

Describe the prevalence of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) in different ancestral groups.

African › Asian › White European

Describe the clinical manifestations of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

  • Clinical manifestations of lupus are highly variable which causes diagnostic difficulties
  • The disease may range from mild to life-threatening

Describe the skin and mucosal clinical features of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

  • Malar (butterfly) rash – erythema that spares the nasolabial fold
  • Photosensitive rash
  • Mouth ulcers
  • Hair loss

Nasolabial Fold.

Describe the vascular clinical features of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

Raynaud’s phenomenon

Describe the musculoskeletal clinical features of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

Arthralgia and sometimes arthritis (usually non-erosive) — i.e there are no bony erosions and any deformity is usually due to soft tissue damage

Arthritis In Sle.

Describe the internal organ clinical features of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

  • Serositis (pericarditis, pleuritis, less commonly peritonitis)
  • Renal disease – glomerulonephritis (‘lupus nephritis’)
  • Cerebral disease — ‘cerebral lupus’ e.g. psychosis
  • Myocarditis

Describe the haematological clinical features of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

  • Autoimmune thrombocytopenia (low platelets) — immune system destroys platelets
  • Haemolytic anaemia
  • Lymphopenia

Describe two other clinical features of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

  • Lymphadenopathy
  • Fever in absence of infection

Describe a common hallmark of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

A hallmark of SLE is the presence of anti-nuclear antibodies (ANAs)

What is an autoantibody?

An antibody produced by an organism in response to an antigen on its own tissues

How indicative are anti-nuclear antibodies (ANAs) of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)?

ANAs are found in all SLE patients

Describe the specificity of anti-nuclear antibodies (ANAs) to SLE.

  • However, ANA is not specific for lupus
  • It may be seen in other autoimmune diseases, infection or even healthy people

Describe the significance of a negative anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) test.

Negative ANA effectively rules out SLE

Describe what is meant by ANA pattern and its significance diagnostically. (ADDITIONAL)

  • If ANA is positive, the clinical laboratory will report strength (maximum dilution at which antibody can still be detected) and pattern — E.g. ANA 1:320 is three times stronger than ANA 1:80
  • ANA pattern of staining can suggest the autoantigen the antibodies are reacting to

Recall five other autoantibody tests that can be performed to discover what ANAs are reacting to and narrow down diagnoses.

  • Anti-ds-DNA antibodies
  • Anti-Ro
  • Anti-La
  • Anti-centromere
  • Anti-Smith (Sm)
  • Anti-RNP
  • Anti-Scl-70

What is anti-dsDNA?

Anti-dsDNA antibodies are a type of antinuclear antibody (ANA) that targets double-stranded DNA

What is anti-Sm?

Anti-Sm antibodies are directed against the Sm (Smith) antigen,

which is a complex of proteins associated with small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (snRNPs)

Anti-Sm antibodies are highly specific for systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)

Recall the other major antibody that is present in some SLE patients.

As well as ANA, some SLE patients have antibodies directed to phospholipids on cell membrane known as anti-phospholipid antibodies (APLs)

What are antiphospholipid antibodies (APLs)?

  • Antiphospholipid antibodies are autoantibodies that mistakenly target phospholipid-binding proteins, leading to an increased risk of blood clotting (thrombosis) and pregnancy complications
  • NOTE: Phospholipid-binding proteins interact with phospholipids within cell membranes to induce changes to the cell according to the requirements dictated by the extracellular environment

Example Of Phospholipid-Binding Protein Action.

How phospholipid-binding proteins clear dying cells:

  • When cells undergo apoptosis, they expose phosphatidylserine (PS).
  • MFG-E8 (Lactadherin) and Annexin V (these are both PBPs) bind PS and help phagocytes (like macrophages) recognise and engulf dying cells

Describe the significance of anti-phospholipid antibody (APL) in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

Anti-phospholipid antibodies (APLs) are associated with ↑ risk of: — Thrombosis — arterial (e.g. stroke) — venous (e.g. deep vein thrombosis, DVT) — Pregnancy loss (miscarriage)

Deep Vein Thrombosis.

Recall the two main anti-phospholipid antibodies that are measured clinically in a lab.

  • Anticardiolipin antibodies
  • Anti-beta2glycoprotein 1 antibodies

What is anti-phospholipid antibody syndrome?

  • Persistent presence of APL + a clinical event = “ anti-phospholipid antibody syndrome ”
  • NOTE: Anti-phospholipid antibody syndrome can also occur in absence of SLE. This is ‘primary anti-phospholipid antibody syndrome’

Recall the five main autoantibodies present in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

  • Antinuclear antibodies (ANA)
  • Anti-double stranded DNA antibodies (anti-dsDNA)
  • Anti-phospholipid antibodies
  • Anti-Sm antibodies — antigen is a ribonucleoprotein
  • Anti-Ro antibodies — antigen is a ribonucleoprotein
  • Anti-La antibodies — antigen is a ribonucleoprotein

Describe the significance of antinuclear antibodies (ANAs) to systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) diagnosis.

  • Seen in all SLE cases
  • Not specific for SLE

Describe the significance of anti-double stranded DNA antibodies (anti- dsDNA) to systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) diagnosis.

  • Specific for SLE
  • Serum level of antibody correlates with disease activity

Describe the significance of anti-phospholipid antibodies to systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) diagnosis.

  • Associated with risk of arterial and venous thrombosis in SLE
  • May also occur in absence of SLE in what is termed the ‘primary anti- phospholipid antibody syndrome’

Describe the significance of anti-Sm antibodies to systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) diagnosis.

  • Specific for SLE
  • Serum level of antibody does NOT correlate with disease activity

Describe the significance of anti-Ro and anti-La antibodies to systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) diagnosis.

  • Secondary Sjögren’s syndrome
  • Neonatal lupus syndrome (transient rash in neonate, permanent heart block)
  • NOTE: Secondary Sjögren's syndrome occurs when Sjögren's syndrome symptoms appear alongside another autoimmune disease, such as rheumatoid arthritis or lupus, rather than as a standalone condition

Describe a characteristic of anti-Ro antibodies that increase their potency.

Anti-Ro antibodies can cross the placenta, so can cause babies to be born with transient rashes

Recall the autoantibodies present in rheumatoid arthritis.

  • Rheumatoid factor
  • Anti-cyclic citrullinated protein antibodies (anti-CCP antibodies or ACPAs)

Describe how systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) affects the innate immune system.

  • Overactivity of type 1 interferon pathway
  • Complement pathway abnormalities

Describe how systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) affects the adaptive immune system.

Autoreactive B and T cells

Describe the role of Type 1 interferons. (ADDITIONAL)

  • Type 1 interferons are proteins normally produced in response to viral infections
  • Type 1 interferons include several different proteins, including interferon-alpha
  • Gene expression studies comparing the blood of lupus patients to healthy controls revealed overexpression of genes that are activated downstream of binding of the type 1 interferon receptor. This is sometimes called the “interferon gene signature”. A high interferon gene signature is associated with worse disease
  • A drug blocking the type 1 interferon receptor (anifrolumab) has recently shown success in randomised clinical trials in SLE; it’s not yet available in the UK

What is meant by ‘the waste disposal hypothesis’ of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

This is the hypothesis that explains how the immune system is involved in generating a response to nuclear antigens even though these antigens are supposed to be hidden in cells

Describe ‘the waste disposal hypothesis’ of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

  • Apoptosis leads to translocation of nuclear antigens to membrane surface
  • Impaired clearance of apoptotic cells results in enhanced presentation of nuclear antigens to immune cells
  • This leads to B cell autoimmunity
  • Tissue damage by antibody effector mechanisms — e.g. complement activation and Fc receptor engagement

Why are manifestations of lupus so varied?

  • This is because lupus is very much on systemic end of the organ specific → systemic spectrum of autoimmunity
  • The presence of anti-nuclear antibodies (ANAs) which can literally act against any cell with a nucleus (pretty much all cells except RBCs), makes the manifestations of lupus much less predictable too

Describe the findings on investigations for inflammation that are conducted to identify systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

High ESR but usually normal C-reactive protein unless infection or serositis/arthritis

Describe the haematological findings that can be made to identify systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

Haemolytic anaemia, Lymphopenia, Thrombocytopenia

Describe the renal investigations that can be conducted to identify systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

  • Very important to measure urine protein (urinalysis + quantify with urine protein:creatinine ratio [uPCR])
  • Creatinine (part of “U&E”, measure of renal function)
  • Look at albumin
  • Kidney biopsy if persistent proteinuria

Describe the immunological investigations that can be conducted to identify systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

  • Antinuclear antibodies (ANAs)
  • Anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies - highly specific, correlate with disease activity
  • Complement consumption – e.g. low C4 and C3
  • Antiphospholipid (APL) antibodies

Describe some other investigations that may be conducted to identify systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

Imaging of specific organs — e.g. CT thorax, echocardiogram or cardiac MRI for heart as dictated by symptoms

Describe the three principles of management for systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

  • Treatment in SLE aims at remission or low disease activity and prevention of flares
  • Balance of controlling disease vs avoiding iatrogenic harm (especially steroids)
  • Specific choice of treatment will depend on disease severity and organ manifestations

Describe how iatrogenic harm may arise in treatment of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

  • Steroid side effects (see RA lecture), especially: — Infection — Osteoporosis — Avascular necrosis (AVN) - often affects hips: higher incidence in lupus, especially in presence of APL Abs
  • Appropriate initiation of immunomodulatory agents can expedite the tapering/discontinuation of glucocorticoids

Recall the drug recommended for all patients of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

Hydroxychloroquine is recommended in all patients with lupus — immunosuppressant
