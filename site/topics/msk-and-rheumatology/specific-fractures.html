Overview

Describe the principles of orthopaedics as a specialty.

  • Look
  • Feel
  • Move
  • X-Ray
  • Orthopaedics is more about examination, investigation and then making management plans

Describe the principles of trauma as a specialty.

  • Reduce
  • Hold — Plaster — External fixator — Internal fixation
  • Rehabilitate — Normally six weeks later
  • Trauma is more about “keeping the patient alive first”

Describe the main objective within trauma.

  • To keep the patient alive first using ATLS
  • In trauma, the fracture is the least important bit

What is ATLS?

Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) is a method used by healthcare practitioners to immediately assess and treat injured patients

Recall the components of advanced trauma life support (ATLS) when used to deal with trauma.

  • Airway
  • Breathing
  • Circulation
  • Disability — i.e neurology
  • NOTE: Orthopaedics becomes involved as part of the Circulation region of trauma. — i.e when you break a bone and damage its blood supply

Recall five factors we consider when we are assessing fractures.

  • Pain
  • Swelling
  • Crepitus
  • Deformity
  • Collateral damage — Nerves — Vessels

What is crepitus?

Crepitus describes a popping, clicking or crackling sound in a joint

X-Rays

Recall when a CT scan is used in fracture diagnosis.

Describe when an MRI scan is used in fracture diagnosis.

  • MRIs are useful when we are unsure of the fracture pattern from an X-Ray or CT scan
  • MRIs (T2-weighted) can also be particularly useful in identifying oedema at the site of a fracture — This would usually appear very white on the scan
  • NOTE: MRIs also provide detailed imaging on soft tissues, bone marrow and subtle fractures

Regions Of A Long Bone.

Describe the difference between simple and multifragmentary fractures.

  • A simple fracture refers to a bone break with only two fragments
  • A multifragmentary fracture is a bone break with multiple fragments

Describe the difference between transverse, oblique and spiral fractures.

  • A transverse fracture occurs when a bone breaks straight across, perpendicular to the mid-diaphyseal line
  • An oblique fracture breaks diagonally across the bone at an angle to the mid-diaphyseal line
  • A spiral fracture twists around the bone in a helical pattern, usually caused by a rotational force
  • SUMMARY: A transverse fracture is a straight break, an oblique fracture is a diagonal break, and a spiral fracture is a twisting break

Twisting Force In A Spiral Fracture.

Describe the difference between translated and angulated fractures.

  • A translated fracture refers to a bone break where the fractured ends of the bone have shifted sideways relative to each other, essentially sliding out of alignment
  • An angulated fracture means the broken bone pieces are tilted at an angle to one another, creating a distinct bend in the bone's axis

Different Types Of Fracture.

Describe the difference between an impacted and an avulsion fracture.

  • An impacted fracture occurs when the broken ends of a bone are driven into each other
  • An avulsion fracture happens when a piece of bone is pulled away from the main bone by a ligament or tendon

Impacted Vs Avulsion Fractures.

Describe the difference between high and low energy trauma.

  • High energy trauma refers to injuries caused by a significant force — i.e a major fall from a height, a car accident, or a violent impact
  • Low energy trauma refers to injuries from a relatively minor force — i.e a simple slip and fall from standing height

Describe the difference in the patient demographic between high and low energy trauma.

High energy trauma is more commonly observed in younger patients, however low energy trauma is more commonly observed in older patients

Recall three characteristics/signs that indicate high energy trauma.

  • Periosteal stripping — blood supply cut off
  • Wide zone of injury — soft tissue
  • Other local and distant injuries
  • Comminution — bone crushed into tiny pieces

What is meant by an AP radiograph?

An AP (anteroposterior) radiograph refers to an image where the X- ray beam passes through the patient from the front of the body to the back

Recall the two types of angulation observed in fractures.

  • Varus
  • Valgus

What is a varus deformity?

Varus deformity is where a bone or joint angulates inward too much — The distal part of the bone goes medial

What is a valgus deformity?

Valgus deformity is where a bone or joint angulates outward too much — The distal part of the bone goes lateral

Varus Vs. Valgus Deformity.

Describe what is meant by displacement in reference to fractures.

Displacement is the general term used to describe the abnormal positioning of a part of bone after it has been fractured

Describe what is meant by translation in reference to fractures.

  • Translation is a form of displacement where the fractured ends of the bone have shifted sideways relative to each other
  • The two types of translation are medial and lateral

Forms Of Displacement.

Recall the two types of fracture healing.

  • Direct fracture healing
  • Indirect fracture healing

What is direct fracture healing?

  • Direct fracture healing, also known as primary bone healing, is a process that occurs when a fracture is rigidly immobilised and the bone ends are perfectly aligned
  • Osteoclast and osteoblast activity at the site of the fracture allows for smooth bone remodelling
  • EXAMPLE: This type of healing is enabled when lag screws are used

What is indirect fracture healing?

  • Indirect fracture healing, also known as secondary fracture healing, is a process that involves both endochondral and intramembranous bone healing as well as callus formation
  • EXAMPLE: This type of healing is observed when casts or external fixation is used

Describe how we choose between direct and indirect fracture healing.

  • Indirect fracture healing may be chosen when — The fracture site cannot be rigidly stabilised — i.e comminuted fractures — Natural bone healing is preferred — i.e in young patients — Minimally invasive techniques are used to preserve blood supply — Works well for diaphyseal fractures
  • Direct fracture healing may be chosen when — Rigid stabilisation of the fracture is possible — Anatomical alignment of the fracture is critical to its function upon healing — i.e intra-articular fractures, to prevent arthritis

Describe the difference between direct and indirect fracture healing.

  • In direct fracture healing, anatomical reduction is created by the application of force — There is absolute stability/compression at the fracture site — There is also no callus
  • In indirect fracture healing, sufficient but not complete reduction is created — There is micromovement at the fracture site — There is callus formation

Describe briefly the steps involved in indirect fracture healing.

  • Inflammation — Haematoma formation — Release of cytokines — Granulation tissue and blood vessel formation
  • Repair — Soft callus formation - Type II collagen (cartilage) — Hard callus formation - Type I collagen (bone)
  • Remodelling — Callus responds to activity, external forces, functional demand and growth. Wolff’s Law — Excess bone is removed

What is a haematoma?

  • A haematoma is a collection of blood that forms outside of blood vessels, usually due to an injury or trauma
  • For example, a direct hit to the body can damage blood vessels, causing blood to leak into the surrounding tissue

What is a bony callus?

  • A bony callus is a temporary, hard tissue that forms over a broken bone to help it heal
  • NOTE: Bony calluses are not to be confused with skin calluses which are hardened areas of skin used to protect it from damage

Describe the purpose of bony callus formation.

  • Bridges the fracture gap and prevents movement
  • Supports blood vessel growth for oxygen and nutrient supply
  • Helps restore mechanical strength to the bone before full healing is complete

What is Wolff’s Law?

  • Bone grows and remodels in response to the forces that are placed on it
  • This is important for bone growth and preventing bone from growing in the wrong direction

What is the difference between Type I and Type II Collagen in the context of indirect fracture healing?

  • Soft callus formation - Type II collagen (cartilage)
  • Hard callus formation - Type I collagen (bone)

Healing Of A Paediatric Humerus.

Recall the three principles in managing fractures.

  • Reduce
  • Hold
  • Rehabilitate

Describe how reduction is involved in the management of a fracture.

  • Open or closed reduction is used to reduce the space between the two bones of a fracture
  • Open reduction is a surgical procedure used to reduce the fracture — surgically repositioning the broken pieces of bone using plates, screws or intramedullary rods
  • Closed reduction is a non-surgical procedure used to reduce the fracture — i.e reducing the fracture manually by realigning the bone

Describe the benefit of closed reduction over open reduction.

Closed reduction does not disrupt the blood supply to the bone in question, as it is a non-invasive procedure, whereas open reduction typically does — However, the open reduction typically allows us to reduce the fracture more accurately

Describe how holding is involved in the management of a fracture.

Holding is used to maintain the aforementioned reduction so that the bone can grow back and repair accurately

Recall three ways we can hold a fracture in place.

  • Plaster/splint
  • External fixation
  • Internal fixation

Describe how rehabilitation is involved in the management of a fracture.

  • Early/late rehab
  • Weight bearing
  • Physiotherapy

Open Vs. Closed Reduction.

Where is the medullary cavity of a bone located?

  • The medullary cavity of a bone is the hollow part of the bone that contains bone marrow
  • NOTE: The spongy/trabecular bone is located on the ends of the bone, either side of the medullary cavity

Medullary Cavity (2).

Recall how fracture complications can be grouped.

  • General — Early — Late
  • Specific

Recall three general (early or late) fracture complications.

  • Fat embolus
  • Deep vein thrombosis (DVT)
  • Infection
  • Prolonged immobility — UTI, chest infections, sores

What is fat embolus?

  • Fat embolism is where particles of fat get into your bloodstream and block blood flow
  • This can happen after a fracture if the bone marrow is disrupted and fat globules are released into the bloodstream

Recall three specific fracture complications.

  • Neurovascular injury
  • Muscle/tendon injury
  • Non union/mal union
  • Local infection
  • Degenerative change — intra-articular
  • Reflex sympathetic dystrophy (complex regional pain syndrome) — severe burning pain

What is a non-union fracture?

A non-union, also known as an un-united fracture, is a broken bone that doesn't heal at all — The two ends of the bone fail to rejoin together

What is a mal-union fracture?

A fracture mal-union occurs when a broken bone heals in an abnormal position, such as being bent, twisted, or rotated

Non-Union Vs. Mal-Union Fracture.

What is meant by a fracture having a bimodal distribution?

  • A bimodal distribution is a data set with two distinct peaks, or modes
  • In the case of a fracture, it indicates that there are two distinct populations in which the fracture is commonly observed

The proximal femur has a head, neck and greater and lesser trochanters

The circumflex arteries that supply the femur wrap around the femoral neck

Blood Supply Of Femur (Y1 Csi).

Describe the difference between an intracapsular and an extracapsular fracture.

  • An intracapsular fracture is a fracture that occurs within the fibrous tissue that envelops a bone at a joint — blood supply is more likely to be compromised
  • An extracapsular fracture occurs outside this fibrous tissue

Capsule Of Hip Joint.

Recall five types of hip fracture and whether each one is intracapsular or extracapsular.

  • Subcapital (intracapsular)
  • Transcervical (intracapsular)
  • Basicervical (extracapsular)
  • Sub-trochanteric (extracapsular)
  • Part-trochanteric (extracapsular)
  • NOTE: Capital = relating to the head, cervical = relating to the neck.

Hip Fractures.

What is avascular necrosis?

Avascular necrosis is the death of bone tissue due to interruption of the blood supply

Describe what causes avascular necrosis in the femur.

  • It is commonly due to a fracture that interrupts the blood supply to the femur
  • Commonly, the circumflex artery within the femoral neck is affected

What is Shenton’s line?

  • An imaginary curved line drawn along the inferior border of the superior pubic ramus, along the inferomedial border of the neck of femur
  • It should be continuous and smooth

Superior Pubic Ramus.

Describe how Shenton’s line is clinically relevant.

If Shenton’s line is “broken” it indicates that the hip is dislocated and something is out of position SHENTON’S LINE.

Describe the management of hip fractures.

  • Extracapsular hip fractures are commonly managed by dynamic hip screws, which connect the broken pieces of bone back together
  • Intracapsular hip fractures are more difficult to manage and often involve hemiarthroplasties or hip replacements for management, depending on displacement/ no displacement

When are screws used to manage intracapsular hip fractures?

  • If they are undisplaced
  • If patient is under 55 with displaced fracture

Describe the management of displaced intracapsular hip fractures. Less than 55

  • Reduce and fixation with screws Older than 65
  • Fit and mobile: total hip replacement
  • Less fit: hemiarthroplasty

Describe the benefits of dynamic hip screws.

  • Allows patients to start walking again sooner
  • Relieves pain from the broken bone ends rubbing together
  • Helps prevent complications like pressure sores and chest infections

Dynamic Hip Screws.

What is a hemiarthroplasty?

A hemiarthroplasty is a surgical procedure to replace the head of the femur

Describe in which demographic hemiarthroplasty is most commonly used as treatment.

  • Elderly patients
  • NOTE: Hemiarthroplasty is used in less fit patients, total hip replacement is used in fit and mobile patients

Describe the difference between a hemiarthroplasty and a total hip replacement.

  • In a hemiarthroplasty, only the femoral head is replaced
  • In a total hip replacement, both the femoral head and acetabulum are replaced
