Describe the function of the cochlea.

  • Its function is to transduce vibration into nervous impulses that the brain can interpret as sound
  • It does so in a way that captures the frequency (or pitch) and intensity (or loudness) of the sound and allows us to perceive differences accurately

Cochlear Compartments.

Describe the structure of the scala vestibuli and the scala tympani.

The scala vestibuli and scala tympani are both bony structures that contain perilymph (high in sodium )

Describe the structure of the scala media.

  • The scala media is a membranous structure that contains endolymph (high in potassium )
  • It is also where the hearing organ/Organ of Corti is located

What is perilymph and where is it found?

Perilymph is a fluid found in the inner ear, specifically within the two outer chambers of the cochlea: the scala vestibuli and the scala tympani.

Describe the chemical properties of perilymph.

Perilymph has a composition similar to that of extracellular fluid, with a high concentration of sodium ions (Na⁺) and a low concentration of potassium ions (K⁺). — This ionic composition is important for maintaining the electrochemical gradients necessary for the function of the auditory and vestibular systems.

Describe the physical properties of perilymph.

Perilymph is a viscous fluid, providing a medium through which sound vibrations can travel effectively.

Describe the function of perilymph.

Perilymph plays a crucial role in conducting sound waves • When sound vibrations from the oval window (transmitted by the stapes) enter the scala vestibuli, they create waves in the perilymph, which then travel through the cochlea. • These fluid waves displace the basilar membrane, leading to the activation of hair cells in the organ of Corti, which ultimately results in the conversion of sound vibrations into electrical signals sent to the brain.

What is endolymph and where is it found?

  • Endolymph is a specialised fluid found within the inner ear, particularly in the scala media (cochlear duct) of the cochlea, as well as in the semicircular canals and utricle and saccule of the vestibular system
  • It hence plays a role in both the auditory and vestibular systems

Describe the chemical properties of endolymph.

  • Endolymph has a unique ionic composition that is distinct from other bodily fluids
  • It is characterised by a high concentration of potassium ions (K⁺) and a low concentration of sodium ions (Na⁺).

Describe the function of endolymph.

Within the cochlea, endolymph fills the scala media and plays a vital role in the process of hearing • When sound vibrations create waves in the perilymph (scala vestibuli and scala tympani), these vibrations cause the basilar membrane to move • This movement displaces the hair cells in the organ of Corti against the tectorial membrane, leading to the conversion of mechanical vibrations into electrical signals • The hair cells' response is influenced by the unique ionic environment of the endolymph, which is crucial for their depolarisation and subsequent signal transmission to the auditory nerve

Cochlear Membranes.

What is the Organ of Corti?

  • The organ of Corti is the sensory structure located within the scala media (cochlear duct) of the cochlea in the inner ear
  • It is essential for the process of hearing, as it contains the hair cells that convert sound vibrations into electrical signals, which are then sent to the brain via the auditory nerve

What is the name of the structure that the Organ of Corti lies on?

Basilar membrane — The Organ of Corti is situated directly on top of the basilar membrane

What is the basilar membrane?

  • The basilar membrane is a flexible, fibrous membrane within the cochlea of the inner ear that plays a crucial role in the process of hearing
  • It serves as the foundation for the organ of Corti and is essential for translating sound vibrations into neural signals

Describe how the basilar membrane is structured.

  • The basilar membrane is arranged tonotopically, using the same principle as a xylophone
  • This means it is narrow and tight (stiff) at the base (near the oval window) but wider and loose (more flexible) at the apex

Basilar Membrane In The Cochlea.

Describe the structures present within the Organ of Corti.

The organ of Corti contains two types of hair cells: — Inner hair cells (IHC) — Outer hair cells (OHC)

Organ Of Corti.

What is the tectorial membrane?

  • The tectorial membrane is a critical component of the auditory system located within the cochlea of the inner ear
  • It plays a vital role in the process of hearing by interacting with the hair cells of the organ of Corti

Describe how the tectorial membrane interacts with the Organ of Corti.

  • The tectorial membrane is above the hair cells of the organ of Corti and allows hair deflection, which in turn will depolarise the hair cell — This helps to transduce sound into nerve impulses
  • NOTE: The hair cells within the organ of Corti are the “sensory receptors for sound.”

Describe how the tectorial membrane receives the energy

to move the stereocilia. • The tectorial membrane itself doesn’t actively receive or generate energy to move ; rather, it participates passively in the process of sound transduction by interacting with the basilar membrane and organ of Corti. • Here is how it works: • Sound waves induce pressure changes in the cochlear fluid (perilymph + endolymph). ◦ stapes transmits energy from the sound waves to the perilymph and endolymph via the oval window • These pressure changes cause the basilar membrane to vibrate. • The relative motion between the basilar membrane and the tectorial membrane bends the stereocilia.

What is the function of the inner hair cells?

Their function is the transduction of the sound (vibrations) into nerve impulses, (which the brain can interpret)

What do the inner hair cells carry 95% of?

  • Inner hair cells carry 95% of the afferent information in the auditory nerve
  • This includes initial conversion of the sound into nerve impulses and transmission to the brain — the signal goes on the auditory nerve from the cochlea → the brain

What is the function of the outer hair cells?

Their function is modulation of the sensitivity of the response

What do the outer hair cells carry 95% of?

  • Outer hair cells carry 95% of the efferent information of the auditory nerve
  • This includes modulation of the sensitivity of the hearing response — the signal goes on the auditory nerve from the brainstem → cochlea, and the outer hair cells carry this signal coming from the brainstem, to modulate the sensitivity hearing response

Describe the difference between afferent and efferent auditory information.

  • Afferent auditory information refers to the sensory signals travelling from the ear to the brain, allowing us to perceive and interpret sounds
  • In contrast, efferent auditory information refers to the signals travelling from the brain back to the ear, modulating and fine- tuning the auditory response

Recall the primary neurotransmitter involved in the stimulation of the auditory nerve.

Glutamate (excitatory)

Describe how potassium and potassium channels are involved in signal transduction to the afferent auditory nerve.

Potassium (K+) is present in high levels in the endolymph, the fluid that circulates within the scala media and ultimately comes in contact with the hair cells on the Organ of Corti

When a potassium (K+) channel opens, potassium influx from the endolymph to the hair cells occur and the (hair) cells become depolarised

(see lecture diagram / notes)

which induces depolarisation Describe how higher amplitudes may impact the transduction of sound.

  • Higher amplitudes (louder sounds) will cause greater deflection of stereocilia and K+ channel opening
  • This in turn causes greater release of glutamate (excitatory neurotransmitter) and greater stimulation of the auditory nerve

Describe the roles of depolarisation and hyperpolarisation in transduction.

  • Depolarisation is used to open K+ channels and hence induce the release of glutamate from stereocilia → auditory nerve — It is hence more effective for listening
  • Hyperpolarisation instead is used to close K+ channels and the reduce the firing rate into the auditory nerve by reducing glutamate secretion — It is hence less effective for listening but it is useful in ensuring that the hair cell only transmits glutamate → auditory nerve when the sound is being transmitted and the stereocilia are bent away from the kinocilium — NOTE: Hyperpolarisation thus acts as an inhibitory phase
  • The combination of depolarisation and hyperpolarisation helps us to understand and encode the characteristics of the sounds we have heard

Hyper-Polarisation And Depolarisation Of Hair Cells.

What is the auditory nerve?

The auditory nerve (cochlear nerve) is part of the vestibulocochlear nerve (cranial nerve VIII), and it carries sound information from the cochlea in the inner ear to the brainstem and beyond, allowing us to perceive and process sounds

Describe the course of the auditory nerve from the cochlea to the brain.

  • The left and right cochlear nerves go from the cochlea to the cochlear nucleus which can be found at the pons level of the brainstem — At this point, innervation at the cochlear nucleus is unilateral
  • The nerve then goes from the cochlear nucleus to the superior olivary nucleus — The left and right cochlear nerves innervate both the left and right superior olivary nuclei, so innervation from this point onwards is bilateral
  • Signals then pass bilaterally → lateral lemniscus (fibre tract) → inferior colliculus within the midbrain of the brainstem → medial geniculate body within the thalamus
  • The signals then go from this medial geniculate body to the primary auditory cortex located within the temporal lobe of the brain — Specifically, the sound goes to Heschl’s gyrus within the primary auditory cortex

Describe how we can use the Rinne test to test hearing.

  • Tap tuning fork to generate vibrations through it
  • Place tuning fork on mastoid process (adjacent to ear) and then place it near the ear opening
  • In normal individuals, the second (near ear opening) should be louder because this is the natural way for us to hear — However in those with conductive hearing problems, this is compromised and they may often say the mastoid process provides louder sounds

Describe how we can use the Weber test to test hearing.

  • Tap tuning fork to generate vibrations through it
  • Place tuning fork on forehead and ask patient to localise where the sound is coming from
  • In a patient with bilaterally functioning ears, the patient would not be able to tell where exactly the sound is coming from
  • However if there is a sensorineural issue with one of the ears, then the patient may say that they can hear sound louder in one ear

The Audiogram.

Describe how Audiometry/an Audiogram can be used to assess conductive hearing loss.

  • In conductive hearing loss, the problem lies with the outer/middle ear
  • This means the Organ of Corti and subsequent structures in the hearing response are unaffected, however the problem is that sound cannot adequately reach this organ, as amplification is impaired
  • Therefore, when an Audiometry test is run with headphones, the patient requires higher amplitudes to hear sounds of the same frequency but with bone conduction (i.e sound through mastoid), there is no noticeable difference to normal hearing
  • This is because bone conduction is unaffected by conductive hearing loss

Conductive Hearing Loss.

Describe how Audiometry/an Audiogram can be used to assess sensorineural hearing loss.

  • With sensorineural hearing loss, the problem lies further down the auditory process
  • This means there is no problem with initial conduction in the outer and middle ear and so no noticeable difference will be observed when using bone and normal conduction techniques
  • Both bone and air conduction will be impaired, and patients will require higher decibels (louder sounds) to hear sounds of the same frequency

What are kinocilium?

  • The term kinocilium generally refers to the longest stereocilia, usually located at the lateral end of a group of stereocilia
  • This particular stereocilium is crucial to the depolarisation of its associated hair cell as its tilting allows K+ influx

Describe the difference between a stereocilium and a hair cell.

  • Hair cells are specialised cells that detect mechanical stimuli and convert them into electrical signals and transducing them to nerves (auditory or vestibular)
  • Stereocilia are the hair-like structures that extend from the hair cells and actually detect the mechanical movement — i.e sound or head motion

What are the semicircular canals?

  • The semicircular canals are three curved, fluid-filled tubes located in the inner ear that are essential for detecting head rotation and maintaining balance
  • These canals are part of the vestibular system, which provides information about body position and motion, helping with balance and spatial orientation
  • They are essential components of the vestibular system, enabling us to detect head rotation

Describe the internal structure of the semicircular canals. Semicircular canal

  • The orientation of the canals in the head defines three planes
  • The semicircular canals are curved tubes that are filled with endolymph (a fluid) Ampulla
  • At the base of each semicircular canal, there is a bulging region known as the ampulla — The ampulla contains sensory hair cells that detect changes in the fluid movement (due to head rotation)
  • Within the ampulla, there is a gelatinous structure called the cupula — The cupula moves in response to the flow of endolymph, causing the hair cells to bend, which triggers the release of electrical signals sent to the brain via the vestibular nerve
  • Also within the ampulla, there is a sensory organ called the ampullary crista — It contains hair cells (similar to those in the cochlea and otolith organs), and the movement of the cupula due to rotational movements causes the stereocilia of these hair cells to bend, which generates nerve impulses
  • SUMMARY: The sensory part of the semicircular canals is located in the ampulla, at its base. This ampulla contains a gelatinous mixture called the cupula, and a sensory organ called the ampullary crista, which houses the hair cells. The movement of the cupula dictated by angular movement, leads to the movement of the hair cells in the ampullary crista → depolarisation

What is the cupula?

The cupula is a gelatinous structure within the ampulla that moves in response to fluid motion, bending the hair cells

Describe the role of the cupula.

  • The cupula, similar to the endolymph in the auditory system is vital in inducing the movement of the stereocilia and hair cells within the ampulla of the semicircular canals
  • The cupula is a gelatinous mass that moves in response to the flow of endolymph fluid within the semicircular canals.
  • It bends in the opposite direction of the fluid movement when the head rotates, causing the stereocilia of hair cells to bend.

What is the ampulla of the semicircular canals?

The ampulla is the expanded region at the base of each canal

Describe the role of hair cell potentials in the vestibular system.

Hair cells have a resting potential which has a basal discharge of neurotransmitter to the nerve — Hairs moving towards the kinocilium generates depolarisation and an increase in nerve discharge — Hairs moving away from the kinocilium generates hyperpolarisation and a reduction in nerve discharge

Describe how hair movement towards and away from the kinocilium generates depolarisation.

  • Depolarisation is used to open K+ channels and hence induce the release of glutamate from stereocilia → vestibular nerve — It is hence more effective for listening
  • Hyperpolarisation instead is used to close K+ channels and the reduce the firing rate into the auditory nerve by reducing glutamate secretion — It is hence less effective for listening but it is useful in ensuring that the hair cell only transmits glutamate → auditory nerve when the sound is being transmitted and the stereocilia are bent towards the kinocilium
  • NOTE: Hyperpolarisation thus acts as an inhibitory phase
  • The combination of depolarisation and hyperpolarisation helps us to understand and encode the characteristics of the sounds we have heard

Describe the course of the primary afferents of the vestibular nerve.*

  • The primary afferents of the vestibular nerve originate from the vestibular apparatus, which includes the semicircular canals, otolith organs, and the associated sensory structures like the hair cells
  • Primary afferents then end and synapse in the vestibular nuclei in the brainstem (pons) — NOTE: Projections from the vestibular nuclei go to the abducens nuclei before decussating and going to the oculomotor nuclei — Neurons from the oculomotor nuclei then go to multiple brain regions via the medial longitudinal fasciculus, including the spinal cord, cerebellum, thalamus, and cortex, contributing to balance, eye movements, and spatial orientation

What is the medial longitudinal fasciculus?

  • The medial longitudinal fasciculus (MLF) is a critical bundle of nerve fibres in the brainstem that serves as a communication highway between the cranial nerve nuclei involved in eye movement and head coordination
  • It connects the vestibular, abducens, oculomotor and trochlear nuclei and is vital in maintaining the vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR)

Why does the medial longitudinal fasciculus decussate before reaching the oculomotor nucleus?

  • The function of the abducens nerve is to abduct the eye
  • If we want to look to the right, abduction will be useful for the right eye, but for our left eye to look to the right, we will need adduction
  • This is performed by the medial rectus which is supplied by the oculomotor nerve
  • Hence, the right abducens nerve will need to communicate with the left oculomotor nerve to move both eyes to the right and so the decussation allows communication of these two sides

What is the vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR)?

The vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR) is a reflex mechanism that stabilises vision during head movements by producing compensatory eye movements

Describe the compensatory eye movements made during the vestibulo- ocular reflex (VOR).

Eye movements occur in the opposite direction to head movement, but at the same velocity and amplitude — The result of this is when the head moves in any direction, the VOR keeps the visual field stable by automatically adjusting the position of the eyes to maintain a steady gaze on a fixed point, even when the head is turning

Describe the role of the vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR).

  • To keep images fixed in the retina, even when moving the head
  • NOTE: This reflex is essential for clear vision and spatial orientation during activities that involve head movements, such as walking, running, or simply turning the head

Describe the mechanism behind the vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR). EXAMPLE: We move our heads to the right and we want to keep our eyes fixed on an image

  • The right lateral semicircular canal becomes depolarised and sends a signal up the vestibular nerve
  • This signal jumps to the the left abducens nucleus before jumping again to the right oculomotor nucleus to cause both eyes to look left
  • This helps us to keep focused on the image on the left side even when we look right

  • Timing
  • Laterality

Describe the clinical presentation of an acute and unilateral vestibular disorder.

  • The main complaints of an acute and unilateral disorder are imbalance, dizziness, vertigo and nausea
  • NOTE: Due to the basal firing rate which is uniformly present on both semicircular canals to give the brain information on which direction we are moving in, acute onset is usually unilateral

Describe the clinical presentation of a slow&unilateral or bilateral vestibular disorder.

  • The main complaints of a slow and unilateral/bilateral disorder are imbalance and nausea – NO vertigo — This is because unilateral disorders would cause the basal firing rate to the vestibular nerve to be present on one side but lost on the other — This asymmetry causes confusion and makes one feel as if one is moving to the unaffected (firing) side when they are not
  • However in a bilateral disorder there is no asymmetry in basal firing rate so the brain doesn’t receive any signals from any side to suggest movement in that direction

What are peripheral vestibular disorders?

These are vestibular disorders which involve the vestibular organ or cranial nerve VIII

What are central vestibular disorders?

These are vestibular disorders which involve the CNS (i.e brainstem/cerebellum)

Recall three peripheral vestibular disorders.

  • Vestibular neuritis — VIIIth nerve
  • Benign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo (BPPV) — crystal dislodgement
  • Meniere’s disease — episodic vertigo, hearing loss, tinnitus

What is vestibular neuritis?

Vestibular neuritis is an inflammation of the vestibular nerve, a branch of the vestibulocochlear nerve (cranial nerve VIII) that is responsible for transmitting balance-related information from the inner ear to the brain — It is often triggered by viral infections, but less likely bacterial infections

Describe how vestibular neuritis affects the vestibular system.

  • Vestibular neuritis disrupts the normal transmission of balance signals from the inner ear to the brain
  • This inflammation impairs the nerve’s ability to carry information about head position and movement, leading to an imbalance between the affected (inflamed) and unaffected sides

What is vertigo?

Vertigo is a sensation of spinning, dizziness, or feeling off- balance, often described as if the world is moving around you or as if you yourself are moving when you are actually still.
