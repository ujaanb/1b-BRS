Overview

What is inflammation?

  • Inflammation is the body’s natural response to injury, infection, or harmful stimuli — i.e toxins
  • It serves as a protective mechanism, mobilising the immune system to defend and begin the healing process.

What is meningitis?

This is irritation, inflammation and swelling of the meninges caused by viral or bacterial infection — Meningitis is typically in the subarachnoid space

What is encephalitis?

Inflammation of the brain caused by infection or autoimmune mechanisms — This inflammation can lead to swelling or damage

What is cerebral vasculitis?

Inflammation of blood vessel walls (sometimes called angiitis)

Meningitis & Encephalitis.

What is the blood-brain barrier (BBB)?

The blood-brain barrier (BBB) is a highly selective, protective barrier that separates the blood circulating in the body from the brain's sensitive central nervous system (CNS)

Describe how the discovery of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) came about.

  • In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, it was noted that dyes, and other tracers, injected intravenously, accumulated in most tissues, but were excluded from most areas of the CNS
  • This suggested the existence of a Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB)

Discovery Of The Blood-Brain Barrier (Bbb).

Describe the vascular supply of the CNS.

  • The brain and the CNS are very intensely supplied by blood to the extent that no neuron is more than 100µm from a capillary
  • This is because the brain is incredibly metabolically active

What are pial vessels?

  • Pial vessels are the blood vessels located on the surface of the brain within the thin, innermost meningeal layer called the pia mater
  • They can deliver oxygen and nutrient-rich blood to the brain, while aiding with the removal of carbon dioxide and waste products

Describe an adaptation that blood-brain barrier capillaries have to help form the blood-brain barrier (BBB).

  • Blood-brain barrier (BBB) capillaries have extensive tight junctions at the endothelial cell-cell contacts, massively reducing solute and fluid leak across the capillary wall and into the CNS
  • It is these endothelial cells that can be thought of as the “MVPs” of this BBB system

Pial Vessels.

Describe how the blood-brain barrier (BBB) works to regulate the influx and efflux of substances to and from the CNS.

  • The tight junctions formed by the endothelial cells of the BBB capillaries make it difficult for solutes that usually exchange across peripheral capillaries to enter and exit the CNS
  • This means the BBB has to control the exchange of these substances using specific membrane transporters to transport into and out of the CNS — influx and efflux transporters
  • Blood-borne infectious agents hence have reduced entry into CNS tissue.

Recall three components of a physiological blood-brain barrier (BBB).

  • Endothelial cells
  • Astrocytes
  • Pericytes

What are astrocytes?

  • Astrocytes are star-shaped glial cells in the brain that contribute to the BBB by providing support to endothelial cells and aiding in its selective permeability
  • They also perform metabolic, structural, homeostatic, and neuroprotective tasks such as clearing excess neurotransmitters, stabilising and regulating the blood-brain barrier, and promoting synapse formation

What are pericytes?

Pericytes are cells that surround endothelial cells, further helping regulate blood flow and the barrier's integrity

Blood-Brain Barrier (Bbb).

Describe what happens in the event of blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption.

  • For example if the integrity of the BBB is depleted by trauma, the endothelial cells may be removed
  • This means the content of the capillaries can leak into the parenchyma of the brain — i.e fibrinogen
  • Contents of the blood such as fibrinogen can cause adverse reactions in glial cells such as astrogliosis (accumulation of astrocytes in response to trauma) and the astrocytes prioritise their response to trauma over their function in the BBB
  • This systemic leakage ultimately leads to collagenous build-up around the BBB, impairing its integrity
  • NOTE: Collagen is physiologically important in maintaining the integrity of the BBB in capillary walls, but the build-up in response to trauma causes the BBB to lose its integrity

Blood-Brain Barrier (Bbb) Disruption.

Describe the initial symptoms of encephalitis.

Initially, symptoms are flu-like with pyrexia (high body temperature) and headache

Recall three subsequent symptoms of encephalitis.

  • Subsequently, within hours, days or weeks, symptoms may include:
  • Confusion or disorientation
  • Seizures or fits
  • Changes in personality and behaviour
  • Difficulty speaking
  • Weakness or loss of movement
  • Loss of consciousness

What is the most common cause of encephalitis?

In most cases, encephalitis is caused by a viral infection

Recall three causes of encephalitis.

  • Herpes Simplex
  • Measles
  • Varicella (chickenpox)
  • Rubella (German measles)

Recall three other rarer non-viral causes of encephalitis.

  • Mosquito, tick and other insect bites
  • Bacterial and fungal infections
  • Trauma
  • Autoimmune

Recall three treatments for encephalitis.

Treatment depends on the underlying cause, but may include: — Antivirals (i.e acyclovir) — Steroids — may reduce inflammation — Antibiotics/antifungals — Analgesics — Anti-convulsants (prophylactic) — Ventilation — last resort, used in states of hypoxia

What is multiple sclerosis?

  • Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic, immune-mediated disease that affects the central nervous system (CNS), particularly the brain and spinal cord — The body’s immune system mistakenly attacks the myelin sheath — This leads to inflammation and damage to the myelin, in what is known as demyelination
  • NOTE: Areas of damaged myelin form scar tissue (sclerosis), hence the term “multiple sclerosis,” meaning “multiple scars.”

Recall the four major types of multiple sclerosis.

  • Relapsing-Remitting Multiple Sclerosis (RRMS)
  • Primary Progressive Multiple Sclerosis (PPMS)
  • Secondary Progressive Multiple Sclerosis (SPMS)
  • Progressive-Relapsing Multiple Sclerosis (PRMS)
  • MS is classified on being either progressive/remitting and relapsing/persistent

What is Relapsing-Remitting Multiple Sclerosis (RRMS)?

  • It is the most common type of multiple sclerosis (MS) which initially affects ~85% of all MS sufferers
  • It is marked by episodes of new or worsening symptoms (relapses) followed by periods of partial or complete recovery (remissions)

What is Primary Progressive Multiple Sclerosis (PPMS)?

  • PPMS involves a gradual worsening of symptoms from the onset (progressive), without distinct relapses or remissions.
  • It accounts for approximately 10-15% of MS cases.

What is Secondary Progressive Multiple Sclerosis (SPMS)?

  • SPMS typically develops after an initial period of RRMS, where the disease shifts to a more steadily progressive course.
  • Over time, relapses become less frequent, and there is a continuous worsening of symptoms and disability.

What is Progressive-Relapsing Multiple Sclerosis (PRMS)?

  • This form is progressive from the start but also includes intermittent relapses or acute episodes of symptom worsening
  • PRMS is the least common type of MS

Multiple Sclerosis.

Recall the four features of the cellular pathology of multiple sclerosis.

  • Inflammation
  • Demyelination — damage to oligodendrocytes
  • Axonal loss
  • Neurodegeneration

Describe the role of the oligodendrocytes.

  • They allow signals to move faster across the nervous system - amplify the signal
  • They produce glia which produces myelin

Describe what an MRI scan of a patient with multiple sclerosis looks like.

  • An MRI of a patient with multiple sclerosis typically shows bright lesions (hyperintensities) on T2 weighted scans, particularly in the white matter of the brain and spinal cord
  • The white matter is located centrally, but the grey matter is more peripheral
  • NOTE: On a T2 weighted MRI scan, the white matter appears dark, but the grey matter appears light grey. CSF and lesions (with fluid) appear bright
