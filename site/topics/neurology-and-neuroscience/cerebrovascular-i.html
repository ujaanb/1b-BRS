10-20%

What percentage of oxygen does the brain use?

20%

What percentage of liver glucose does the brain use?

66%

  • Internal carotid artery
  • Vertebral artery

Which part of the brain does the internal carotid artery supply?

  • Anterior part of the brain
  • The carotid artery branches off the brachiocephalic artery

Which part of the brain does the vertebral artery supply?

  • Posterior part of the brain

Circle Of Willis.

Describe the path that blood takes during venous drainage of the brain.

  • The cerebral veins drain each part of the brain. They drain into the dural venous sinuses — These include the superior and inferior sagittal sinuses
  • The superior and inferior sagittal sinuses then drain into the internal jugular vein — via the transverse and sigmoid sinuses

What is the purpose of the dural venous sinuses?

The dural venous sinuses form the primary drainage pathways of the brain, ultimately draining into the internal jugular veins through the sigmoid veins

Meningeal Anatomy.

What is a burr hole?

A burr hole is a small surgical hole drilled into the skull, typically by a neurosurgeon, to access the brain

In which layer of the meninges is the Circle of Willis located?

Sub-arachnoid space

In which layer of the meninges is the middle meningeal artery located?

Extradural space — i.e on the surface of the dura, between the dura and the skull

Describe the vasculature of the extradural space.

The extradural space's vasculature is predominantly arterial, with the middle meningeal artery being the most important structure — Other vessels include the accessory meningeal, meningeal branches of the occipital, meningeal branches of the vertebral, and meningeal branches of the ascending pharyngeal artery

What usually causes extradural haemorrhages?

Trauma — i.e a blow to the pterion (superficial to the middle meningeal artery)

Describe the clinical presentation of extradural haemorrhages.

  • Extradural haemorrhages usually have immediate clinical effects
  • The pressure in the intracranial cavity builds up very quickly and the brain herniates through the foramen magnum
  • This is a medical emergency

Why do extradural haemorrhages have immediate clinical effects?

  • The extradural space is largely more arterial in vasculature than the subdural space
  • Since arteries have a generally have higher blood pressure than veins, an extradural haemorrhage would lead to the accumulation of blood and formation of an extradural haematoma much faster than a subdural or any other haemorrhage
  • Also, there is no anatomical space between the dura and the skull, so accumulation of blood within this area significantly increases intracranial pressure

What usually causes subdural haemorrhages?

Trauma

Describe and explain the clinical presentation of subdural haemorrhages.

  • Because the subdural space is much more venous than the extradural space, subdural haemorrhages take longer to form cerebral haematomas — This is also because of the anatomical subdural space, which allows blood to accumulate within it initially before intracranial pressure rises
  • There are hence delayed clinical effects

What usually causes subarachnoid haemorrhages?

Ruptured aneurysms — i.e in the Circle of Willis

What is an aneurysm?

  • An aneurysm is a bulge in a blood vessel caused by a weakness in the blood vessel wall, usually where it branches.
  • As blood passes through the weakened blood vessel, the blood pressure causes a small area to bulge outwards like a balloon

Describe the clinical presentation of subarachnoid

haemorrhages. • Sudden, severe headache ("thunderclap" headache) • Loss of consciousness (variable) • Neck stiffness (meningeal irritation) • Nausea, vomiting, and photophobia • Neurological deficits (e.g., weakness, visual disturbances, seizures) • Altered mental status or confusion • NOTE: Subarachnoid haemorrhage (SAH) is a medical emergency, and patients with suspected SAH require urgent evaluation and treatment to reduce the risk of severe complications, including death or permanent neurological damage

What usually causes intracerebral haemorrhages?

Chronic hypertension

Describe the clinical presentation of intracerebral haemorrhages.

Because intracerebral haemorrhages are often caused by chronic hypertension, they often present spontaneously

What is the main difference between the clinical presentation of extradural and subdural haemorrhages?

  • Extradural haemorrhages show immediate clinical effects
  • Subdural haemorrhages show delayed clinical effects

Types Of Haemorrhage.

What is a stroke?

A rapidly developing focal disturbance of brain function of presumed vascular origin and of ›24 hours duration

What is another name for a stroke?

Cerebrovascular accident (CVA)

What are the two types of stroke?

  • Haemorrhagic (leakage)
  • Thrombo-embolic (blockage)

Which of these is more common?

  • Thrombo-embolic (85%)
  • Haemorrhagic (15%)

What is the difference between thrombosis and an embolism?

  • Thrombosis is a blood clot that forms in a blood vessel
  • Embolism is plugging of small vessel by material carried from larger vessel — e.g. thrombi from the heart or atherosclerotic debris from the internal carotid

What is a transient ischaemic attack (TIA)?

A rapidly developing focal disturbance of brain function of presumed vascular origin that resolves completely within 24 hours

Describe the difference between a stroke and a transient ischaemic attack (TIA).

Both are cerebrovascular accidents which involve the loss of blood supply to the brain, however a stroke lasts for ›24 hours, and a TIA is resolved within 24 hours

What is cerebral ischaemia?

Lack of sufficient blood supply to nervous tissue resulting in permanent damage if blood flow is not restored quickly

Recall three key facts about strokes.

  • 3rd commonest cause of death
  • 100,000 deaths in UK per annum
  • 50% of survivors are permanently disabled
  • 70% show an obvious neurological deficit

What is the FAST scheme?

  • This is a mnemonic (F.A.S.T.) used to help people recognise the signs of a stroke quickly and act fast by calling 999
  • The signs include
  • F ace — Has their face fallen on one side? Can they smile?
  • A rms —

Can they raise both arms and keep them there?

  • S peech — Is their speech slurred?
  • T ime — Time to call 999 if you see any one of these signs

Recall three risk factors for strokes.

  • Age — i.e wear and tear to blood vessels
  • Hypertension — i.e risk factor for haemorrhagic strokes
  • Cardiac disease
  • Smoking
  • Diabetes mellitus

What is perfusion?

Perfusion is the passage of fluid through the circulatory system or lymphatic system to an organ or a tissue, usually referring to the delivery of blood to a capillary bed in tissue

Which aspect of the brain does the middle cerebral artery perfuse more of, the lateral or medial?

Lateral

Cerebral Artery Perfusion Fields (Superior).

Describe two problems that may arise with anterior cerebral artery damage.

  • Paralysis of contralateral structures (leg › arm, face) — This is due to the location of the primary motor cortex
  • Disturbance of intellect, executive function and judgement (abulia)
  • Loss of appropriate social behaviour

Why is paralysis of contralateral structures associated with anterior cerebral artery damage?

  • This is because the primary motor cortex is supplied by the anterior cerebral artery (and middle cerebral artery)
  • The lateral corticospinal tract which is responsible for the movement of arms and legs decussates at the medulla, which explains the contralateral paralysis of arms and legs

Which is more affected by anterior cerebral artery damage, arms or legs?

Legs — This is because of the somatotopic organisation within the primary motor cortex. The region within the primary motor cortex that is responsible for motor control of the legs is located medially, whereas the corresponding area for the arms is located more laterally (which is also perfused by the middle cerebral artery)
