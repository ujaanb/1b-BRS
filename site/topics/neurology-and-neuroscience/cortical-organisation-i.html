Overview

What is meant by the term cerebral cortex?

  • The term cerebral cortex refers to the outer layer of the brain.
  • It can be split into four regions

Describe the difference between the gyri and sulci of the brain.

  • Gyri refers to the ridges and bumps seen within the brain
  • Sulci refers to the folds seen in between these bumps

Gyri Vs. Sulci.

Recall the four lobes of the cerebral cortex.

  • Frontal
  • Parietal
  • Temporal
  • Occipital

What is the difference between grey matter and white matter?

  • Grey matter houses cell bodies
  • White matter houses tracts/axons (e.g corticospinal) — and involves myelinated axons, enables fast communication between grey matter

Answers.

Recall three main functions of the frontal lobe.

  • Regulating and initiating motor function
  • Language
  • Cognitive functions
  • Attention
  • Memory

Recall two main functions of the parietal lobe.

  • Sensation - touch, pain
  • Sensory aspects of language
  • Spatial orientation and self-perception

Recall the main function of the occipital lobe.

Processing visual information

Recall two main functions of the temporal lobe.

  • Processing auditory information
  • Emotions
  • Memories

Each Other.

Recall the structures that comprise the limbic lobe.

  • Amygdala
  • Hippocampus
  • Mamillary body
  • Cingulate gyrus

Recall four functions that the limbic lobe is responsible for.

  • Learning
  • Memory
  • Emotion
  • Motivation
  • Reward

The Limbic System.

Where is the insular cortex located?

  • Deep within the lateral fissure
  • The insular cortex is marked in green here

Recall three functions that the insular cortex (lobe) is responsible for.

  • Visceral sensations
  • Autonomic control
  • Interoception (i.e feeling hungry, thirsty)
  • Visual-vestibular integration

What is the lateral (Sylvian) fissure?

A small tear/opening in the brain in each hemisphere that separates the parietal and frontal lobes from the temporal lobe

Describe the relative positioning of the grey and white matter in the brain and spinal cord.

  • In the brain, grey matter is on the outside and white matter is on the inside
  • In the spinal cord, grey matter is on the inside and white matter is on the outside

What are glial cells?

  • Glial cells, also known as neuroglia, are non-neuronal cells in the central nervous system (CNS) and peripheral nervous system (PNS) that support and protect neurons providing structural support, nutrient delivery, and immune surveillance
  • NOTE: Microglia (neuronal macrophages) are a type of glial cell

Grey Vs. White Matter.

What are white matter tracts?

White matter tracts are bundles of nerve fibres in the brain and spinal cord that connect different areas and transmit information — These tracts act as pathways for communication between different brain regions, enabling coordinated function

What are the three types of white matter tract in the brain?

  • Association fibres
  • Commissural fibres
  • Projection fibres

What do association fibres do?

Connect areas within the same hemisphere

What do commissural fibres do?

Connect homologous structures in the left and right hemispheres

What do projection fibres do?

Connect the cortex with lower brain structures — i.e thalamus, brainstem and spinal cord

Recall three tracts that contain association fibres.

  • Superior longitudinal fasciculus
  • Arcuate fasciculus
  • Inferior longitudinal fasciculus
  • Uncinate fasciculus

What does the superior longitudinal fasciculus do?

Connects the frontal and occipital lobes

What does the arcuate fasciculus do?

Connects the frontal and temporal lobes

What does the inferior longitudinal fasciculus do?

Connects the temporal and occipital lobes

What does the uncinate fasciculus do?

Connects the frontal and temporal lobes

Association Fibres.

Recall two tracts that contain commissural fibres.

  • Corpus callosum
  • Anterior commissure

Commissural Fibres.

What are the two types of projection fibres?

  • Afferent (sensory)
  • Efferent (motor)

Describe the course of tracts carrying projection fibres.

  • Deeper (inferior) to the cortex, the tracts radiate and form the corona radiata
  • They then converge through the internal capsule between thalamus and basal ganglia before continuing to the brainstem or spinal cord depending on their function

What is the corona radiata?

This is a white matter tract in the brain that connects the cerebral cortex with the brainstem

Internal Capsule (2).

What are primary cortices of the brain?

Primary cortices are areas of the brain which are associated with specific functions related to sensory or motor processing — i.e primary motor cortex

What are secondary cortices of the brain?

Secondary/association cortices are areas of the brain which are associated with primary cortices — i.e supplementary motor area

Recall three differences between the primary and secondary cortices of the brain.

  • Primary cortices have predictable functions, secondary cortices are less predictable in function
  • Primary cortices are organised topographically, secondary cortices are not — i.e organised topographically means that a specific area can be mapped directly onto a function
  • Primary cortices are typically symmetrical between the left and right sides, secondary cortices are weakly symmetrical

In which lobe of the brain are the motor areas of the brain mainly located?

Frontal lobe

Recall the three motor areas of the frontal lobe.

  • Primary motor cortex
  • Supplementary area
  • Premotor area

Describe the positional relationship between the primary motor cortex, supplementary area and the premotor areas in the brain.

  • Primary motor cortex sits directly anterior to the central sulcus
  • Both the supplementary and premotor areas are directly anterior to the primary motor cortex, but the premotor area is lateral, while the supplementary area is medial

Describe the function of the primary motor cortex.

  • Controls fine, discrete, precise voluntary movements
  • Provides descending signals to execute movements

Describe the function of the premotor area.

Involved in planning movements — i.e externally cued events such as walking when traffic lights turn green

What is the function of the supplementary area?

Involved in planning complex movements — i.e internally cued events such as initiating speech in a conversation

Recall the two main cortices of the parietal lobe.

  • Primary somatosensory cortex
  • Somatosensory association cortex

Describe the function of the primary somatosensory cortex.

To process somatic sensations that arise from receptors in the body such as fine touch, vibration, two-point discrimination, proprioception, pain and temperature

Describe the function of the somatosensory association cortex.

  • To interpret the significance of sensory information — i.e recognising a particular object placed in hand
  • Awareness of self and awareness of personal space

Describe the positional relationship between the primary somatosensory cortex and the somatosensory association cortex in the brain.

  • The primary somatosensory cortex lies directly posterior to the central sulcus
  • The somatosensory association cortex is directly posterior to the primary somatosensory cortex

Recall the two main cortices of the occipital lobe.

  • Primary visual
  • Visual association

Describe the function of the primary visual cortex.

To process visual stimuli

Describe the function of the visual association cortex.

To give meaning and interpretation of visual input

Recall the two main cortices of the temporal lobe.

  • Primary auditory cortex
  • Auditory association cortex

Describe the function of the primary auditory cortex.

To process auditory stimuli

Describe the function of the auditory association cortex.

To give meaning and interpretation to auditory input

Describe the effects that frontal lobe lesions can have on sufferers.

  • Changes in personality
  • Inappropriate behaviour

Describe the effects that parietal lobe lesions can have on sufferers.

Contralateral neglect — i.e lesion in right hemisphere →lack of awareness of self on left side, lack of awareness of left side of extrapersonal space

Effects Of Contralateral Neglect.

What effect can temporal lobe lesions have on sufferers?

  • Inability to recognise objects, shapes, sounds or smells - agnosia
  • Inability to form new memories - anterograde amnesia
  • NOTE: This was discovered via a famous patient, Patient HM who had bilateral resection of anterior medial temporal lobe structures to cure epilepsy. They could not form new memories - anterograde amnesia

Lateral And Medial Aspects Of Temporal Lobe.

What effect can Broca’s area lesions have on sufferers?

Expressive aphasia - poor production of speech, comprehension intact

What effect can Wernicke’s area lesions have on sufferers?

Receptive aphasia - poor comprehension of speech, production is fine

Which white matter tract is associated with Broca’s and Wernicke’s area and which type of tract is it?

  • Arcuate fasciculus — (frontal → temporal)
  • This is a tract that carries association fibres BROCA’S AND WERNICKE’S AREAS.
