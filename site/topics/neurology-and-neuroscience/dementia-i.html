Overview

What is dementia?

  • Dementia is a general term for a severe loss of memory and other cognitive abilities which leads to impaired daily function (regardless of the underlying cause) — it is a syndrome, not a specific disease and can be thought of as “chronic brain failure”
  • It can also be described as a fatal neurodegenerative disorder characterised by progressive cognitive, social and functional impairment

How many people live with dementia worldwide?

46 million people

What is the most common cause of dementia?

The commonest cause of dementia is Alzheimer’s disease (AD)

Describe how dementia generally presents in patients.

  • This fatal neurodegenerative disorder is characterised by progressive cognitive, social and functional impairment
  • Diagnosis in life is only probable however this is changing Is there a cure for dementia?
  • There is no current cure, with acetylcholinesterase inhibitors having modest symptomatic benefit in early stages

Cognition In Dementia.

Describe why dementia is generally difficult to diagnose.

  • In old age, the presentation of dementia is often one of multiple co- morbidities
  • This means that other diseases are often associated with the dementia and so the presentation of the dementia can vary between patients — i.e vascular issues, systemic inflammation
  • Also, the disease follows a heterogenous course meaning that mutations in many different genes can cause the same disease — Lots of mixed and uncertain pictures

Which patients have a more “typical” presentation of dementia?

Younger patients are more typical

Recall three “neuro-inflammatory” risk factors for dementia.

  • Ageing
  • Oral health
  • Brain trauma
  • Infections or systemic inflammation
  • Genetic factors
  • Midlife obesity
  • Reduced physical activity

Brain Issues On Mri.

Describe the steps involved in treating patients with dementia from referral.

  • Referral
  • History
  • Examination
  • Investigations
  • Diagnosis
  • Management

Describe how a clinical interview/history can be used to aid the diagnosis of dementia.

  • A clinical interview can help us to gain greater insight into the severity of a patient’s condition, and whether or not it is necessary to investigate further
  • A collateral history may also be obtained to understand things when the patient lacks insight into their condition

Recall ten components of the clinical history that can help us to diagnose dementia.

  • Memory
  • Language
  • Numerical skills — important as patients may struggle to manage finances → increased risk of financial exploitation
  • Executive skills
  • Visuospatial skills
  • Neglect phenomena — failure to respond to stimuli despite having the necessary sensory and motor functions
  • Visual perception
  • Route finding and landmark identification
  • Personality and social conduct
  • Sexual behaviour
  • Eating
  • Mood
  • Motivation/Apathy
  • Anxiety/Agitation
  • Delusions/Hallucinations — i.e delusions of theft - delusions that patients hold are often not amenable to be challenged
  • Activities of daily living

Describe which examinations can be used to aid the diagnosis of dementia.

  • Neurological examinations — i.e cranial nerves II-XII
  • Mental State examinations — i.e this assesses appearance, behaviour, motor activity, speech, mood, affect, thought process, thought content, perceptual disturbances, cognition, insight, and judgment

Describe which investigations can be used to aid the diagnosis of dementia.

  • Neuropsychology
  • Blood Tests
  • MRI scans
  • PET scans

What is an MMSE in respect to the diagnosis of dementia?

The Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) is a brief 30-point questionnaire commonly used to screen for cognitive impairment and to help diagnose conditions like dementia.

Mini Mental State Examination (Mmse).

What is ACE in respect to the diagnosis of dementia?

The Addenbrooke's Cognitive Examination (ACE) is a comprehensive cognitive assessment tool often used in clinical settings to evaluate dementia and differentiate types of cognitive impairment, including Alzheimer's disease, frontotemporal dementia, and other neurodegenerative disorders

Recall five blood tests that can be used to aid the diagnosis of dementia.

  • Full Blood Count
  • Inflammatory markers — i.e to rule out secondary causes of disease
  • Thyroid function
  • Biochemistry and renal function
  • Glucose
  • Vitamin B12 and folate
  • Clotting
  • Syphilis serology
  • HIV
  • Caeruloplasmin

What is caeruloplasmin?

  • Caeruloplasmin is a protein in the blood that carries copper and plays a role in iron metabolism
  • It's produced in the liver and released into the bloodstream to transport copper to parts of the body that need it.
  • Low caeruloplasmin levels is indicative of Wilson's disease, copper deficiency, or certain liver diseases — i.e chronic hepatitis or cirrhosis
  • NOTE: Wilson's disease can lead to dementia or cognitive decline, though this is not the most common presentation. This is why it is tested for

What is the general purpose of these tests in the diagnosis of dementia?

These tests are used to rule out or identify any other possible causes of dementia secondary to those we are already aware of

What is an sMRI?

Structural Magnetic Resonance Imaging (sMRI)

Describe the features identifiable on an Alzheimer patient’s MRI scan.

  • Narrowed gyri, widened sulci
  • Dilated ventricles
  • Hippocampal volume loss — this explains the amnestic presentation of Alzheimer’s

Hippocampus On Mri.

What is Florbetapir?

Florbetapir is a radioactive tracer

Describe how Florbetapir is helpful to us in the diagnosis of dementia.

  • Florbetapir is used in positron emission tomography (PET) scans to help detect amyloid plaques in the brain, a hallmark of Alzheimer’s disease
  • Florbetapir binds specifically to amyloid-beta plaques, allowing these structures to become visible in PET images
  • This can help to improve diagnostic accuracy

Describe the specificity of amyloid and tau to Alzheimer’s disease.

Amyloid and tau are pathognomonic to Alzheimer’s disease

Recall four types/causes of dementia.

  • Alzheimer’s disease
  • Vascular dementia
  • Dementia with Lewy bodies
  • Frontotemporal dementia
  • Rapidly progressing dementias

Describe four ways we can manage the progression of dementia post- diagnosis.

  • Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors
  • Watch and wait
  • Treating behavioural/psychological symptoms
  • Occupational therapist/Social services
  • Specialist therapies

The Clinical Process With Reiterations.

What is Alzheimer’s disease?

  • Alzheimer’s disease is a progressive, neurodegenerative disorder that primarily affects memory, thinking, and behaviour
  • It is the most common form of dementia, accounting for 60-80% of all cases, and typically affects older adults, although early-onset cases

can occur

  • Alzheimer’s is caused by lower acetylcholine levels which is affected by amyloid plaques, NFTs and characterised by brain atrophy
  • NOTE: Acetylcholine plays a role in memory and learning within the brain

Describe the nature of the clinical presentation of Alzheimer’s.

Subtle, insidious amnestic/non amnestic presentation

What is the most important thing to obtain in a case where a patient lacks insight into their own condition?

Collateral history — if possible

What is meant by a collateral history?

A collateral history is information about a patient gathered from someone other than the patient, such as a family member, friend, carer, or other health professional

Describe why family members can be so integral to the diagnosis of dementia.

  • It is extremely common for sufferers of dementia to lack insight into their own condition
  • Family members who live with them and see them everyday are very useful, as they can provide useful insight into how debilitating their condition is to themselves and others on a day-to-day basis

What is the head-turning sign?

  • This is when a patient is asked a question and instead of/after responding, they look to their right or left for validation of their answer
  • It is a sign of mild cognitive impairment (MCI)

Can diagnosis of Alzheimer’s be certain during life?

  • Diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease can only be made certain at post- mortem
  • Diagnosis in life was only probable until recently – these criteria are changing given new ways of assessing in vivo pathology

Describe why dementia does not only affect the patient.

The disease takes it’s toll on partner/carer/family to a nearly equivalent amount

What is the entorhinal cortex?

This is a key brain region within the medial temporal lobe, acting as a crucial interface between the hippocampus and the neocortex — It is implicated in the pathophysiology of Alzheimer’s

Entorhinal Cortex.

What is amyloid?

  • Amyloid-beta (Aβ) is a protein fragment derived from a larger protein called amyloid precursor protein (APP), which is involved in cellular functions like signalling and growth
  • Under normal conditions, amyloid-beta fragments are produced in small amounts and cleared from the brain

Describe the role of amyloid in Alzheimer’s.

  • In Alzheimer’s, amyloid-beta fragments accumulate and clump together, forming sticky plaques between neurons
  • These amyloid plaques disrupt cell-to-cell communication, trigger inflammation, and contribute to neuron damage and death

Describe how amyloid PETs can aid us in the diagnosis of Alzheimer’s.

  • Amyloid PET scans can help us to identify the presence of amyloid within the brain, which is usually indicative of Alzheimer’s
  • A lack of amyloid indicates low likelihood of Alzheimer’s

What is tau?

Tau is a protein that stabilises microtubules, the structural “skeletons” within neurons that help transport nutrients and other important molecules along the neuron

Describe the role of tau in Alzheimer’s.

  • In Alzheimer’s and certain other neurodegenerative diseases, tau proteins undergo chemical changes that cause them to detach from microtubules and stick to each other, forming neurofibrillary tangles inside neurons.
  • These tangles disrupt the cell’s internal transport system, leading to nutrient deprivation, cellular dysfunction, and ultimately cell death.

Describe how amyloid and tau are useful to us in the diagnosis of Alzheimer’s.

  • Amyloid plaque buildup is one of the earliest detectable signs of Alzheimer’s, often appearing years before cognitive symptoms.
