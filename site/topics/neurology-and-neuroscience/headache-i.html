Overview

What is the one-year prevalence of headaches?

50% (1 in 2)

What percentage of neurologist consultations are for headaches?

20% (1 in 5)

Recall how many people are affected by headache disorders every day.

  • 100,000 people are absent from work or school everyday due to headaches
  • This makes it a highly disabling condition

Recall the two broad types of headache.

  • Primary headache
  • Secondary headache

What is a primary headache?

  • A primary headache is a type of headache that is not caused by another medical condition or underlying disease — They can be perceived as idiopathic headaches
  • Instead, the headache itself is the main problem, often stemming from issues related to pain-sensitive structures in the head, such as nerves, blood vessels, or muscles

Recall the three types of primary headache.

  • Migraines
  • Tension-type headaches
  • Trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias — Cluster headache

What is secondary headache?

  • A secondary headache is a headache that arises as a symptom of another underlying condition or medical issue — i.e subarachnoid haemorrhage
  • NOTE: Serious causes of secondary headache are uncommon

Compare the prevalence of primary headaches to secondary headaches.

Primary headaches are far more prevalent than secondary headaches

Describe a way that primary headaches can be classified.

  • Short-lasting headache
  • Long-lasting headache

Describe the difference between a short-lasting and a long-lasting headache.

  • A short-lasting headache lasts for ‹4 hours
  • A long-lasting headache lasts for ›4 hours

Recall the long-lasting primary headache(s).

  • Migraine
  • Tension-type headache

Recall the short-lasting primary headache(s).

Trigeminal autonomic cephalalgia — cluster headache

Describe the difference between a trigeminal autonomic cephalalgia and a cluster headache.

  • Trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias are a group of primary headaches that share features like unilateral pain, autonomic symptoms, and activation of the trigeminal-autonomic system
  • A cluster headache is a specific type of trigemnial autonomic cephalalgia, characterised by severe, unilateral pain around the eye, lasting 15 minutes to 3 hours, and occurring in episodes

Recall another type of headache.

  • Medication-overuse headache
  • NOTE: This type of headache is fairly common in clinical presentation and is often precipitated by medication such as opiates, paracetamol and ibuprofen

Describe the pathway in the clinical approach to tackling headaches.

  • First, a clinical history is taken and examinations are performed
  • If there is no red flag (indicating secondary headache) in the history or examinations, a preliminary diagnosis of primary headache can be made until there are any red flags — Investigation still continues
  • If there is a red flag in history or examination, a diagnosis of secondary headache can be made and the red flag will be further investigated
  • Diagnostic tests will be made to investigate the cause of the secondary headache

Describe what the letters in SNOOP stand for and hence recall the red flag symptoms for secondary headaches.

  • Systemic — fever, night sweats, weight loss, chills, myalgias, history of malignancy
  • Neurological — personality changes, diplopia, focal or global neurological symptoms, motor weakness, sensory loss, ataxia
  • Onset, sudden — reaches peak intensity in less than one minute (thunderclap)
  • Onset, age — new onset of the headaches aged ‹5 or ›65. These are both red flags
  • Pattern change — progressive headache or change in headache characteristics
  • Papilloedema — swollen eye discs - usually due to raised intracranial pressure. could be caused by brain tumour, intracranial bleeding etc.
  • Pregnancy — new-onset/change of headache during pregnancy
  • Phenotype of rare headache — Trigeminal autonomic cephalalgia; hypnic (occurs during sleep), exercise, cough or sex-induced

What is meant by a focal neurological symptom?

Focal neurological symptoms are symptoms that affect specific parts of the body, reflecting dysfunction in a particular area of the brain, spinal cord, or nervous system — i.e weakness/paralysis, speech difficulties, vision changes, coordination problems, seizures/altered consciousness — It could indicate a stroke, is not usually associated with primary headache and hence is a red flag symptom

What is a thunderclap headache?

A thunderclap headache is a headache that reaches its peak intensity in less than 1 minute

Describe how we can identify a pattern change in headaches clinically. Valsalva manouevre

  • This is a forced exhalation against a closed airway, typically with the mouth closed and nose pinched — intracranial pressure rises as a result of this
  • This could trigger a pattern change in headache

What type of disorder is a migraine?

  • Migraines are neurovascular disorders
  • This means that there are both neurological and vascular factors that contribute to the expression of symptoms seen in migraines

Describe the pathophysiology of migraines. Cortical Spreading Depression (CSD)

  • This is a wave of electrical activity followed by suppression
  • It primarily affects the occipital lobe leading to associated visual aura phase of migraines Trigeminal System Activation and CGRP Release
  • No clarity on where trigger in migraines begin - centrally or peripherally! But what we know is it involves the trigeminal and sphenopalatine ganglia and many key peptides of which the most important one to be aware of for now is the Calcitonin gene- related peptide (CGRP) which is released at several areas within the brain and peripherally!
  • CGRP causes blood vessel dilation (vasodilation) and promotes inflammation (neurogenic inflammation)
  • These factors contribute to the headache phase of migraines and are a different mechanism to aura
  • KEY TAKEAWAY: CGRP (Calcitonin-gene related peptide), released centrally and peripherally is the major implicated peptide in this system. It causes neurogenic inflammation and vasodilation → migraine EXTRA:

Pathophysiology Of Migraines.

Recall three characteristics of a migraine.

  • Unilateral location
  • Pulsating quality
  • Moderate or severe pain intensity
  • Aggravation by routine physical activity
  • Last hours and sometimes days!
  • With or without aura Is a migraine typically unilateral or bilateral?
  • Unilateral

Recall the two symptoms a patient usually has one or more of in order to be diagnosed with a migraine.

  • Nausea and/or vomiting
  • Photophobia and/or phonophobia

What is photophobia?

Photophobia is a medical symptom of abnormal intolerance to visual perception of light.

What is phonophobia?

Phonophobia is a fear of or aversion to loud sounds

What is meant by visual aura when associated with a migraine?

  • Visual aura is described as a complex array of symptoms reflecting focal cortical or brainstem dysfunction
  • They are related to the cortical spreading depression seen in the pathophysiology of migraines

Describe how visual aura is associated with a migraine.

  • Visual aura usually arrives before a migraine — Gradual evolution: 5-20 minutes (‹60minutes)
  • NOTE: Not all migraines have aura!

Recall the two types of aura.

  • Expanding ‘C’s
  • Elemental visual disturbance

Types Of Aura.

Recall the chronology of the four phases of a migraine.

  • Premonitory
  • Aura
  • Headache
  • Recovery

Describe what happens in each of the phases involved in a migraine.

  • Premonitory — Yawning, polyuria, mood change, irritable, light-sensitive, neck pain, difficulty concentrating
  • Aura — visual disturbances, sensory (numbness, paraesthesia), weakness, speech arrest
  • Headache — head and body pain, nausea, photophobia
  • Recovery — mood disturbed, food intolerance, feeling hungover — This phase can last for 48 hours

Phases Of Migraine.

Describe the steps involved in the management of migraines.

  • Lifestyle — Avoid triggers (i.e disruption in circadian rhythm) — Diet — Sleep — Exercise — Mindfulness
  • Pharmacological therapy
  • NOTE: Lifestyle changes are the first line of treatment for headache

Recall the two ways that pharmacological therapy is used to manage migraines.

  • Acute/abortive therapy
  • Long-term/preventative therapy

Describe how acute/abortive pharmacological management of migraines works.

Acute/abortive pharmacological management works “hard and fast” to offset symptoms of a headache at that particular time

Recall three acute/abortive therapies for migraines.

  • Paracetamol
  • NSAIDs (high dose and soluble)
  • Prokinetics (anti-emetic) — increases GI motility, prevents vomiting (symptom of migraines)
  • Triptans (5-HT1B/1D/1F (serotonin) receptor agonists) — They block the release of inflammatory neuropeptides, such as calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), which contribute to inflammation and pain

Describe how long term/preventative pharmacological management of migraines works.

  • Long term/preventative pharmacological management works strategically to reduce the impact migraines may have on an individual going forward
  • › 5 days/month
  • “low and slow” with doses until at optimal dose

Recall five pharmacological migraine preventatives for migraines.

  • Tricyclic anti-depressants (TCA)
  • Beta-blockers
  • Serotonin antagonists
  • Anticonvulsants
  • Calcium channel blockers
  • Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor
  • Angiotensin II receptor blockers
  • Non-prescription
  • Parenteral
  • CGRP Antibodies – new medications on the block for preventing migraine

Which drugs should be avoided in the treatment of headaches?

Opiate-based and mixed analgesics should be avoided

What is a tension-type headache?

A tension-type headache is a type of headache which feels very restrictive for the patient

Describe the main clinical presentation of a tension-type headache.

Patients usually say it feels like they have tight muscles around the head and neck, as though their head is in a vice

Describe how long a tension-type headache lasts for.

They generally last for 30 minutes but can be hours long

Recall three clinical features of a tension-type headache.

  • Bilateral
  • Mild or moderate
  • Not aggravated by movement No added features typically
  • No nausea or vomiting
  • No photophobia or phonophobia

Describe the treatment given for tension-type headaches.

  • Reassurance may suffice in the majority of patients — i.e tell them it’s infrequent
  • Individual attacks can be treated with simple analgesics such as Aspirin or Paracetamol
  • Preventative medications rarely required

What is a cluster headache?

The most severe type of headache that is characterised by severe unilateral pain — i.e each episode always affects just one side

How long does a cluster headache last for?

It can last for 15-180 minutes if left untreated — short-lasting

Recall three clinical features of a cluster headache. At least one of the following, ipsilaterally:

  • Conjunctival redness and/or lacrimation
  • Nasal congestion and/or rhinorrhoea
  • Eyelid oedema — These are autonomic symptoms Also:
  • Forehead and facial sweating
  • Miosis and/or ptosis
  • A sense of restlessness or agitation
  • Not associated with a brain lesion on MRI
  • NOTE: At least one of the above present in cluster headaches

What is rhinorrhoea?

Rhinorrhea (runny nose) is mucus (snot) dripping or “running” out of your nose

What is miosis?

Miosis of the eye refers to having small or constricted pupils
