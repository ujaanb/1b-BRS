Overview

Describe what is meant by hierarchical organisation of motor function.

  • The hierarchy goes from high order to low order
  • High order areas of hierarchy are involved in more complex tasks — i.e coordination of muscle activity, programming movements
  • Low order areas of hierarchy are involved in less complex tasks — i.e execution of movement

Describe what is meant by functional segregation of motor function.

Functional segregation refers to how the different areas of the motor system are organised in a number of different areas which are involved in the control of different aspects of movement

What is the basal ganglia?

The basal ganglia are a group of structures linked to the thalamus in the base of the brain and are primarily involved in coordination of movement

Basal Ganglia.

What is the primary motor cortex?

The primary motor cortex is a region of the frontal lobe of the brain that controls voluntary movement

Primary Motor Cortex.

What is the non-primary motor cortex?

The non-primary motor cortex (nPMC) is a part of the frontal lobe of the brain that is involved in movement planning, initiation, and selection

Recall the two regions of the non-primary motor cortex.

  • Supplementary motor area
  • Premotor area

Non-Primary/Supplementary Motor Cortex.

Describe the difference between the primary and non-primary motor cortex.

  • The primary motor cortex is essential for executing movements by sending direct signals to muscles
  • The non-primary motor cortex (premotor area and supplementary motor area) are crucial for planning, preparing, and coordinating movements, particularly complex and learned motor sequences

Which is a higher-order region, the primary or non-primary motor cortex?

  • The non-primary cortex is considered a higher-order region than the primary motor cortex
  • This is because it is involved in the planning of movements and control of complex, coordinated movements — i.e it is involved in more complex function than the primary motor cortex

Primary Motor Cortex.

Describe the role of the cerebellum in the motor system.

  • The cerebellum and basal ganglia adjust the commands received from other parts of the motor control system — For example, when a signal exits from the motor cortex, it will go to the basal ganglia and cerebellum for processing and modulation
  • The cerebellum also has direct motor function by sending signals from the cerebellum to the brainstem, which passes commands to the spinal cord (or muscles of the face and neck)

Describe the role of the thalamus in the motor system.

The thalamus acts as a central relay station in the motor system, receiving motor information from various brain regions like the basal ganglia and cerebellum, then transmitting back it to the motor cortex

Describe the role of the brainstem in the motor system.

The brainstem passes commands from the cortex to the spinal cord — i.e corticospinal and corticobulbar tracts pass through the brainstem

Motor System Hierarchy.

Describe the structural difference between a pyramidal and an extrapyramidal tract.

  • Pyramidal tracts pass through the pyramids of the medulla and instead run from the motor cortex to the brainstem to the spinal cord
  • Extrapyramidal tracts do not pass through the pyramids of the medulla and instead run from the brainstem to the spinal cord

Describe the functional difference between a pyramidal and an extrapyramidal tract.

  • Pyramidal tracts are responsible for voluntary movements of the body and face
  • Extrapyramidal tracts are responsible for involuntary (automatic) movements for balance, posture and locomotion

Medullary Pyramid.

Recall two pyramidal tracts.

  • Corticospinal tract
  • Corticobulbar tract

Corticospinal Tract (Schematic).

Describe the general course of the main pyramidal tracts.

  • The pyramidal tracts originate within the primary motor cortex (within pre-central gyrus) to synapse at either the spinal cord (corticospinal) or cranial nerve nuclei in the brainstem (corticobulbar)
  • Their lower motor neurones then go on to innervate the muscles associated with them

Describe the role of the pyramidal tracts.

Voluntary movements of body and face

Describe the role of the extrapyramidal tracts.

Involuntary (automatic) movements for balance, posture and locomotion

Recall the four extrapyramidal tracts.

  • Vestibulospinal
  • Tectospinal
  • Reticulospinal
  • Rubrospinal

Describe the general course of the extrapyramidal tracts.

  • All of the non-pyramidal tracts originate within the brainstem
  • Their upper motor neurones originate within a specific area of the brainstem before synapsing at the ventral horn of the spinal cord
  • Their lower motor neurones then go on to innervate the relevant muscle ORIGIN OF EXTRAPYRAMIDAL TRACTS (”EXTRAPYRAMIDAL CORTEX”).

Describe the difference between the gyri and sulci of the brain.

  • Gyri refers to the ridges and bumps seen within the brain
  • Sulci refers to the folds seen in between these bumps

Where exactly is the primary motor cortex located?

  • Precentral gyrus of the frontal lobe
  • This is located anterior to the central sulcus

What does the primary motor cortex do?

The primary motor cortex is responsible for the control of fine, discrete, precise voluntary movements

Where is the premotor area located?

Anterior to the primary motor cortex — lateral side

What does the premotor area do?

It is involved in planning movements and regulating externally cued movements

Premotor Area.

Describe what is meant by an externally cued movement.

An externally cued movement is a type of movement that is initiated or guided by an external stimulus or environmental event. — This could be something visual, auditory, tactile, or any other kind of sensory signal that prompts the body to react or move

Recall an example of an externally cued movement.

  • Catching a ball when you see it flying towards you (VISUAL)
  • Moving when you hear a buzzer in a game or race (AUDITORY)
  • Flinching or withdrawing your hand after feeling a sudden touch or heat (TACTILE)

Describe what is meant by an internally cued movement.

An internally cued movement is a type of movement that is initiated or guided by a person's internal decision or intention, rather than in response to an external stimulus

Recall an example of an internally cued movement.

  • Reaching for a cup because you feel thirsty
  • Deciding to stand up and walk across the room
  • Typing on a keyboard based on the internal thought process of

what you want to say Describe the difference between internally and externally cued movements.

Internally cued movements are voluntary and originate from within (i.e spontaneously planning to move), while externally cued movements occur in response to an outside stimulus (i.e responding to a sound or visual cue)

Where is the supplementary motor area located?

Anterior to the primary motor cortex — medial side

Describe the role of the supplementary motor area (SMA).

  • It is involved in planning complex movements — i.e internally cued movements — It hence becomes active prior to voluntary movement
  • It is also involved in planning speech

What is the corticospinal tract?

  • This is the major descending pathway for voluntary movement that descends from the brain to the spinal cord
  • It is composed of motor neurones that pass through the spinal cord to innervate lower motor neurons which then innervate muscles of the trunk and limbs

What are the two divisions of the corticospinal tract?

  • Anterior corticospinal tract
  • Lateral corticospinal tract

What do these two divisions innervate?

  • Anterior corticospinal tract - trunk muscles
  • Lateral corticospinal tract - limb muscles

What is the difference between the structure of the two divisions of the corticospinal tract?

  • Anterior corticospinal tract - nerves do not decussate at medulla — i.e right primary motor cortex innervates right sided trunk muscles
  • Lateral corticospinal tract - nerves decussate at medulla — i.e right primary motor cortex innervates left sided limb muscles

Which of these divisions are more abundant in nerve fibres?

  • Lateral corticospinal tract (85-90%)
  • Anterior corticospinal tract (10-15%)

What is a cerebral peduncle?

The cerebral peduncles are paired structures at the front of the midbrain that connect the cerebrum (not cerebellum) to the brainstem

Cerebral Peduncle.

What does the concept of somatotopy describe in the nervous system?

  • It describes how specific regions of the nervous system are involved in controlling specific areas of the body
  • i.e the primary motor cortex is involved in controlling voluntary movements. Since all the cells are located in a similar region, this is described as somatotopy.

What does the motor homunculus tell us?

The motor homunculus tells us approximately how much of the brain is devoted to the control of a specific part of the body

Motor Homunculus.

What is the corticobulbar tract?

  • The major pathway that is used to innervate from the primary motor cortex to the muscles of our face and neck
  • The nerves involved in this pathway are the cranial nerves
  • It is used to control voluntary movement of these face and neck muscles

Describe why the corticobulbar tract has its name.

  • Corticobulbar can be broken down into “cortico” and “bulb”
  • These are the areas from which all the nerves in the corticobulbar tract originate from
  • NOTE: Cortico = cortex, Bulb = old name for brainstem

What is the difference between a nucleus and a ganglion?

  • Ganglia and nuclei are both structures that are used to describe a cluster of neuronal cell bodies
  • However, the main difference between the two is that nuclei lie exclusively within the central nervous system (CNS) while ganglia lie in the peripheral nervous system (PNS).
  • NOTE: Central nervous system (CNS) = brain and spinal cord

What does the oculomotor nerve (CN III) innervate?

Extraocular muscles

What does the trochlear nerve (CN IV) innervate?

Extraocular muscles

What does the trigeminal nerve (CN V) innervate?

Muscles of mastication

What does the abducens nerve (CN VI) innervate?

Extraocular muscles

What does the facial nerve (CN VII) innervate?

Muscles of facial expression

What does the hypoglossal nerve (CN IX) innervate?

Muscles of the tongue

Corticobulbar Tract.

Recall the four brainstem tracts that are not under voluntary control.

  • Vestibulospinal
  • Tectospinal
  • Reticulospinal
  • Rubrospinal

Describe a distinct property that the lower motor neurones of the extrapyramidal tracts have.

Multiple upper motor neurones from different pathways (i.e the corticospinal tract + extrapyramidal tracts like the rubrospinal)

can synapse onto a single lower motor neurone

  • This phenomenon is known as convergence
  • Hence, lower motor neurones that innervate muscles are often shared by upper motor neurones in these pathways (corticospinal + extrapyramidal)
  • NOTE: The upper motor neurones of the extrapyramidal tracts originate from the brainstem

Describe the purpose of the vestibulospinal tract.

  • To stabilise the head during body movements, or as the head moves
  • To coordinate head movements with eye movements
  • To mediate postural adjustments

Describe the purpose of the reticulospinal tract.

  • Changes in muscles tone are associated with voluntary movement
  • To control postural stability

Recall the origin of the tectospinal tract.

Superior colliculus of midbrain

Describe the purpose of the tectospinal tract.

To control orientation of head and neck during eye movements

Superior Colliculus.

Describe the purpose of the rubrospinal tract.

  • To innervate the lower motor neurons of the upper limb flexor muscles
  • NOTE: In humans, the function of the rubrospinal tract is largely taken over by the corticospinal tract

What is the difference between a positive and negative sign of neuronal lesion?

  • A positive sign is when something that shouldn’t be happening is happening
  • A negative sign is when something that should be happening isn’t happening

Recall three negative signs of an upper motor neuron lesion.

  • Loss of voluntary motor function
  • Paresis: Graded weakness of movements
  • Paralysis/Plegia: Complete loss of voluntary muscle activity
  • NOTE: Paresis = partial paralysis

Describe a role of upper motor neurones secondary to muscle contraction.

Upper motor neurons (UMNs) can inhibit motor actions, such as spinal cord reflexes, while also stimulating other motor actions

Recall three positive signs of an upper motor neuron lesion.

  • Increased abnormal motor function due to loss of inhibitory descending inputs
  • Spasticity: increased muscle tone
  • Hyper-reflexia (exaggerated reflexes)
  • Clonus (abnormal oscillatory muscle contraction) — NOTE: This could be in response to contraction of another muscle
  • Babinski’s sign
  • NOTE: Most of these signs are attributed to the loss of inhibitory function usually gained via the upper motor neurones

What is clonus?

Clonus is an abnormal reflex response that involves involuntary and rhythmic muscle contractions. — It can be a sign of an underlying medical condition, such as a brain injury or cerebral palsy.

What is Babinski’s sign?

  • Babinski's sign, also known as the extensor plantar reflex, is a reflex that occurs when the sole of the foot is stroked and the big toe moves upward and the other toes fan out
  • It's a normal reflex in infants and children under two years old, but it's abnormal in children older than two and adults
  • In adults, it can indicate a problem with the brain or spinal cord.

Describe what Babinski’s sign looks like.

  • Babinski sign involves the stimulation of the sole of the foot with a blunt instrument
  • Usually, all of the toes should curl downwards
  • In a positive Babinski sign, the big toe curls upwards

What is apraxia?

A disorder of skilled movement where patients are not paretic but have lost information about how to perform skilled movements

Describe the causes of apraxia.

Any disease of the premotor and supplementary motor areas (SMAs) can cause apraxia, although stroke and dementia are the most common causes — i.e inferior parietal lobe, frontal lobe

Recall three signs of a lower motor neuron lesion.

  • Weakness
  • Hypotonia (reduced muscle tone)
  • Hyporeflexia (reduced reflexes)
  • Muscle atrophy — nerves deliver neurotrophic factors to muscles
  • Fasciculations — damaged motor units produce spontaneous action potentials, resulting in a visible twitch
  • Fibrillations — spontaneous twitching of individual muscle fibres; recorded during needle electromyography examination
  • NOTE: During a needle EMG, a needle electrode inserted directly into a muscle records the electrical activity in that muscle

What is motor neurone disease (MND)?

Motor neurone disease is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder of the motor system

Recall another name for motor neurone disease (MND).

Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS)

Describe what happens in motor neurone disease (MND).

In motor neurone disease, there is damage to both upper and lower motor neurones which leads to more severe degenerative alterations to the CNS

Recall three signs of motor neurone disease attributed to upper motor neurone damage.

  • Spasticity (increased tone of limbs and tongue)
  • Brisk limbs and jaw reflexes
  • Babinski’s sign
  • Loss of dexterity
  • Dysarthria (difficulty speaking)
  • Dysphagia (difficulty swallowing)

Describe three signs of motor neurone disease attributed to lower motor neurone damage.

  • Weakness
  • Muscle wasting
  • Tongue fasciculations and wasting
  • Nasal speech —

when air escapes through the nose while speaking

Dysphagia

Motor Neurone Disease (Mnd).

What are the basal ganglia?

The basal ganglia are a group of subcortical nuclei (clusters of neurons) located deep within the brain, playing a critical role in regulating motor control, motor learning, and various other functions such as behaviour and emotion

Describe the functions of the basal ganglia.

  • The basal ganglia do not directly initiate movement but are essential for modulating movements initiated by the motor cortex, ensuring they are smooth and purposeful
  • They are responsible for:
  • Decision to move
  • Elaborating associated movements — i.e swinging arms when walking, changing facial expression to match emotions
  • Moderating and coordinating movement (suppressing unwanted movements)
  • Performing movements in chronological order

Recall three structures within the basal ganglia.

  • Caudate nucleus
  • Lentiform nucleus
  • Nucleus accumbens
  • Olfactory tubercle
  • Globus pallidus
  • Subthalamic nucleus
  • Substantia nigra
  • Putamen

What is the caudate nucleus?

The caudate nucleus is a C-shaped structure in the brain that plays a key role in higher neurological functions

Caudate Nucleus.

What is the lentiform nucleus?

The lentiform nucleus describes the putamen and the external global pallidus together

The substantia nigra is a small, essential part of the brain that controls movement, learning, and reward

Substantia Nigra.

What is Parkinson’s disease?

Parkinson's disease is a progressive brain condition that causes movement problems, mental health issues, and other health concerns

What happens to the brain in Parkinson’s disease?

In Parkinson’s, there is degeneration of the dopaminergic neurons that originate in the substantia nigra and project to the striatum

Describe the difference between the rostral and caudal

substantia nigra. • The rostral substantia nigra's degeneration is more directly linked to the classic motor symptoms of Parkinson's disease ◦ i.e bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor • The caudal substantia nigra is associated with non-motor symptoms, including mood changes, reward-processing deficits, and cognitive effects seen in advanced Parkinson’s disease and other neuropsychiatric conditions.

Recall three clinical symptoms of Parkinson’s disease.

  • Bradykinesia — slowness of (small) movements (doing up buttons, handling a knife)
  • Hypomimic face — i.e expressionless, mask-like (absence of movements that normally animate the face)
  • Akinesia — difficulty in the initiation of movements because cannot initiate movements internally
  • Rigidity — muscle tone increase, causing resistance to externally imposed joint movements
  • Tremor at rest — 4-7 Hz, starts in one hand (“pill-rolling tremor”); with time spreads to other parts of the body

Basal Ganglia Circuitry.

What is Huntington’s disease?

  • Huntington's disease (Huntington’s chorea) is an incurable neurodegenerative disease that is usually inherited
  • It is a genetic neurodegenerative disorder that is autosomal dominant in nature caused by a CAG repeat on chromosome 4

What happens in Huntington’s disease?

Degeneration of GABAergic neurons in the striatum, caudate and then putamen

Describe the function of GABA when it acts on GABAergic neurons.

  • GABA (Gamma-Aminobutyric Acid) is the main inhibitory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system (CNS).
  • Its primary function is to reduce neuronal excitability by inhibiting nerve transmission, helping maintain a balance between neuronal excitation and inhibition

Recall four clinical symptoms of Huntington’s disease.

  • Choreic movements — i.e chorea = dance
  • Rapid jerky involuntary movements
  • Speech impairment
  • Difficulty swallowing
  • Unsteady gait
  • Later stages, cognitive decline and dementia

What is ballism?

Ballism or ballismus consists of sudden, uncontrolled flinging of the extremities

What causes ballism?

Ballism is usually caused by a stroke affecting the subthalamic nucleus

How do symptoms present in ballism?

Symptoms often occur contralaterally

Describe the role of the subthalamic nucleus.

In essence, the subthalamic nucleus serves as a critical regulatory component within the basal ganglia, helping balance motor and non-motor functions — Its modulatory role is essential for smooth, controlled movement and behavioural flexibility

Subthalamic Nucleus.

Where in the skull is the cerebellum located?

Cerebellum.

Describe the role of the cerebellum.

  • The cerebellum can be thought of as the coordinator and predictor of movements — It compares movements that are on their way to muscles and then receives the feedback from sensory organs — It can then provide information to the CNS on whether certain movements need adjustments/refining
  • It basically fine tunes movements

  • Vestibulocerebellum Damage to which part of the cerebellum is closely associated with chronic alcoholism?
  • Spinocerebellum Damage to which part of the cerebellum is closely associated with tremor?
  • Cerebrocerebellum

Cerebrocerebellum.

Recall three main signs of cerebellar dysfunction.

  • Ataxia — disturbances of posture or gait: wide-based, staggering (“drunken”) gait
  • Dysmetria
  • Intention tremor
  • Dysdiadochokinesia
  • Scanning speech

What is ataxia?

  • Ataxia is a term for a group of disorders that affect co- ordination, balance and speech.
  • Areas affected also include general impairments in movement coordination and accuracy, disturbances of posture or gait: wide-based, staggering (“drunken”) gait

What is dysmetria?

Inappropriate force and distance for target-directed movements — i.e knocking over a cup rather than grabbing it

What is intention tremor?

Increasingly oscillatory trajectory of a limb in a target-directed movement

What is dysdiadochokinesia?

Inability to perform rapidly alternating movements — i.e rapidly pronating and supinating hands and forearms

Pronation And Supination.

What is scanning speech?

Staccato, due to impaired coordination of speech muscles — i.e The sentence "Walking is good exercise", for example, might be pronounced as "Walk ing is good ex er cise”

What is an alpha motor neurone?

Alpha motor neurones describe the lower motor neurons of the brainstem and spinal cord

What do alpha motor neurones innervate?

  • They innervate the extrafusal muscle fibres of the skeletal muscles
  • NOTE: Extrafusal muscle fibres are the majority of skeletal muscle fibres responsible for generating force and movement

What does activation of an alpha motor neuron lead to?

Activation of an alpha motor neuron leads to muscle contraction

What is a motor neuron pool?

A motor neuron pool describes all the alpha motor neurons innervating a single skeletal muscle

Recall the two types of lower motor neurone.

Alpha motor neurone • Gamma motor neurone • NOTE: Together, alpha and gamma motor neurons work in tandem, with alpha neurons driving movement and gamma neurons fine-tuning the sensory feedback to ensure coordinated and smooth motor control.

What is a motor unit?

  • A single motor neuron together with all the muscle fibres that it innervates (supplies)
  • There is never a case where multiple neurons innervate one muscle fibre
  • NOTE: Multiple muscle fibres make up a muscle On average how many muscle fibres does a motor neuron innervate?
