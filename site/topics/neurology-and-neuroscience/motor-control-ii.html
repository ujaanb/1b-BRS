Overview

Recall three signs of an upper motor neuron lesion.

  • Tone increased (spastic) — stiff muscles
  • Clonus present
  • Power weakness — pyramidal distribution flexors stronger than extensors in Upper Limb and extensors stronger than flexors in Lower Limb
  • Reflexes brisk
  • Extensor plantar responses
  • No wasting
  • No fasciculations

Explain why the flexors are stronger than extensors in the arm

and extensors are stronger than flexors in the leg when there is a lower motor neuron lesion. • Some muscles have more pathways (backup systems) from the brain helping them out - extra support or duplicate signals to help control certain muscles • This “backup” is from extrapyramidal pathways such as the vestibulospinal tract, reticulospinal tract and rubrospinal tracts • Because biceps are more essential for survival stuff, your brain has more connections going to them. So even if you lose some UMN input, they’re still working okay-ish. But triceps? Fewer pathways → they weaken faster ◦ Rubrospinal tracts help innervate the lower motor neurons of the upper limb flexor muscles • Quads have stronger brain input to help you stay upright. They get more "backup". So when UMNs get damaged, hamstrings lose out more. ◦ Vestibulospinal and reticulospinal tracts help maintain postural stability, and they help mitigate the effects of motor neurone disease • NOTE: In classic presentations of motor neurone disease, extrapyramidal tracts are not typically affected

Describe the purpose of the vestibulospinal tract.

  • To stabilise the head during body movements, or as the head moves
  • To coordinate head movements with eye movements
  • To mediate postural adjustments

Describe the purpose of the reticulospinal tract.

  • Changes in muscles tone are associated with voluntary movement
  • To control postural stability

Describe the purpose of the rubrospinal tract.

  • To innervate the lower motor neurons of the upper limb flexor muscles
  • NOTE: In humans, the function of the rubrospinal tract is largely taken over by the corticospinal tract

What is the extensor plantar response?

This is Babinski’s sign

Why is there slurred speech and dysphagia in motor neurone disease?

This is because of the corticobulbar tract impairment which leads to weakening of the pharyngeal muscles and tongue muscles

Recall three signs of a lower motor neuron lesion.

  • Tone flaccid
  • Power weakness (distal weaker than proximal)
  • No clonus
  • Reflexes reduced or absent
  • Flexor plantar responses
  • Wasting may be present
  • Fasciculations may be present

Monosynaptic Reflex.

Describe how transmission of nervous impulses occurs in the reflex arc, using the patellar tendon reflex as an example.

  • Tendon hammer hits the tendon in the kneecap, causing the tendon and hence the muscles in the thigh (quadriceps) to stretch as they are pulled down
  • This change in muscle length stimulates the sensory receptors in the muscles (muscle spindles) and signal is sent from this sensory neuron from the muscle — A sensory signal is sent from the muscle to the brain, allowing us to feel the hammer — A sensory signal is sent from the muscle to the spinal cord, synapsing with a motor neuron which tells the muscles in the thigh to contract again after they have been hit to return their length back to normal (the reflex)
  • This quadricep contraction leads to movement of the lower leg
  • NOTE: Muscle spindles are sensory receptors within skeletal muscles that detect changes in muscle length and the rate of change in length
  • SUMMARY: Stimulus → Sensory nerve activation → Transmission to brain and spinal motor neuron → Activation of motor neuron → Muscle contraction

Describe a common defect in reflexes with corticospinal tract lesions.

  • Abdominal reflexes (superficial reflexes) are absent — Superficial reflexes are not monosynaptic, as they involve interneurons between the sensory and motor neurones involved in the reflex. (i.e sensory neurons → interneurons → motor neurons) — Superficial reflexes need corticospinal tract input to show up, unlike deep tendon reflexes which are simply modulated by the corticospinal tract
  • Other reflexes (deep tendon reflexes) are brisk
  • NOTE: Stroking the skin of the abdomen causes the abdominal wall muscles to contract, sometimes pulling the umbilicus towards the stimulus. These reflex contractions are often absent in upper motor neurone disorders

What is a fasciculation?

  • A fasciculation is an involuntary and abnormal firing of a single motor neuron and all its innervated muscle fibres
  • They are visible to the eye

Explain how denervation and reinnervation can cause fasciculations.

  • This case is about denervation and reinnervation
  • This can be demonstrated by miming two motor units side by side in a muscle with your arms
  • Hold up both your hands - they are two motor neurones
  • Spread out your fingers - they are the lower motor neurone axon sprouts
  • Each axon sprout innervates several muscle fibres (the motor axon and all the muscle fibres it innervates is the motor unit )
  • Cortical command comes down the motor neurones and causes the muscle fibres to contract in a coordinated fashion
