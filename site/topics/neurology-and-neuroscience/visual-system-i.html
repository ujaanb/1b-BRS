Overview

What is the cornea?

  • The cornea is the transparent, dome-shaped outer layer at the very front of the eye
  • It covers the iris (the coloured part of the eye), the pupil (the black centre), and the anterior chamber (the fluid-filled space behind the cornea and in front of the iris)

Cornea.

What is the palpebral fissure?

The palpebral fissure is the space between the open eyelids, also known as the opening between the upper and lower eyelids

Palpebral Fissure.

What is the canthus?

The canthus is either corner of the eye where the upper and lower eyelids meet

What is the limbus?

  • The limbus is the border area in the eye where the cornea (meets the sclera (the white part of the eye)
  • NOTE: In terms of the visible eye, the cornea only covers the iris and the pupil

What is the caruncle?

Also known as the lacrimal caruncle, this is a small, pink, fleshy nodule located in the inner corner (medial canthus) of the eye

Eye Anatomy.

Recall the three layers in the coat of the eye from outermost to innermost.

  • Sclera — hard and opaque
  • Choroid — pigmented and vascular
  • Retina — neurosensory tissue

What is the sclera?

The sclera, commonly referred to as the “white of the eye”, is the tough, protective tissue that serves as the eye’s protective outer coat

Describe a property of the sclera.

  • It is hard and opaque, and has a high water content
  • This helps it to perform its protective duties

What is the choroid?

The choroid is a layer of the eye located between the sclera (the outer white layer) and the retina (the light-sensitive layer at the back of the eye)

Describe the function of the choroid.

The choroid is rich in blood vessels, and its primary function is to supply oxygen and nutrients to the outer layers of the retina, which are crucial for maintaining vision

What is the uvea?

The uvea is the vascular coat of the eyeball and it lies between the sclera and retina

Recall the three parts of the uvea.

  • Iris
  • Ciliary body
  • Choroid

Describe the structure of the uvea.

  • The uvea is composed of three parts – iris, ciliary body and choroid
  • These parts are all intimately connected

What implications does the intimate connections of all the uveal parts have?

The intimate connection of the iris, ciliary body and choroid means that a disease of one part also affects the other portions though not necessarily to the same degree

Sclera.

What is the iris?

The iris is the coloured, circular part of the eye

Describe the function of the iris.

It controls the size of the pupil (the black centre of the eye) and thus regulates the amount of light that enters the eye

What is the ciliary body?

The ciliary body is a circular structure in the eye located just behind the iris and is part of the uvea

Describe the function of the ciliary body.

  • It plays a central role in focusing, maintaining eye shape, and regulating intraocular pressure by producing the fluid inside the eye
  • As part of the uveal tract, the ciliary body is also intimately connected to the iris and choroid, supporting overall eye function and vision

What is the retina?

  • The retina is a very thin, light-sensitive layer of tissue that lines the inner part of the eye
  • QUOTE: “The wallpaper at the back of the eye”

Describe the role of the retina.

  • The retina is responsible for capturing the light rays that enter the eye, much like the film's role in photography
  • These light impulses are converted to electrical impulses and sent to the brain for processing, via the optic nerve

Retina.

What is the optic nerve?

The optic nerve, also known as cranial nerve II, is responsible for transmitting visual information from the retina to the brain

Describe the role of the optic nerve.

  • The optic nerve transmits electrical impulses from the retina to the brain
  • NOTE: The retina has converted the light impulses → electrical impulses

Describe the course of the optic nerve.

  • The optic nerve runs from the back of the eye (retina) → primary visual cortex (brain)
  • During its course, it crosses at the optic chiasm, enters the optic tracts before travelling in the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) → primary visual cortex (occipital lobe)

What is the visible portion of the optic nerve called?

The visible portion of the optic nerve is called the optic disc

Why is the optic disc known as the blind spot?

  • The optic disc is known as the blind spot because it is the region of the retina where the optic nerve fibres exit the eye and form the optic nerve
  • The area lacks photoreceptor cells (rods and cones), which are r esponsible for detecting light and forming visual images
  • As a result, no visual information is detected in this region of the retina

Optic Disc.

What is the macula?

The macula is a small, specialised area in the centre of the retina, located at the back of the eye

Describe the role of the macula.

  • The macula is a small and highly sensitive part of the retina responsible for detailed central vision
  • It is essential for sharp, central vision, allowing you to see fine details, colour, and textures

Macula.

What is the fovea?

  • The fovea is the very centre of the macula in the retina
  • The macula allows us to appreciate detail and perform tasks that require central vision such as reading

Describe the function of the fovea.

It is the area responsible for the sharpest and most detailed vision, playing a key role in tasks that require high visual acuity, such as reading, recognising faces, and discerning fine details

Describe what is meant by the blind spot of the optic nerve.

  • The blind spot of the optic nerve (optic disc) is the area where no visual information is processed as the optic nerve is emerging out of the eye to the brain
  • The area lacks photoreceptor cells (rods and cones), which are responsible for detecting light and forming visual images
  • As a result, no visual information is detected in this region of the retina

What is the corresponding anatomic landmark for the physiological blind spot?

Optic disc

What is central vision?

Central vision refers to visual recognition such as detail day vision, colour vision, reading, facial recognition

Recall which area of the eye has the highest concentration of cone photoreceptors.

The fovea has the highest concentration of cone photoreceptors, so it plays a vital role in helping us maintain central vision

Describe how we assess central vision.

  • Central vision is assessed using the visual acuity assessment
  • Loss of foveal vision → Poor visual acuity

What is visual acuity?

(see lecture diagram / notes)

Describe how you would assess visual acuity (CN II). Describe the consequences of a loss in central vision.

  • Poor visual acuity
  • Visual acuity is a measure of the eye's ability to distinguish small details and poor visual acuity refers to a condition in which a person experiences blurred or unclear vision, meaning they are unable to see fine details or sharp images clearly
  • This makes it difficult to see clearly or function in daily life

What is peripheral vision?

  • Peripheral vision refers to visual recognition of shape, movement and night vision
  • It is also referred to as navigation vision

Describe how we assess peripheral vision.

Peripheral vision is assessed using the visual field assessment

Describe how you would assess visual field (CN II). Describe the purpose of assessing visual fields. Describe the consequences of a loss in peripheral vision.

Extensive loss of visual field means individuals are unable to navigate in their environment, and they may need white stick even with perfect visual acuity

What is a white stick?

A white cane is a mobility aid used by people who are blind or visually impaired to help them navigate their surroundings

Describe the difference between central and peripheral vision.

  • Central vision is responsible for sharp, clear, and detailed vision, primarily driven by cones in the fovea of the retina, and is used for tasks like reading, writing, and seeing fine details
  • Peripheral vision is more about wide awareness of the environment and is driven by rod cells in the outer retina, providing the ability to detect motion and shapes in the periphery of the visual field, though it is not as detailed as central vision

Central (Foveal) Vs Peripheral Vision.

Describe the general structure of the retina.

The retina has three layers: the outer layer, middle layer and the inner layer

What cells are present in the retina’s outer layer?

Photoreceptors (1st order neuron)

Describe the role of the retina’s outer layer.

This layer contains photoreceptors, which are important in the detection of light and its conversion to electrical signals

What cells are present in the retina’s middle layer?

Bipolar cells (2nd order neuron)

Describe the role of the retina’s middle layer.

  • This layer contains bipolar cells, which are important in local signal processing to improve contrast sensitivity
  • They are intermediaries in the visual pathway (interneurons) allowing transmission of signals from photoreceptors → retinal ganglion cells

What cells are present in the retina’s inner layer?

Retinal ganglion cells (3rd order neuron)

Describe the role of the retina’s inner layer.

This layer contains retinal ganglion cells, which are important in the transmission of signal from the eye to the brain

What is the difference between first, second and third order neurons?

  • The first-order neurons are the sensory receptors or neurons that detect stimuli and transmit signals
  • The second-order neurons relay the information
  • The third-order neurons carry the processed sensory information to the cortex, where it is interpreted and perceived

Retinal Layers.

Recall the two main classes of photoreceptor.

  • Rods
  • Cones

Describe the properties and the roles of rods.

  • Rods are 100 times more sensitive to light than cones
  • Rods are responsible for night vision (scotopic vision) and they have a slow response to light

Describe the properties and the roles of cones.

  • Cones are less sensitive to light, but have a faster response
  • Cones are responsible for daylight fine vision and colour vision (phototopic vision)

Describe the difference between rods and cones.

  • Rods are 100 times more sensitive to light than cones, which makes sense as their function is to enable night vision, however they are slow to respond to light whereas cones have a faster response to light
  • Rods (120 million) are significantly more abundant than cones (6 million)
  • Rods are responsible for night vision (scotopic vision), cones are responsible for daylight fine vision and colour vision (phototopic vision)

Rods And Cones.

What is refraction?

  • Refraction is the bending or change in direction of light (or other waves) as it passes from one medium to another with a different density — This is because the velocity of the waves change
  • NOTE: Lenses are an application of refraction

Recall the two main types of lenses.

  • Convex lens
  • Concave lens

What is a convex lens?

A converging lens ( convex ) takes light rays and brings them to a point.

What is a concave lens?

A diverging lens ( concave ) takes light rays and spreads them outward

Lenses.

What is emmetropia?

  • Emmetropia is the term for a normal, healthy eye with perfect vision
  • People with emmetropia can generally see clearly at all distances without needing corrective lenses for vision correction

Describe what happens to light rays in emmetropia.

In emmetropia, there is adequate correlation between axial length and refractive power and so parallel light rays fall on the retina

What is axial length?

The term axial length refers to the distance from the front to the back of the eye

Emmetropia.

What is ametropia?

Ametropia (refractive error) refers to a group of refractive errors in the eye where light does not focus precisely on the retina, resulting in blurred or distorted vision

Describe what happens in ametropia.

In ametropia, there is a mismatch between axial length and refractive power which means that parallel light rays don’t fall on the retina

Recall the three main types of ametropia.

  • Myopia
  • Hyperopia
  • Presbyopia

What is myopia?

Myopia, commonly known as short-sightedness, is a refractive error of the eye where distant objects appear blurry while close objects are seen clearly

Describe what happens in myopia.

Parallel rays converge at a focal point anterior to the retina

Recall two causes of myopia.

  • Excessive long globe/long eye (axial myopia)
  • Excessive refractive power (refractive myopia)
  • NOTE: The aetiology of myopia is not clear, but there is likely a genetic factor

Which is more common, axial or refractive myopia?

Axial myopia — Excessive long globe/long eye

Recall two symptoms of myopia.

  • Blurred distance vision
  • Squint in an attempt to improve uncorrected visual acuity when gazing into the distance
  • Headache

Myopia.

What is hyperopia?

Hyperopia, commonly known as long-sightedness, is a refractive error where distant objects are generally seen more clearly than close objects

Describe what happens in hyperopia.

In hyperopia, parallel rays converge at a focal point posterior to the retina

Recall two causes of hyperopia.

  • Excessive short globe (axial hyperopia) — more common
  • Insufficient refractive power (refractive hyperopia)

Which is more common, axial or refractive hyperopia?

Axial hyperopia — Excessive short globe

Hyperopia.

Recall two symptoms of hyperopia.

  • Visual acuity at near tends to blur relatively early — nature of blur vary from inability to read fine print to near vision is clear but suddenly and intermittently blur — blurred vision is more noticeable if person is tired, printing is weak or light inadequate
  • Asthenopic (eye straining) symptoms — eye pain, headache in frontal region, burning sensation in the eyes

What is the near response triad?

The near response triad, also known as the near reflex or near triad, is a coordinated set of three eye responses that occur when we focus on a nearby object

Describe what occurs in the near response triad.

  • Pupillary miosis (by the sphincter pupillae ) to increase depth of field
  • Convergence (medial recti from both eyes) to align both eyes towards a near object
  • Accommodation (circular ciliary muscle) to increase the refractive power of lens for near vision — i.e by thickening the lens

What is pupillary miosis?

  • Pupillary miosis refers to the narrowing of the pupil of the eye
  • Miosis is primarily controlled by the sphincter pupillae muscle in the iris, which contracts to make the pupil smaller.

What is meant by depth of field in vision?

  • Depth of field is the range over which objects appear in focus
  • A greater depth of field allows a wider range of distances to appear sharp, reducing the eye's need for constant refocusing

Depth Of Field.

When is an increased depth of field useful?

An increased depth of field allows us to see more of our environment more clearly without needing to refocus constantly • This is useful in: ◦ Reading and close-up work ◦ Navigating in complex environments

Describe the difference between an increased and decreased depth of field.

  • A large depth of field means that objects within a wider range of distances (both closer and farther from the focal point) will still appear reasonably in focus.
  • A shallow depth of field means that only the object or area at the exact focal distance is sharp, while other objects, even a little closer or farther, appear blurry.

How is depth of field increased and decreased?

  • Depth of field is increased using the sphincter pupillae muscles
  • Depth of field is decreased using the dilator pupillae muscles
  • NOTE: Squinting increases depth of field

Medial Rectus.

What is the circular ciliary muscle?

  • The circular ciliary muscle is one part of the ciliary muscle, a ring of smooth muscle located in the ciliary body of the eye
  • NOTE: Circular, radial and longitudinal fibres all form the ciliary muscle

Describe the role of the circular ciliary muscle.

Its specific function is to control the tension on the lens, enabling it to change shape for near or far vision.

When it contracts, it rounds the lens for close focus and increases its refractive power

When it relaxes, it flattens the lens for distant focus and decreases its refractive power

What is accommodation in vision?

Accommodation refers to the eye's ability to adjust its focus to see objects clearly at different distances — It usually refers to near vision

Describe how accommodation is performed.

  • The ciliary muscles are incredibly important in the process of accommodation
  • When the ciliary muscles contract, this reduces the tension on the zonular fibers (suspensory ligaments) that hold the lens in place — The lens becomes rounder and thicker, increasing its curvature,

which increases its refractive power

When the ciliary muscles relax, this increases the tension on the zonular fibers (suspensory ligaments) that hold the lens in place

Ciliary Muscles, Suspensory Ligaments And The Lens.

What is presbyopia?

  • Presbyopia is naturally occurring loss of accommodation (focus for near objects) — Its onset is from around age 40 years however distant vision is still intact
  • NOTE: It is understood that presbyopia is secondary to lens stiffness that occurs when we age

Describe how presbyopia can be corrected.

Corrected by reading glasses (convex lenses) to increase refractive power of the eye

What is the visual pathway?

The visual pathway is the pathway involved in the transmission of signal from eye to the visual cortex

Recall the seven important components within the visual pathway.

  • Eye
  • Optic nerve
  • Optic chiasm
  • Optic tract
  • Lateral geniculate nucleus
  • Optic radiation
  • Primary visual cortex/striate cortex

What is the optic chiasm?

The optic chiasm is an X-shaped structure located at the base of the brain, just in front of the hypothalamus, where the optic nerves from each eye partially cross

Describe the importance of the optic chiasm.

The optic nerve crossover at the optic chiasm is crucial for binocular vision and depth perception because it allows visual information from each eye to be processed in both hemispheres of the brain

Optic Chiasm.

What is the optic tract?

The optic tract is a bundle of nerve fibres that carries visual information from the optic chiasm to the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) of the thalamus in the brain

Describe the difference between the optic nerve and the optic tract.

  • The optic nerve carries visual information from one eye to the optic chiasm, and continues within the optic tract
  • The optic tract carries information from a single visual field (nasal or temporal tracts) of both eyes from the optic chiasm to the brain
  • This is because the optic nerve runs from retina → optic chiasm, before continuing within the optic tract but the optic tract runs from the optic chiasm → lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN)

Explained Further.

compression of the optic chiasm?

  • This is because the temporal (lateral) retina actually receives light from the nasal field of vision and the nasal (medial) retina actually receives light from the temporal (lateral) side of vision
  • This means that although the nasal fibres are the ones that decussate at the optic chiasm, because they receive light from the temporal field of vision, compression at the optic chiasm will result in compression of the nasal fibres and hence a loss of temporal vision (bitemporal hemianopia)
  • NOTE: Information from the temporal visual field is received by the nasal side due to the basic principles of optics and the way light enters the eye. The lens is a convex structure that bends light rays, causing them to invert and reverse the image on the retina. Conversely, light from the left visual field projects onto the right side of each eye’s retina.

Optic Nerve Vs Optic Tract.

What is the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN)?

The lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) is a relay centre within the thalamus that processes visual information received from the optic tract and transmits it to the primary visual cortex in the brain’s occipital lobe

Lgn/Visual Pathways.

What is optic radiation?

The optic radiation is a bundle of nerve fibres that relays visual information from the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) to the primary visual cortex in the occipital lobe.

What is the primary visual cortex?

The primary visual cortex (often abbreviated as V1 ) is the region of the brain that receives and processes visual information from the eyes.

Describe an additional function of the primary visual cortex.

It relays information to the extra-striate cortex for higher visual processing

Visual Pathway Anatomy.

What percentage of the optic nerve’s fibres decussate at the optic chiasm?

53% of ganglion fibres decussate (cross) at the optic chiasm to the contralateral optic tract

Recall the first order neurons of the visual pathway.

Rods and cones (photoreceptors)

Recall the second order neurons of the visual pathway.

Retinal bipolar cells

Recall the third order neurons of the visual pathway.

Retinal ganglion cells

Describe the role of the retinal ganglion cells.

These retinal ganglion cells form the optic nerve which passes signals → optic chiasm → optic tract → LGN → optic radiation

Recall the fourth order neurons of the visual pathway.

Optic radiations

Describe the role of the optic radiations.

They pass signals from the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) in the thalamus to the primary visual cortex

Visual Pathway Anatomy (2).

How does the perception of our left and right visual fields work?

  • Left visual field - Projects onto the right side of the retina
  • Right visual field - Projects onto the left side of the retina

Visual Fields.

Describe the difference in the functions of crossed and uncrossed fibres in the optic chiasm.

  • Crossed fibres decussate at the optic chiasm — Only nasal fibres decussate, so they are responsible for the temporal field of vision
  • Uncrossed fibres do not decussate at the optic chiasm — Only temporal fibres fail to decussate, so they are responsible for the nasal field of vision

Describe the effect of a lesion anterior to the optic chiasm.

A lesion anterior to the optic chiasm affects visual field loss in only one eye — This is because the optic nerve has not split into two and is only coming from one side’s retina

Describe the effect of a lesion posterior to the optic chiasm.

A lesion posterior to the optic chiasm affects visual field loss in both eyes — This is because the optic tract innervates the temporal or nasal field of vision for both eyes, so vision is lost in both eyes

Optic Chiasm.

Describe the effect of a lesion at the optic chiasm.

A lesion at the optic chiasm causes bitemporal hemianopia — temporal field deficit in both eyes

What is a bitemporal hemianopia?

Bitemporal hemianopia is a type of visual field defect in which there is a loss of vision in the outer (temporal) half of the visual field in both eyes

Describe what generally causes a bitemporal hemianopia.

A lesion at the optic chiasm via a pituitary tumour

What is a homonymous hemianopia?

Homonymous hemianopia is a type of visual field defect in which there is a loss of vision in the same side of the visual field in both eyes — i.e the right or left half of the visual field in both eyes

Describe what generally causes a homonymous hemianopia.

Lesions in the optic tract (posterior to optic chiasm) via a stroke/cerebrovascular accident (CVA) — i.e perfusion of one optic tract is affected — this is because the optic fibres have decussated, so fibres that receive information from the same sides (i.e left or right) are within the same optic tract

Describe the effects of a homonymous hemianopia.

  • Right sided lesion → Left Homonymous Hemianopia in Both Eyes → loss of left half of visual field
  • Left sided lesion → Right Homonymous Hemianopia in Both Eyes → loss of right half of visual field
  • REMEMBER: The image is flipped by the lens. A right sided cerebral lesion means the nasal fibre that has decussated from the left and the temporal fibre from the right will be affected. Because the image is flipped, the left nasal fibre carries information from the left temporal visual field and the right temporal fibre carries information from the right nasal visual field, this right cerebral lesion leads to loss of the left visual field in both eyes, which is a left homonymous hemianopia

Disorders Of Visual Pathway.

What is a homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing?

This is a homonymous hemianopia but the central vision ( macula and fovea ) remains intact, or is "spared."

What causes homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing?

Damage to Primary Visual Cortex, often due to a stroke

Why is the macula sometimes spared in homonymous hemianopia?

This is because the macula receives dual blood supply from both sides of the brain, so a cerebrovascular accident that affects the left visual cortex may not affect the left macula, since it receives dual blood supply

Recall which artery supplies the macula.

The macula receives blood from the posterior cerebral artery (PCA) and the middle cerebral artery (MCA)

What is a contralateral homonymous hemianopia?

  • Contralateral homonymous hemianopia refers to the pattern typically observed when homonymous hemianopia is caused by a lesion in the primary visual cortex
  • The affected half of the visual field is on the opposite side (contralateral) of the brain lesion
  • For example, a lesion at 6 would lead to left homonymous hemianopia

What is the pupil?

The pupil is the black, circular opening in the centre of the iris (the coloured part of the eye) that allows light to enter the eye

Describe the role of the pupil.

The pupil plays a central role in regulating the amount of light reaching the retina, which is the layer at the back of the eye responsible for detecting light and converting it into neural signals for vision

Describe how the pupil responds to light.

In light, the pupil constricts. This: — Decreases glare — Increases depth of field

What is glare in the context of the eye?

  • Glare is the light that enters your eye and interferes with your vision
  • It can be uncomfortable

Describe how the pupil responds to darkness.

  • In darkness, the pupil dilates
  • This increases light sensitivity in the dark by allowing more light into the eye

Which nerve mediates pupillary constriction?

Parasympathetic nerve (within CN III)

Which muscle is responsible for pupillary constriction?

Circular muscles

What is the purpose of cranial nerve III in pupillary function?

This is the oculomotor nerve which adjusts the amount of light going into the eye by constricting and dilating the pupil and thickening/thinning the lens

Which nerve mediates pupillary dilation?

Sympathetic nerve — within sympathetic nervous system

Which muscle is responsible for pupillary dilation?

Radial muscles

Pupillary Constriction Vs. Dilation.

What is the pupillary reflex?

The pupillary reflex is an automatic response in which the pupil changes size in reaction to the amount of light entering the eye

Describe the functional difference between the afferent and efferent pathways of the pupillary reflex.

  • The afferent pathway is responsible for detecting and transmitting the light signal to the brain — This helps us to understand the magnitude of this light signal
  • The efferent pathway carries the response from the brain to the eye muscles, resulting in pupil constriction or dilation

Describe how the afferent pathway of the pupillary reflex works.

  • At the retina, photoreceptors detect light and convert it into electrical signals
  • A small sub-section of retinal ganglion cells participate in the pupillary reflex pathway — The others perform their normal function on the optic nerve of conducting visual impulses to the LGN
  • The ganglion cell axons transmit electrical signals from the retina → pretectal nucleus of the brainstem (midbrain), where they synapse — via the normal optic tract route — this is bilateral
  • The signals synapse again when they exit the pretectal nucleus → Edinger-Westphal nucleus, ready for the efferent response — Once again, this is bilateral

Where are the pretectal and Edinger-Westphal nuclei located?

The pretectal nucleus and the Edinger-Westphal nucleus are both located in the midbrain of the brainstem

Describe how the efferent pathway of the pupillary reflex works.

  • The motor signal begins in the Edinger-Westphal nucleus
  • The signal travels along the parasympathetic fibres in the oculomotor nerve (CN III), before they synapse in the ciliary ganglion, which acts as a relay station
  • These fibres then exit the ciliary ganglion and travel to the sphincter pupillae muscle via the short posterior ciliary nerve
  • This final motor signal causes the sphincter pupillae muscle to contract, resulting in pupillary constriction

Pupillary Reflex.

What is the direct pupillary reflex?

The direct pupillary reflex is the constriction of the pupil in the light- stimulated eye

What is the consensual pupillary reflex?

The consensual pupillary reflex is the constriction of the pupil in the opposite eye when light is shone into an eye

Describe the difference between the direct and consensual pupillary reflexes.

  • The direct pupillary reflex is the constriction of the pupil in the same eye that is exposed to a source of light
  • However, the consensual pupillary reflex is the constriction of the pupil in the opposite eye when light is shone into the other eye

Describe why the direct and consensual pupillary reflexes co-exist.

The reason why the direct and consensual pupillary reflexes co-exist is because the afferent pathways (of the reflex) on either side alone will stimulate the efferent (outgoing) pathway on both sides

What is the afferent pupillary reflex defect?

This refers to the event where the eye/retina/brain doesn’t actually process the light coming into the eye

Describe the effects of a right afferent pupillary reflex defect.

  • No pupil constriction in both eyes when right eye is stimulated with light
  • Normal pupil constriction in both eyes when left eye is stimulated with light

What could cause a right afferent pupillary reflex defect?

Damage to the right optic nerve (CN II) — afferent

What is the efferent pupillary reflex defect?

This is the event where the eye is able to process (receive) light, but not able to react to it

Describe the effects of a right efferent pupillary reflex defect.

  • No right pupil constriction whether right or left eye is stimulated with light
  • Left pupil constricts whether right or left eye is stimulated with light

What could cause a right efferent pupillary reflex defect?

Damage to the right oculomotor nerve (CN III) — efferent

Afferent Vs. Efferent Defect.

What is the relative afferent pupillary defect?

Relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD) describes the event of damage to the afferent pathway but it is usually incomplete, or relative — relative = in comparison to other normal, physiological eye

Describe the effects of the relevant afferent pupillary defect (RAPD) on individuals.

Relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD) indicates a problem with the afferent pathways within the eye, and it leads to decreased perception and sensitivity to light within the affected eye

How can we identify relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD)?

Swinging torch test

What is the swinging torch test?

The swinging torch test is a diagnostic test used to identify relative afferent pupillary defects (RAPD)

Describe how the swinging torch test works.

  • The basis of the swinging torch test is alternating stimulation of right and left eye with light
  • For example, in a right RAPD, what we see is that: — Both pupils constrict when light swings to left undamaged side — This is a normal response to light showing no pathological signs — Both pupils paradoxically dilate when light swings to the right damaged side — This is not right, it shows that the right eye is less sensitive to light than it should be, indicating afferent damage

What is the difference between a relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD) and an afferent pupillary defect and how is this detected by light stimulation?

  • Relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD) describes the event of damage to the afferent pathway but it is usually incomplete, or relative — relative = in comparison to other normal, physiological eye
  • In an afferent pupillary defect, the eye is completely unable to sense light
  • This is detected by paradoxical dilation upon light stimulation in RAPD, but simply an absence of constriction in afferent pupillary defect

What is the function of eye movement?

  • Eye movement refers to voluntary or involuntary of movement of the eyes
  • It is necessary for acquiring and tracking visual stimuli

What controls eye movement?

The extraocular muscles

How many extraocular muscles are there?

Six.

Describe the importance of the extraocular muscles.

The extraocular muscles are a group of six muscles surrounding each eye that are essential for controlling eye movement and maintaining proper alignment of the eyes

Recall the six extraocular muscles.

  • Superior rectus
  • Inferior rectus
  • Medial rectus
  • Lateral rectus
  • Superior oblique
  • Inferior oblique

Recall the four straight extraocular muscles.

  • Superior rectus
  • Inferior rectus
  • Medial rectus
  • Lateral rectus
  • NOTE: These muscles are either directly above or directly lateral to the eye At what point do the rectus extraocular muscles originate from?
  • Common tendinous ring

Recall the three cranial nerves involved in the innervation of the extraocular muscles.

  • CN III - oculomotor
  • CN IV - trochlear
  • CN VI - abducens

Why do the rectus muscles have their name?

“Recti” = straight in Latin which indicates the direct, straight path that all the rectus muscles take from the common tendinous ring

Describe the attachment of the superior rectus to the eye.

The superior rectus is attached to the eye at 12 o’clock

Describe the role of the superior rectus.

The superior rectus moves the eye up (and inwards)

Describe the attachment of the inferior rectus to the eye.

The inferior rectus is attached to the eye at 6 o’clock

Describe the role of the inferior rectus.

The inferior rectus moves the eye down (and outwards)

Recall another name for the lateral rectus.

External rectus

Describe the attachment of the lateral rectus to the eye.

The lateral rectus attaches on the temporal side of the eye
