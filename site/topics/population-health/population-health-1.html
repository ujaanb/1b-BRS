Population health and disease burden

What does it mean to improve population health?

  • Increase the amount of health in a population.
  • Reduce variation in health within the population.
  • This includes preventing disease, protecting people from threats, promoting self-care and community resilience, and improving health services.

What is disease burden?

The impact of a health problem beyond mortality. It includes morbidity, financial costs, and other measures.

What are Years of Life Lost (YLL)?

YLL = expected age of death − actual age of death.

What are Years Lived with Disability, Illness or Injury (YLD)?

YLD = time lived with a condition × condition-specific disability weighting. The disability weight runs from 0 (perfect health) to 1 (equivalent to death).

What are Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs)?

DALY = YLL + YLD. One DALY represents one lost year of healthy life.

What does the distribution of DALYs show across a population?

It describes the population burden of disease and allows causes of lost healthy life to be compared.

What are Quality-Adjusted Life Years (QALYs)?

QALY = time lived in a health state × the utility assigned to that state. QALYs combine quantity and quality of life. Accuracy note: the standard utility convention is 1 = perfect health and 0 = death; the PDF reverses these labels.

When are DALYs and QALYs typically used?

  • DALYs: health-system planning, international health, and epidemiology.
  • QALYs: cost-effectiveness analysis and clinical trials.

Policy and planning

What is a policy?

A principle or protocol used to guide decisions and achieve rational outcomes. Policies are influenced by evidence, politics, public opinion, and the media. Examples include Scottish minimum unit pricing and parental-leave policies.

What are the main stages and considerations in policy planning?

  1. Determine the policy goal: objectives, outcomes, measures, and standards.
  2. Identify the problem: definition, significance, connectedness, timing, priority, and current resources.
  3. Develop the solution and identify additional resources: responsibility, restraint, adequacy, durability, timing, accountability, and oversight.
Planning should take a broad view of available information and identify evidence gaps.

What can result from good policy planning?

Well-designed and implemented policies can improve health and reduce inequalities; unintended consequences can also be positive. The lecture gives the sugar tax as an intervention that reduces DALYs and increases QALYs.

What can result from poor policy planning?

Policies introduced without careful consideration can cause negative unintended consequences or perverse incentives. The lecture gives the Northern Irish Renewable Heat Incentive Scheme as an example.

What is a perverse incentive?

An incentive that causes undesirable behaviour as people attempt to receive a reward or meet a target.

What healthcare examples illustrate perverse incentives?

  • Funding structures may encourage doctors to focus on treatment rather than prevention.
  • Long waiting lists may increase private-market use, creating incentives for clinicians who also work privately.

What is the nature of policymaking?

Policymaking is inconsistent, sporadic, and opportunistic. It is influenced by external events, political cycles, and societal actors; it may be reactive or proactive. Experts may be invited into the process, creating both opportunities and challenges.

How does public trust in scientists and medical professionals compare with other professions?

The lecture states that public trust is substantially higher in scientists and medical professionals than in many other professions.

Evidence and advocacy

What is the sequence in evidence-based policymaking?

Primary studies → body of evidence → recommended policy → enacted policy.

Why can a vague desirable policy outcome create problems?

When the desired outcome is ambiguous or controversial, scientists may become “stealth advocates” or be forced to take a side because neutrality may be interpreted as endorsing the status quo. Stealth advocacy means presenting personal policy preferences or ethical judgements in a way that is indistinguishable from scientific judgement.
