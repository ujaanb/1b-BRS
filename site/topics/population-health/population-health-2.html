The NHS

What are the three founding principles of the NHS?

  1. Universal: available to all.
  2. Free at the point of delivery.
  3. Provision based on clinical need, not ability to pay.
The NHS was founded on 5 July 1948.

How is the NHS structured?

  • About 215 NHS hospital providers, around 6,500 general practices, and 850+ independent organisations (lecture figures).
  • 42 Integrated Care Systems (ICSs).
  • Integrated Care Boards (ICBs): NHS management structures within ICSs.
  • Integrated Care Partnerships (ICPs): bring the NHS and local government together.
  • Providers include acute, integrated, specialist, mental-health, community, ambulance, primary-care-network, and independent-sector organisations.

International healthcare systems

What characterises the UK healthcare system?

  • Publicly funded universal coverage.
  • Central management with regional delivery.
  • Funded mainly through general taxation.
  • The Department of Health and Social Care allocates budgets.
  • No out-of-pocket payment for most treatments.

What characterises the US healthcare system?

  • Mix of public and private providers.
  • Employer-based private insurance, with Medicare and Medicaid for specified groups.
  • Coverage is strongly related to employment.
  • Complex payment systems and fee-for-service payment.
  • No universal coverage and high out-of-pocket costs.

What characterises the French healthcare system?

  • Universal coverage funded publicly through social health insurance.
  • Funding mainly from taxes and payroll contributions.
  • Private “complémentaire” insurance covers co-payments.
  • Cost-sharing applies to most services.

What characterises the Australian healthcare system?

  • Mix of public and private providers.
  • Medicare provides universal coverage.
  • Private insurance is incentivised through rebates.
  • Public services are tax-funded.
  • Delivery is decentralised to states, with a strong private-sector role.

What strengths of the French system does the lecture identify relative to the UK?

  • Lower out-of-pocket costs (as stated in the lecture).
  • Greater freedom to choose clinicians, including direct specialist access.

What weaknesses of the French system does the lecture identify relative to the UK?

  • Administrative complexity from mixed payroll taxes and insurance arrangements.
  • Greater focus on acute care than prevention.

What strengths of the US system does the lecture identify relative to the UK?

  • Faster access to advanced treatments for insured patients.
  • Shorter waiting lists.

What weaknesses of the US system does the lecture identify relative to the UK?

  • Very high per-capita healthcare spending.
  • The lecture states that 9% of the population is uninsured.

What strengths of the Australian system does the lecture identify relative to the UK?

  • Lower spending as a proportion of GDP for better outcomes (lecture claim).
  • States manage healthcare without centralised control.

What weaknesses of the Australian system does the lecture identify relative to the UK?

  • A two-tier divide between private and public services.
  • The hybrid public/private system may confuse patients.

Health inequalities

What are health inequalities?

Unfair and avoidable differences in health between groups. The lecture describes these differences as a sustained and growing UK problem.

What are inclusion health groups?

Socially excluded groups, including people experiencing homelessness; substance or alcohol dependence; vulnerable migration; sex work; modern slavery; contact with the criminal-justice system; and Gypsy, Roma, and Traveller communities.

What is Core20PLUS5?

An NHS England approach for identifying target populations and reducing healthcare inequalities in adults.

What are the components of Core20PLUS5?

  • Core20: the most deprived 20% of the population according to the Index of Multiple Deprivation.
  • PLUS: locally defined groups experiencing inequalities but not necessarily included in Core20, such as ethnic minorities, people with learning disabilities, people experiencing homelessness, migrants or asylum seekers, and Gypsy, Roma, and Traveller communities.
  • Five clinical priorities: maternity, severe mental illness, chronic respiratory disease, early cancer diagnosis, and hypertension case-finding.

Patient and public involvement

What is the Patient and Public Ladder of Engagement and Involvement?

A framework describing levels of public involvement in health and care decisions. It helps organisations progress from one-way information or consultation toward meaningful collaborative partnership.

What are the levels of the Patient and Public Ladder of Engagement and Involvement?

  1. Co-producing: equal partnership, responsibility, and decision-making.
  2. Co-designing: people help design services and influence decisions without responsibility for them.
  3. Engaging: regular opportunities to influence some decisions.
  4. Consulting: meetings or surveys seek people’s opinions.
  5. Informing: people are told what was decided and sometimes why.
  6. Educating: people are told what is offered and why, sometimes with persuasion.
  7. Coercing: people are made to use a service without establishing what they want or need.
