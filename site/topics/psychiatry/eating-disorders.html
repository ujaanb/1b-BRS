Overview

What is a 5:2 diet?

The 5:2 diet is a type of intermittent fasting diet, where an individual eats normally for five days while fasting for two days by eating ‹800 kcal

Describe what is meant by having a “lapse” while on a diet.

This would refer to when an individual is not adhering to the directions of the diet, likely temporarily

What is a “binge”?

A binge is when you feel like you have lost control over your eating

What is binge eating disorder?

A binge eating disorder is when you eat a large amount of food in a short amount of time and feel like you can't control what or how much you are eating — It causes significant psychological distress

What is the commonest eating disorder?

Binge eating disorder is the commonest eating disorder

Recall a factor that increases one’s risk of developing an eating disorder.

Higher weight — i.e people at a higher weight are at higher risk of developing eating disorders

Describe what actually defines an eating disorder.

The thoughts, feelings and behaviours behind the eating habits is

what defines an eating disorder, not the eating habit or weight itself Describe how weight stigma operates in males and females.

  • Females typically start to get stigmatised for their weight when their BMI is 27 and above
  • With men, it's not until they're about BMI 35

What is an eating disorder?

  • ‘A persistent disturbance of eating behaviour or behaviour intended to control weight, which significantly impairs physical health or psychosocial functioning
  • NOTE: Eating disorders are mental disorders

Describe what drives eating disorders.

Eating disorders are driven by fear of fatness or extreme distress about eating

Recall the two forms of disturbances to eating behaviours.

  • Binge eating
  • Restricted eating

Describe the two different ways that restricted eating can present.

Restricted eating may present as a restriction to quantity or range of foods being consumed

Recall four examples of behaviours intended to control weight.

  • Restricted eating (fasting)
  • Self-induced vomiting
  • Excessive exercise — difficult to define, often a disorder when the exercise is of a compulsive nature
  • Laxative, diuretic and other energy burning or appetite suppressing medications — e.g. caffeine, smoking, Ozempic

Recall three ways that eating disorders can affect physical health.

  • Impacts growth and development
  • Stop periods
  • Negative effects on the brain
  • Results in osteoporosis
  • High mortality

Recall three ways that eating disorders can affect psychosocial health.

  • Functional impairment — Impacts work — Relationships (family, peers, intimate) — Daily living
  • Distress

Describe how eating disorders typically affect psychosocial health.

Eating disorders typically affect psychosocial functioning by significantly impairing intimate relationships

What is DSM-5?

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Illnesses (DSM-5) is the latest edition of the American Psychiatric Association’s professional reference book on mental health and brain-related conditions

What is ICD-11?

  • ICD–11 is the international standard for systematic recording, reporting, analysis, interpretation and comparison of mortality and morbidity data
  • NOTE: DSM-5 and ICD-11 tend to overlap a lot

Recall three DSM-5 and ICD-11 eating disorders.

  • Anorexia Nervosa
  • Bulimia Nervosa
  • Binge Eating Disorder
  • Other Specified Feeding and Eating Disorders (OSFED)
  • Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID)
  • Rumination Disorder/Syndrome
  • Pica

What is anorexia nervosa?

Anorexia nervosa is an eating disorder where you feel a need to keep your weight as low as possible

Describe two characteristics of anorexia nervosa.

  • A. Restriction of energy intake relative to requirements leading to significantly low body weight in the context of age, sex, developmental trajectory and physical health
  • B. Intense fear of gaining weight or becoming fat, or persistent behaviour that interferes with weight gain
  • C. Disturbance in experience of weight/shape, undue influence of weight/shape on self-evaluation, or persistent lack of recognition of seriousness of low body weight
  • NOTE: Amenorrhoea used to be one of the DSM-5 criteria but it is no longer there

Recall the two forms of anorexia nervosa.

  • Restricting
  • Binge-eating/Purge

Describe the difference between the restricting and binge-eat/purge forms of anorexia nervosa.

  • Restricting anorexia nervosa tends to be characterised by extreme desire to restrict weight by reducing caloric intake and in a lot of cases, excessive exercise
  • Binge-eating/purging anorexia nervosa tends to be characterised by binge-eating and purging to the point where an individual’s weight has become so low
  • While both forms involve a desire to keep one’s weight as low as possible, the difference is the strategies used to achieve this goal

Which of these two anorexia nervosa subtypes are more common?

Binge-eating/purge

What is bulimia nervosa?

Bulimia nervosa is an eating disorder in which a person has regular episodes of eating a very large amount of food (bingeing) during

which the person feels a loss of control over their eating followed by inappropriate compensatory mechanisms to prevent weight gain Describe two clinical features of bulimia nervosa.

  • Over eating episodes — large amount of food in discrete time period — sense of lack of control
  • Inappropriate compensatory mechanisms
  • Body image disturbance

Describe how long bulimia nervosa characteristics need to be going on for to reach a diagnosis.

Occur at least 1x per week for 4 weeks or more

Describe the characteristics of binge eating disorder.

  • Episodes of over eating at least once a week for 4 weeks or more
  • No or minimal compensation
  • Hence, individuals are frequently overweight

Describe a way we can distinguish between bulimia nervosa and anorexia nervosa clinically.

  • Patients with bulimia nervosa cannot be clinically underweight — They must be in the normal or high weight range
  • If a patient is thought to have bulimia nervosa and they are clinically underweight, it is evident that this patient has the binge/purge subtype of anorexia nervosa

What is Rumination disorder/syndrome?

Rumination syndrome, or merycism, is a chronic motility disorder characterised by effortless regurgitation of most meals following consumption, due to the involuntary contraction of the muscles around the abdomen

What is pica?

  • Pica is the craving or consumption of objects that are not normally intended to be consumed — It is classified as an eating disorder but can also be the result of an existing mental disorder
  • The ingested or craved substance may be biological, natural or manmade

Describe what the weight of patients is like in anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa and binge eating disorder.

  • Anorexia nervosa - Low or significant weight loss
  • Bulimia nervosa - Normal or high
  • Binge eating disorder - Normal or high

Describe whether binge eating is present in anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa and binge eating disorder.

  • Bulimia nervosa - Yes

Describe whether self-induced vomiting is present in anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa and binge eating disorder.

  • Anorexia nervosa - Maybe
  • Bulimia nervosa - Yes

Describe whether excessive exercise is present in anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa and binge eating disorder.

  • Anorexia nervosa - Maybe

Describe whether guilt and shame is present in anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa and binge eating disorder.

  • Anorexia nervosa - Yes
  • Bulimia nervosa - Yes
  • Binge eating disorder - Maybe

Describe the difference in anorexia nervosa classification between DSM-5 and ICD-11.

  • In ICD-11, you can have anorexia nervosa at any weight — ICD-11 diagnoses anorexia on the basis of weight loss and starvation status, rather than BMI (weight status)
  • In DSM-5, you can only have anorexia nervosa if you are clinically underweight

which is a type of Other Specified Feeding and Eating Disorder (OSFED) What is a purging disorder?

This is effectively the opposite of a binge-eating disorder and it involves an individual purging but not eating

Describe the characteristics of a purging disorder.

  • Defined by recurrent purging behaviour to influence weight or shape — i.e self-induced vomiting, misuse of laxatives, diuretics, or other medications including insulin in the absence of binge eating
  • Weight is in the normal range

Describe how insulin can be used to control weight.

  • Insulin, which directly reduces blood sugar levels after meals by facilitating glucose uptake into cells also sends signals to the hypothalamus which induce satiety
  • This reduces a person’s desire to eat, which can cause them to significantly reduce their caloric intake
  • NOTE: This can be an issue in individuals who have Type I diabetes

What are Other Specified Feeding and Eating Disorders (OSFEDs)?

Other Specified Feeding and Eating Disorders (OSFED) is a category in the DSM-5 used for individuals who have significant eating disorder symptoms but do not meet the full criteria for conditions like anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, or binge-eating disorder

Recall four Other Specified Feeding and Eating Disorders (OSFEDs).

  • Atypical anorexia nervosa
  • Purging disorder
  • Atypical bulimia nervosa
  • Night eating syndrome

What is atypical bulimia nervosa?

  • Atypical bulimia nervosa (ABN) is bulimia nervosa that presents with the same binge and purge behaviours as those with BN, however the regularity of the episodes is far less frequent — This means people with ABN may not show the same weight loss or body changes as someone with bulimia nervosa
  • Unlike atypical anorexia nervosa, the atypical nature of atypical bulimia nervosa is not defined on the basis of weight, however it is defined by the nature of the bulimia itself

Describe the difference between atypical bulimia nervosa and binge-purge subtype of anorexia nervosa.

  • While both eating disorders involve episodes of binge-eating followed by purging, atypical bulimia nervosa involves binge-eating, purging and a clinically normal/high weight
  • However, binge-purge anorexia nervosa involves binge-eating, purging and a clinically low weight

What are Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorders (ARFIDs)?

  • Avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) is a feeding or eating disorder in which individuals significantly limit the volume or variety of foods they consume, causing malnutrition, weight loss, or psychosocial problems
  • Replaces and extends Feeding Disorders of Infancy and Early Childhood (FdoIEC) — However, ARFIDs have no age limit

Describe three clinical features of Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorders (ARFIDs).

  • Feeding/Eating disturbance — significant weight loss — significant nutritional deficiency — dependance on enteral feeding/nutritional supplements — marked interference with psychosocial functioning
  • No weight/shape concerns

Describe what distinguishes Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorders (ARFIDs) from most other eating disorders.

ARFIDs are a category of eating disorder that aren’t driven by weight or shape concerns

Describe what drives Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorders (ARFIDs). Three main subtypes and a mixed group:

  • individuals who do not eat enough/show little interest in feeding (25.1%)
  • individuals who only accept a limited diet in relation to sensory features (29.5%)
  • and individuals whose food refusal is related to aversive experience (7.2%)
  • combined subtype (38.2%)

Describe what patient weight is like in purging disorders and Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorders (ARFIDs).

  • Purging disorder - Normal or high
  • ARFIDs - Low, normal or high

Describe whether binge eating is present in purging disorders and Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorders (ARFIDs).

  • ARFIDs - No.

Describe whether dietary restriction is present in purging disorders and Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorders (ARFIDs).

Characteristics Of Eating Disorders.

Describe the general epidemiology of eating disorders.

  • ED are relatively common in childhood and adolescence — Around 40% of adolescent girls show ED behaviours by age 16, 11% diagnosable
  • ARFID depends on the context — 3.2% primary school Switzerland, 14% tertiary referral centres in USA

Describe the incidence of anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa.

Incidence of AN and BN are stable – around 1-2% of adolescents

Describe the prevalence of Other Specified Feeding and Eating Disorders (OSFEDs) and binge eating disorders.

Incidence of OSFED & binge-eating disorders may be increasing — lifetime prev. around 2.3%

Recall the most common disorder seen in eating disorder clinics.

  • Anorexia Nervosa is still the most common disorder in ED clinics
  • NOTE: This is different to the most common eating disorder, which is binge eating disorder

Recall the prevalence of eating disorders in men (FINLAND STATS).

1 in 40 young men

What type of illness is an eating disorder classified as?

  • Eating disorders are classified as serious mental illnesses (like psychoses)
  • NOTE: Eating disorders are commoner than psychoses and they affect people of all ages, genders and ethnicities

What is Standardised Mortality Ratio (SMR)?

A Standardised Mortality Ratio (SMR) compares the observed number of deaths in a population to the expected number of deaths, based on a standard population's age-specific death rates

Describe how the Standardised Mortality Ratio (SMR) varies between eating disorders.

  • The standardised mortality ratio (SMR) for anorexia nervosa is 5.35 – 5.86 – up to 13 if hospitalised — For bulimia nervosa it is 1.49 – 1.93 — For binge eating disorder it is 1.5 — For purging disorder it is 3.9
  • NOTE: In comparison to other diseases, for Type 1 diabetes SMR is 3 – 5, for asthma the SMR is 1.47 – 1.9

Describe the key messages passed across in the Medical Emergencies in Eating Disorders Guidance by the Medical Royal Colleges.

  • Medical teams need to actively treat the patient. Responsibilities include: — safely re-feeding the patient, neither under nor over feeding — managing fluid and electrolyte problems, often caused by purging behaviours — arranging discharge to an appropriate setting, in agreement with the mental health team, commissioners, the patient and their family as soon as safe and indicated — managing the behaviours common in patients with eating disorders, in collaboration with the mental health team
  • The mental health team need to additionally: — occasionally assess and treat patients under compulsion using relevant mental health legislation — address family concerns and involve both patients and their families in discussions about treatment — advise on appropriate onward care following medical stabilisation — advise on patients with complex comorbidity, such as personality disorder or autism

Describe three clinical facts that can help in the diagnosis and treatment of eating disorders.

  • Eating disorders are not visible - you need to ask
  • Normal blood tests do NOT mean things are OK
  • Cardiovascular parameters are the best pointers to risk
  • Intake and rate of weight loss is more important than weight
  • Red for risk means ask someone who knows!

Recall five individual risk factors for eating disorders.

  • Female gender
  • Genetics
  • Premature birth
  • Low self esteem
  • Perfectionism
  • Previous depression/anxiety
  • Previous obesity
  • Early puberty
  • Diabetes
  • Crohn’s disease

Recall three family risk factors for eating disorders.

  • History of dieting/eating disorders
  • History of depression/anxiety/alcohol dependence
  • History of obesity

Recall five possible triggers/maintaining factors for eating disorders.

  • Puberty
  • Socio-cultural pressures — i.e thin portrayed as ideal in the media and social media
  • Family factors
  • Pressure to achieve
  • Behaviour of peers
  • Comments about weight

Describe how the prevalence of eating disorders can vary between different families.

  • First degree relatives of individuals with anorexia nervosa were 11 times more likely to develop anorexia nervosa
  • Heritability — AN: ~ 58% -74% — BN: between 54%-83% — BED: between 41%-57%

What is the LD Score Regression?

LD Score Regression (LDSC) is a statistical method used in genetics to estimate: — Heritability of complex traits — Polygenic architecture of diseases — Genetic correlations between different traits — The influence of confounding factors like population structure in genome-wide association studies (GWAS)

Phenotypes.

Describe the significance of eating disorders being regarded as metabolic and psychological.

  • Reduces stigma and blame
  • Might help identify important gene environment interactions
  • Might inform treatment decisions
  • Might help us develop interventions

Describe how childhood can impact one’s risk of developing an eating disorder.

  • Children with anorexia nervosa more likely to have had early feeding & GI problems, picky eating and mealtime conflict
  • Children with bulimia nervosa were less picky and ate faster and more likely to overeat — Genetic factors linked with obesity also drive eating behaviour (FTO gene)

Recall three psychological risk factors for eating disorders.

  • Behaviour
  • Psychopathology
  • Self-esteem
  • Neurocognition
  • Temperament/personality

Recall three sociocultural risk factors for eating disorders.

  • Wider social influences
  • School/peers
  • Family

Describe two generic factors that can impact one’s risk of developing an eating disorder.

  • Trauma
  • Life events
  • NOTE: Trauma such as sexual abuse is more likely to lead to binge- purge type disorders

Describe how perfectionism can affect one’s risk of developing an eating disorder.

Perfectionism can especially increase one’s risk of fasting and purging

Describe how high and low self-esteem can affect one’s risk of developing an eating disorder.

  • High self-esteem → decreased risk of developing anorexia nervosa (protective)
  • Low self esteem → Risk factor for bulimic and compulsive eating

Describe how other mental disorders can affect one’s risk of developing an eating disorder.

  • Anxiety disorders — i.e. OCD increases risk of AN
  • Externalising disorders — i.e. ADHD, history of depression increase risk of BN

Describe the influence that families have when treating eating disorders.

  • No evidence for family interaction or ‘type’
  • Some evidence that maternal emotional wellbeing and protective parenting style are important
  • Maternal dieting and paternal comments about weight influence girls but not boys

Describe the socio-cultural factors that can affect development of eating disorders.

  • Some evidence of increase in developing countries of incidence/prevalence (mass media exposure)
  • Bullying, teasing by peers, social pressure to be thin
  • Exposure to social network media

Life Affecting Eating Habits.

What is the triad of evidence-based practice?

  • These three principles are used to trial new practices in medicine:
  • Best scientific evidence — results of experiments and quantitative studies
  • Clinical experience — expert panels — practice groups — consensus statements
  • Patient preferences — satisfaction — quality of life — treatment burden — qualitative studies

Describe the current availability of scientific evidence for treating eating disorders.

Evidence for effectiveness of existing treatments is weak across the age range — Few large-scale randomised controlled drug trials for anorexia
