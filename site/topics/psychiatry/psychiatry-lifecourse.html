Overview

What is meant by biopsychosocial management?

Biopsychosocial management is a patient-centred approach to healthcare that considers the interconnected nature of biological, psychological, and social factors in understanding and treating health and illness

What is the systemic approach when tackling mental health problems?

The systemic approach involves focus on relationships and social context — e.g. school and family

What is the 4Ps formulation when tackling child and adolescent mental health?

The 4 Ps of therapy is a model used to conceptualise the formulation and treatment of the presenting problem: — Predisposing factors — increase risk of developing problem — Precipitating factors — i.e directly lead to onset of the problem — Perpetuating factors — worsen an existing issue — Protective factors

Describe the proportion of all disease and injury that mental health disorders account for in 10-19 year olds.

Mental health conditions account for 16% of the global burden of disease and injury in people aged 10-19 years

What age do mental health disorders usually start from?

Half of all mental health conditions start by 14 years of age but most cases are undetected and untreated

Recall the crises that people go through in life according to Erikson’s stages of psychosocial development in chronological order.

  • Initiative vs Guilt
  • Industry vs Inferiority

  • Intimacy vs Isolation

  • Integrity vs Despair

Recall the eight virtues developed in life according to Erikson’s stages of psychosocial development in chronological order.

  • Infant - 18 months - Hope

Stages Of Development.

Describe the nature of development of the adolescent brain.

  • Adolescence is a period of neural imbalance caused by early maturation of subcortical brain areas and delayed maturation of prefrontal control areas
  • The prefrontal cortex matures later than other cortical areas — i.e those associated with sensory and motor tasks

Describe in neurological terms what defines adolescence.

What is somatisation disorder?

  • Somatisation disorder, also known as somatic symptom disorder, is a mental health condition where a person experiences multiple, chronic physical symptoms that are distressing and disrupt daily life, but cannot be fully explained by a medical condition EXAMPLE: Describe the clinical presentation of somatisation disorder.
  • 10 year old boy, several attendances to GP and Emergency Department with recurrent abdominal pain, no physical cause found so far

What is depressive disorder?

  • This is a common mental disorder characterised by persistent sadness, loss of interest in activities, and other symptoms that can significantly impact daily life EXAMPLE: Describe the clinical presentation of depressive disorder.
  • 15 year old young woman with tiredness, sleeping 12 hours/day, irritability and reduced enjoyment from her hobbies

What is autism spectrum disorder?

  • This is a neurodevelopmental condition that affects how individuals communicate, interact, and behave EXAMPLE: Describe the clinical presentation of autism spectrum disorder.
  • 4 year old boy, language is behind peers in class, doesn’t interact much with others, prefers watching buses/trains to playing in the playground
  • NOTE: It is normal for children to be obsessed with cars, trains etc. but children usually grow out of this phase as they grow older — In children with autism, they may take much longer to grow out of this obsessive phase with cars/trains etc. because they like the repetition

What is Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)?

  • This is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterised by persistent patterns of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity that interfere with daily functioning and development EXAMPLE: Describe the clinical presentation of Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD).
  • 8 year old boy who has been fidgeting in lessons, speaking out of turn and struggling to stay focused on homework tasks

Recall the two types of ADHD.

  • Inattention
  • Hyperactivity-impulsivity

Which type is more prevalent in which gender?

  • Inattention - girls
  • Hyperactivity-impulsivity - boys

Describe five of the DSM-5 criteria used to diagnose ADHD.

  • Persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity
  • Present for at least 6 months
  • Inappropriate for their developmental level
  • Interferes with functioning or development
  • Several symptoms present before age 12
  • Several symptoms present in two or more settings
  • The symptoms are not better explained by another mental disorder

Describe three genetic risk factors for ADHD.

  • No isolated gene for ADHD, there are likely multiple genes conferring vulnerability for developing it
  • Twin studies have shown a significant heritability for ADHD - as high as 76%
  • First degree relatives of children with ADHD have an ADHD diagnostic probability 4-5x higher than the general population
  • Boys are more vulnerable than girls (2:1 - 3:1)
  • 3-4% prevalence
  • Subtypes - 20-30% inattentive, 15% hyperactive, 50-75% combined

Recall two environmental risk factors for ADHD.

  • Premature birth
  • Low birth weight
  • Prenatal smoking exposure

Describe the general prognosis of ADHD.

  • Prognosis: 70% of children who have this disorder will have the disorder as teenagers, and about 40%-60% will still have it as adults
  • Generally, brain develops and children → adults, the expression of ADHD decreases

What is dementia?

  • Dementia is a general term for a severe loss of memory and other cognitive abilities which leads to impaired daily function (regardless of the underlying cause) — it is a syndrome, not a specific disease and can be thought of as “chronic brain failure”
  • It can also be described as a fatal neurodegenerative disorder characterised by progressive cognitive, social and functional impairment

Describe the general features of the clinical presentation of dementia.

  • Irreversible and progressive changes
  • Global cognitive and behavioural impairment
  • Sufficiently severe to interfere with social and occupational function
  • An umbrella term that has many underlying causes, can be conceptualised as “chronic brain failure”

Recall five reversible (”non-traditional”) causes of dementia.

  • Normal pressure hydrocephalus
  • Intracranial tumours
  • Subdural haematoma
  • Depression
  • B1, B6, B12 deficiency
  • Folate deficiency
  • Hypothyroidism
  • Neurosyphilis
  • Delirium

Causes Of Dementia.

What is important to exclude when looking for reversible causes of cognitive impairment?

Always think to exclude – Surgical, metabolic, infective and psychiatric reversible causes for cognitive impairment

What is normal pressure hydrocephalus?

This is a condition where excess cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) accumulates in the brain's ventricles, causing them to enlarge, but the CSF pressure remains within the normal range

What is the Hakim-Adams triad?

Describe the epidemiology of dementia.

  • Worldwide, 47.5 million people have dementia. Expected to reach 75.5 million by 2030 and 135.5 million by 2050
  • Number 7 on WHO list of leading global causes of death; number 2 in high income countries

Describe how commonly dementia causes death in comparison to other diseases in men and women.

In the UK, dementia is leading cause of death in women and is second to heart disease for men

What is the most common cause of dementia?

Most common cause of dementia is Alzheimer’s disease (AD), 70% of cases

Recall a major non-modifiable factor that can increase risk of Alzheimer’s.

Age

Describe how the risk of Alzheimer’s disease increases with age.

Risk of AD increases with age: — 1% at age 60yrs — doubles every 5yrs — 40% of those aged 85yrs

Describe how mild dementia presents in those who have it.

Living independently but some supervision/support often needed

Can participate in community activities and can appear unimpaired to those who do not know them

  • Judgement and problem solving typically impaired
  • Social judgement may be preserved
  • Difficulty making complex plans/decisions and handling finances

Describe the presentation and characteristics of moderate dementia.

  • Require support to function outside the home and only simple household tasks are maintained
  • Difficulties with basic activities of daily living (ADL’s), such as dressing and personal hygiene
  • Significant memory loss
  • Judgment and problem solving are typically significantly impaired, and social judgment is often compromised
  • May have difficulty communicating with individuals outside the home without caregiver assistance
  • Socialising is increasingly difficult as the individual may behave inappropriately (e.g., in disinhibited or aggressive ways), with associated behaviour changes (e.g., calling out, clinging, wandering, disturbed sleep, or hallucinations)
  • Difficulties are often obvious to most individuals who have contact with the individual

Describe the presentation and characteristics of severe dementia.

  • Severe memory impairment
  • Often disoriented to time and place
  • Often unable to make judgments or solve problems
  • May have difficulty understanding what is happening around them (situational awareness)
  • Dependent on others for basic personal care (bathing, toileting and feeding)
  • Urinary and faecal incontinence may emerge at this stage

What are behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD)?

These are simply symptoms that are exhibited by dementia patients in the more advanced stages of the disease. They include — Agitation, aggression, depression, anxiety, apathy, delusions, hallucinations, and sleep disturbances

Describe the significance of BPSD in the progression of dementia.

  • Common in late stage Dementia
  • Are often the precipitating symptoms which lead to people with dementia being detained under The Mental Health Act (1983)

What is the Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE)?

This is a variation of the Mental State Examination (MSE) commonly used to assess the severity of cognitive impairment in dementia

Compare and contrast the MMSE with Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination (ACE).

The MMSE is a shorter, more basic test focusing on orientation, memory, and language
