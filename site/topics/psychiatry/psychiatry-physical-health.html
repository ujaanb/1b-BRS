Overview

What is a transient ischaemic attack (TIA)?

A rapidly developing focal disturbance of brain function of presumed vascular origin that resolves completely within 24 hours

Describe the difference between a stroke and a transient ischaemic attack (TIA).

Both are cerebrovascular accidents which involve the loss of blood supply to the brain, however a stroke lasts for ›24 hours, and a TIA is resolved within 24 hours

What are cataracts?

Cataracts are cloudy areas that develop in the lens of the eye, causing vision loss

Cataracts.

Recall what the abbreviation NKDA means.

No Known Drug Allergies (NKDA)

What is the Mental State Examination?

A mental state examination (MSE) is a structured assessment used to evaluate a patient's current mental state, focusing on their behavioural and cognitive functioning

Describe the purpose of the Mental State Examination.

  • The Mental State Examination (MSE) gives us a snapshot at a particular moment in time on what the mental state of a patient is like
  • NOTE: It is not used to gain insight into the medical history of the patient, but it is more focused on the present

How many components are there to the Mental State Examination (MSE)?

Nine

Recall the nine components of the Mental State Examination (MSE).

  • Appearance
  • Behaviour
  • Speech
  • Mood/Affect
  • Perceptual disturbances
  • Thought content
  • Thought form/process — i.e train of thought
  • Insight
  • Cognition

Describe how cognition can be measured in the Mental State Examination (MSE).*

  • A patient’s level of cognition can be assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA)
  • NOTE: Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE) can also be used

What is adjustment disorder?

An adjustment disorder is where someone experiences an excessive emotional or behavioural reaction to a stressful life event or change, often involving symptoms like anxiety, sadness, or withdrawal, that are more intense than expected

Describe what we would not expect to see in adjustment disorder.

  • Chronic presentation — Adjustment disorder typically resolves after 6 months
  • Psychotic symptoms are also typically not seen

What is psychotic depression?

Psychotic depression is a serious mental disorder where individuals experience both the symptoms of severe depression and psychosis,

which includes hallucinations and delusions What is less commonly seen in patients with psychotic depression?

  • Paranoid psychosis is rare — Typically a mood congruent psychosis is seen (i.e delusions of nihlism, guilt, Cotard’s syndrome)
  • Visual hallucinations

What is Cotard’s syndrome?

Cotard’s syndrome is the delusional belief that one is dead, non- existent, or has lost vital organs

When is paranoid psychosis more typically seen?

  • Schizophrenia-like illnesses
  • Drug-induced states

What are behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD)?

These are simply symptoms that are exhibited by dementia patients in the more advanced stages of the disease. They include — Agitation, aggression, depression, anxiety, apathy, delusions, hallucinations, and sleep disturbances

What is post-stroke psychosis?

  • Post-stroke psychosis is a condition that involves delusions and/or hallucinations occurring after a stroke, and it can present soon after the stroke or even weeks or months later
  • NOTE: “ Neuropsychiatric symptoms following stroke occur in at least 30% and are a major predictor of poor outcome ”

In which type of stroke is post-stroke psychosis most commonly seen?

Right sided middle cerebral artery (MCA) lesions affecting the frontal and temporal regions

Most Common Location Leading To Post-Stroke Psychosis.

What is the most commonly associated symptom with post-stroke psychosis?

  • Delusions are the most commonly reported psychotic symptom
  • Most delusions of a persecutory or jealous type (Othello’s syndrome)
  • NOTE: Othello syndrome is where someone is convinced, without evidence, that their partner is unfaithful, leading to irrational thoughts, behaviours, and potentially harmful actions

Describe the clinical presentation of post-stroke psychosis.

  • Post-stroke psychosis is characterised by delusions and hallucinations
  • Delusions are the most commonly reported psychotic symptom
  • The most common perceptual abnormalities are auditory hallucinations followed by visual

Describe the management of post-stroke psychosis.

There are no controlled studies looking at treatment although it is thought that there may be some response to antipsychotic medication

Describe why it may be difficult to treat older patients that exhibit psychotic symptoms.

There is an increased risk of stroke with antipsychotic use in those with dementia

Recall three physical conditions that may interplay with mental health conditions and how.

  • Cardiovascular disease — associated with 3x increased risk of depression/anxiety
  • MSK disorders — associated with 2x increased risk of depression
  • Diabetes — associated with 2x increased risk of depression
  • COPD — associated with 10x increased risk of panic disorder
  • NOTE: It is a bit of a “chicken-and-egg problem”, in the sense that it is difficult to pinpoint whether the mental disorders are causing the physical disorders or if it is the other way round

Recall five physical conditions that may directly cause mental health conditions and how.

  • Addison's disease → depression, poor concentration, irritability
  • Hypercalcaemia → depression, anxiety
  • Hyperthyroidism → anxiety, mania
  • Hypothyroidism → depression, cognitive impairment
  • Cushing’s syndrome → depression
  • Infections (syphilis, HIV) → psychosis, dementia
  • Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) → depression
  • Cancer → depression
  • Parkinson’s disease → depression, anxiety, dementia, psychosis
  • Phaeochromocytoma → anxiety
  • Dementia → psychosis, aggression/violence, depression, anxiety
  • Huntington’s disease → psychosis, aggression/violence, cognitive impairment, depression, anxiety
  • NOTE: The effects may be a direct result of the disease or treatments administered for the disease
  • NOTE: The first job of a psychiatrist is to exclude an organic cause for the patient’s presentation

Interplay Between Physical And Mental Health Conditions.

Recall five medications that can have adverse psychiatric side effects.

  • Dopamine agonists → psychosis
  • L-dopa → psychosis, delirium, anxiety, depression
  • Steroids (prednisolone) → depression, mania, psychosis, anxiety
  • Isoniazid (TB antibiotic) → mania, psychosis
  • Anticholinergics → delirium, anxiety, psychosis
  • Isotretinoin (Roaccutane) → depression
  • Digoxin → depression, psychosis
  • Interferon alpha → depression, mania, psychosis

What is meant by the “Mortality gap” in psychiatry?

The mortality gap describes how people with chronic mental illness are at greater risk of all cause mortality compared to the rest of the general population

Recall three causes of the mortality gap in psychiatry.

  • Medication adverse effects — i.e weight gain, dyslipidaemia, insulin insensitivity, hypertension, sedation)
  • Increased rates of smoking, illicit substance use and alcohol intake
  • Poor diet, dental hygiene and low levels of exercise
  • Chaotic lifestyles and low socioeconomic status

Describe three ways we can manage and decrease the mortality gap in psychiatric patients.

  • Choose medication that minimises impact on physical health — e.g. weight gain sparing antidepressants and antipsychotics in those already with increased BMI
  • Monitoring of cardiometabolic factors — i.e BMI, HbA1C, lipid profile, blood pressure
  • Ensure registered with a GP and dental practice
  • Dietary advice and smoking cessation
  • Drug and alcohol support services

Describe how routine can affect older patients.

  • People who are advancing in years are really good at maintaining a familiar routine
  • As soon as that changes, then they rapidly decompensate

What is Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination (ACE)?

Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination (ACE) is a brief, bedside cognitive screening test used to assess cognitive function, particularly in the context of dementia, and differentiate between Alzheimer's and frontotemporal dementia ADDENBROOKE’S COGNITIVE EXAMINATION (ACE).

What is delirium?

  • Delirium is an acute confusional state and a neuropsychiatric manifestation of physical illness/injury/interventions
  • It can be considered as “acute brain failure” instead of “chronic brain failure” (dementia)

What is meant by pseudodementia?

  • Pseudodementia describes how patients may sometimes present with cognitive impairment without traditional neurodegenerative cause
  • It is commonly caused by low mood, hence patients that present with cognitive impairment are often asked about their mood to rule out dementia

Describe the next step to take after mental examination.

Neural imaging (MRI)

Recall the first line treatment against Alzheimer’s.

Donepezil

What is donepezil?

  • Donepezil is a cholinesterase inhibitor that is used to treat Alzheimer’s disease
  • Alzheimer’s is caused by lower acetylcholine levels so it makes sense that medications include drugs that don’t break down acetylcholine

Recall the three classifications of delirium.

  • Hyperactive
  • Hypoactive
  • Mixed

Describe what is meant by hyperactive delirium.

Agitation, hallucinations, inappropriate behaviour

Describe what is meant by hypoactive delirium.

Lethargy, reduced concentration, reduced alertness, reduced oral intake

Describe what is meant by mixed delirium.

A combination of both symptoms from hyperactive and hypoactive delirium

Describe the epidemiology of delirium.

  • Affects 50% of those in hospital aged ›65
  • Complicates 80% of ITU admissions
  • May affect 14% of those ›85 in the community
  • Leads to increased mortality and delays in discharge

Recall three risk factors for delirium.

  • Advancing age
  • Cognitive impairment (e.g. dementia), sensory impairment
  • Poor nutrition
  • Polypharmacy/alcohol misuse
  • Frailty

Describe the common causes/precipitating factors of delirium.

  • Physical illness or injury — i.e infection, constipation, urinary retention, electrolyte disturbance, pain, acute vascular events, dehydration
  • NOTE: Pretty much anything can cause delirium in those sufficiently at risk!

Describe the pathophysiology of delirium.

The pathophysiology of delirium is poorly understood and likely multi- factorial

Describe two theories that may be used to explain the pathophysiology of delirium.

  • A critical illness leads to increased cortisol levels and cerebral hypoxia (older adults predisposed) which leads to ↓acetylcholine synthesis and dysfunction of hippocampal and neocortical areas (↑500 times dopamine and ↑adrenergic output) - (Maldonado, 2008)
  • Likely several neurobiological processes that contribute to delirium pathogenesis, including neuroinflammation, neurovascular dysfunction, altered brain metabolism, neurotransmitter imbalance and impaired neuronal network connectivity - (Wilson et al, 2020)

Describe four components of the management of delirium.

  • Anticipate and address any modifiable risk factors — i.e reducing polypharmacy, visual and hearing aids)
  • Optimise treatment of underlying co-morbidities
  • Treat any underlying cause — e.g. UTI, constipation, physical injury, electrolyte disturbance, dehydration)
  • Re-orientation strategies — familiar environments, use of clocks, reminding of name and current location
  • Normalise sleep-wake cycle
