Overview

What is intoxication?

  • In both the DSM and ICD, intoxication is considered to be a transient syndrome due to recent substance ingestion that produces clinically significant psychological or physical impairment
  • These changes disappear when the substance is eliminated from the body

What is the withdrawal state?

This refers to a group of signs and symptoms that occur when a drug is reduced in dose or withdrawn entirely

What is tolerance?

  • This is a state in which, after repeated administration, a drug produces a decreased effect
  • Increasing doses are therefore required to produce the same effect

What is harmful use?

A pattern of psychoactive substance use that is causing damage to health (physical or mental)

Describe how the definition of harmful use in the ICD-11 differs from ICD-10.

  • ICD-11 includes a new category to delineate single episodes of harmful use from a pattern of harmful use
  • Harmful use also now includes harm to health of others — This includes any form of physical harm, including trauma, or mental disorder that is directly attributable to behaviour related to substance use on the part of the person to whom the diagnosis of harmful pattern of use applies

Describe how the definition of dependence in the ICD-11 differs from ICD-10.

  • The former 6 diagnostic criteria for dependence from ICD 10 have been bundled into 3 pairs – fulfilment of one criterion is sufficient — Impaired control over substance use (i.e. onset, frequency, intensity, duration, termination, context) — Increasing precedence of substance use over other aspects of life (e.g. repeated relationship disruption, occupational or scholastic consequences, negative impact on health) — Physiological features indicative of neuroadaptation to the substance, (e.g. tolerance, withdrawal, use of pharmacologically similar substances to prevent or alleviate withdrawal symptoms).
  • The features of dependence are usually evident over a period of at least 12 months but the diagnosis may be made if use is continuous (daily or almost daily) for at least 3 months

Describe how DSM-5 differs from DSM-4.

  • Single continuum of Mild, Moderate, Severe (dimensional approach)
  • No longer uses the terms Abuse or Dependence. Term “use disorder” is preferred

Recall the nine components of the DSM-5 criteria for opioid use disorder.

  • Opioids are often taken in larger amounts or over a longer period than was intended
  • There is a persistent desire or unsuccessful efforts to cut down or control opioid use
  • A great deal of time is spent in activities necessary to obtain the opioid, use the opioid, or recover from its effects
  • Craving or a strong desire to use opioids
  • Recurrent opioid use resulting in a failure to fulfil major role obligations at work, school, or home
  • Continued opioid use despite having persistent or recurrent social or interpersonal problems caused or exacerbated by the effects of opioids
  • Important social, occupational, or recreational activities are given up or reduced because of opioid use
  • Recurrent opioid use in situations in which it is physically hazardous
  • Continued use despite knowledge of having a persistent or recurrent physical or psychological problem that is likely to have been caused or exacerbated by opioids
  • Tolerance, as defined by either of the following: — Need for markedly increased amounts of opioids to achieve intoxication or desired effect — Markedly diminished effect with continued use of the same amount of opioid
  • Withdrawal, as manifested by either of the following: — Characteristic opioid withdrawal syndrome — Same (or a closely related) substance is taken to relieve or avoid withdrawal symptoms
  • The presence of at least 2 of these symptoms indicates an Opioid Use Disorder (OUD)

When are withdrawal and tolerance not considered to have been met for opioid use disorder?

These criteria are not considered to be met for those individuals taking opioids under appropriate medical supervision

Describe how the severity of opioid use disorder varies. The severity of the OUD is defined as:

  • MILD: The presence of 2 to 3 symptoms
  • MODERATE: The presence of 4 to 5 symptoms
  • SEVERE: The presence of 6 or more symptoms

Recall the components of a clinical history for addictions.

  • Presenting complaint
  • History of presenting complaint
  • Substance misuse history
  • Family (psychiatric) history
  • Past psychiatric history
  • Personal history
  • Past medical history

Describe what we are looking for in the history of presenting complaint for an addiction history.

  • Duration of current problem/s
  • Onset
  • Precipitating factors
  • Signs and symptoms

Describe what we are looking for in the substance misuse history for an addiction history.

  • Length of current use and when last used
  • Current amount (units/grams/£ per day) and for how long at this level
  • Total length of use, maximum use and any periods of abstinence
  • Mode/method of administration (e.g. inhalation, ingestion, IV)
  • Evidence of withdrawal syndrome and severity (e.g. seizures, admissions)
  • Any previous treatments - medication, psychotherapy, detox/rehab admissions
  • Any previous substance overdoses (accidental vs deliberate)
  • Assess triggers to use substances/alcohol
  • Assess motivation to change/engage in treatment

Describe what we are looking for in the family (psychiatric) history for an addiction history.

Include both mental illnesses and addiction disorders that run in the family

Describe what we are looking for in the past psychiatric history for an addiction history.

  • Consider the presence of previous trauma including domestic violence, neglect and abuse (this feeds in to risk assessment)
  • Screen for developmental disorders especially ADHD (25% have comorbid ADHD)

Describe what we are looking for in the personal history for an addiction history.

  • Relationships - partner, family, children (violence in household?)
  • Safeguarding concerns?
  • Accommodation problems?
  • Money and debt? (how is use being funded)
  • Employed / Benefits
  • Forensic history - cautions, convictions, ongoing court cases, crimes committed but not prosecuted for

Describe what we are looking for in the past medical history for an addiction history.

  • Cirrhosis (Alcohol)
  • Endocarditis (IV use)
  • Abscesses (IV use)
  • Blood-borne virus (BBV): Hepatitis B/C & HIV (IV use) (ask about vaccinations)

Recall three of the major causes of morbidity and mortality associated with substance abuse.

  • Trauma — e.g. broken bones from fights
  • Road Traffic Accidents
  • Homicide
  • Suicide
  • Overdose (deliberate and accidental)

What is the equation for calculating units of alcohol?

Unit equation: % strength x ml /1000 = units

Recall the excretion rate for alcohol.

Excretion rate is 1 unit per hour

What percentage of the UK population drinks above the safe limits?

30% of the UK population drink above safe limits

Which gender represents the higher percentage of hospital admissions for alcohol?

Males

Which age group has the heaviest use of alcohol?

15-24 year olds have heaviest use

Describe the absorption of alcohol.

Alcohol is well absorbed from the mouth, stomach and small bowel and maximum blood concentration is reached within 60 minutes of ingestion

Describe the factors that affect alcohol absorption.

Alcohol absorption is slowed by food and sped up by the ingestion of effervescent drinks. It is hydrophilic and therefore widely distributed in all bodily tissues

Describe the pharmacodynamics of alcohol.

  • Alcohol enhances neurotransmission at GABA-A receptors (causing anxiolysis )
  • It also stimulates dopamine release in the mesolimbic system (causing reward ) and inhibits NMDA mediated glutamate release (leads to its amnesic effects )

Describe how alcohol is metabolised by the body.

  • Ethanol is oxidised by alcohol dehydrogenase →acetaldehyde
  • This is oxidised by acetaldehyde dehydrogenase to carbon dioxide and water
  • 98% of alcohol metabolism occurs in the liver and 1 unit of alcohol (8g) can be metabolised per hour

Recall another part of the body that can be affected by alcohol, other than the liver.

Retina

Describe how the retina may be affected by alcohol.

Illicit brew may contain methanol which is broken down to formaldehyde and causes marked toxicity on the retina

What is delirium tremens?

Delirium tremens is a rapid onset of confusion usually caused by withdrawal from alcohol

Describe what we are looking for upon examination within an alcohol assessment.

  • Jaundice
  • Bruising
  • Clubbing
  • Oedema
  • Ascites
  • Spider naevi

Describe the neurological signs used to detect alcohol misuse.

  • Consider Wernicke’s encephalopathy
  • Korsakoff’s syndrome (memory impairment)

Recall three features of Wernicke’s encephalopathy.

  • Ataxia
  • Confusion
  • Ophthalmoplegia WERNICKE’S ENCEPHALOPATHY.
  • Wernicke's encephalopathy is a serious neurological condition caused by a deficiency of thiamine (vitamin B1)
  • It's often associated with chronic alcohol abuse, which interferes with thiamine absorption and utilisation KORSAKOFF’S SYNDROME.
  • Korsakoff's syndrome is a chronic memory disorder primarily caused by thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency, often resulting from chronic alcohol abuse

Describe the investigations done to detect alcohol misuse.

  • Liver Fibro scan / Ultrasound
  • Bloods (LFTs, FBC, GGT, lipids, clotting, amylase)
  • Breathalyser
  • Urine Drug Screen

What is CAGE screening?

CAGE screening is a brief, four-question questionnaire used to

identify potential alcohol problems or dependence Describe the components of CAGE screening.

  • 1.Have you ever felt you needed to Cut down on your drinking?
  • 2.Have people Annoyed you by criticizing your drinking?
  • 3.Have you ever felt Guilty about drinking?
  • 4.Have you ever felt you needed a drink first thing in the morning (Eye- opener)

Describe the stages in the progression of alcohol withdrawal. Worsening pattern of symptoms over time

  • Onset usually from 6 hours, with initial symptoms including tremulousness, diaphoresis, fever, anxiety, agitation, nausea, vomiting and retching
  • Then progresses to hallucinations/seizures — NOTE: Hallucinations can actually occur at any time
  • Delirium tremens is a late sign and a medical emergency
  • NOTE: Alcohol withdrawal is more dangerous than opioid withdrawal

Alcohol Withdrawal Progression.

Describe the difference between opiates and opioids.

  • Opiates refer to natural opioids such as morphine and codeine and heroin to some extent
  • Opioids refer to all natural, semisynthetic and synthetic opioids

What do Opioids do?

  • Relieve pain - Analgesic effect
  • Create a sense of Euphoria in high doses
  • Endogenous opioids (endorphins) regulate pain and mood

Describe what we are looking for upon examination within an opioid misuse assessment.

  • Collapsed veins / track marks
  • Endocarditis (murmurs, splinter haemorrhages)
  • Skin abscesses
  • Signs of Hepatitis / HIV
  • Pneumonia

Describe the investigations done for opioid misuse.

  • Bloods (FBC, LFT, U&E, GGT, Glucose, CRP, BBB viral screen)
  • Breathalyser
  • Urine Drug Screen
  • Blood cultures (endocarditis)

Recall three clinical signs of opioid withdrawal.

  • Tachycardia
  • Sweating
  • Restlessness
  • Dilated pupils
  • Bone pain
  • Rhinorrhoea
  • Diarrhoea
  • Abdominal pain
  • Tremor
  • Yawning
  • Anxiety/Irritability
  • Gooseflesh skin

What is COWS?

This is the Clinical Opiate Withdrawal Scale and it is an 11-item scale designed to be administered by a clinician to reproducibly rate common signs and symptoms of opiate withdrawal and monitor these symptoms over time

How does opioid overdose typically happen?

Most are unintentional

Recall three clinical signs of opioid overdose.

  • Not moving and can’t be woken
  • Slow or no breathing
  • Choking, gurgling sounds or snoring
  • Tiny pupils
  • Clammy or cold skin
  • Blue lips or blue nails

Opioid Overdose.

What is Naloxone?

Naloxone is an opioid antagonist

Describe how Naloxone is used.

  • Naloxone: inject into upper arm or thigh (400mcg), or nasal spray (50% each nostril) — If no response after 3 mins, repeat
  • Provide airway support, recovery position

What are opioid agonists generally used for?

Pain

Recall four opioid agonists.

  • Codeine
  • Fentanyl
  • Morphine
  • Methadone
  • Hydromorphone
  • Oxycodone
  • Pethidine
  • Dextropropoxyphene

Which opioid agonist is also used for opioid dependence and why?

  • Methadone
  • It has a long half-life (15-60 hours)

Recall a partial opioid agonist.

Buprenorphine

What is Buprenorphine used for?

Pain/Opioid dependence

Recall two opioid antagonists.

  • Naltrexone
  • Naloxone

What is Naltrexone used for?

Opioid dependence

What is Naloxone used for?

Emergency treatment of opioid overdose
