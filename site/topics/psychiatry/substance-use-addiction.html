Overview

What is a drug?

A drug is a substance that changes a physiological system

What is a drug of abuse?

Drugs of recreational use largely refer to drugs which change the brain and its neurochemistry

Ghb, Gbl

Recall the two groups of reasons behind taking drugs.

  • Positive reinforcement
  • Negative reinforcement

What is positive reinforcement?

  • The process of encouraging or establishing a pattern of behaviour by offering reward when the behaviour is exhibited — i.e you feel good, something good happens to make you feel even better — i.e you’re in a good mood, someone gives you a cake to make you feel even better
  • To gain a positive state

What is negative reinforcement?

  • Negative reinforcement is the encouragement of certain behaviours by removing or avoiding a negative outcome or stimuli — i.e you feel bad, something good happens to make you feel better — i.e a loud noise is continuous and annoying, but when it stops you feel relief
  • Repeated negative reinforcement, in the context of persistent states of suffering/ distress/tension, is a central part of the development of addictions
  • To overcome an adverse state

Recall two reasons of positive reinforcement behind taking drugs.

  • Escapism
  • To get high
  • To stay awake
  • Because they like it

Recall two reasons of negative reinforcement behind taking drugs.

  • Boredom
  • To get to sleep
  • Reduce anxiety
  • To feel better

Describe why it is important to know the reason why someone takes drugs.

The reasons behind an individual taking drugs can help to direct and inform treatment

Describe the general course of alcohol/drug use. Like → Want → Need

  • Like — Experimental/‘recreational’ use: causing no/limited difficulties (majority of population)
  • Want — Increasingly regular use (fewer people), harmful
  • Need — Spiralling: dependence (smaller number)
  • As you go down this slippery slope, the number of problems caused by the drug use increases

What is dependence syndrome?

  • Dependence syndrome refers to a cluster of physiological, behavioural, and cognitive phenomena where a person's use of a substance takes precedence over other behaviours, despite potential harm
  • Need for a drug can easily spiral into dependence

Describe the six ICD-10 criteria used to characterise dependence syndrome.

  • a strong desire or sense of compulsion to take the substance
  • difficulties in controlling substance taking behaviour in terms of its onset, termination, or levels of use — who has control, you or ‘the drug/behaviour’? —

when did you last have a drink/drug?

  • physiological withdrawal state when substance use has stopped or been reduced — a ‘negative’ state (from uncomfortable to intolerable) so user takes drug/alcohol to get relief from it or ‘treat’ it
  • evidence of tolerance: need to take more to get same effect
  • progressive neglect of alternative interests
  • persisting with substance use despite clear evidence of overtly harmful consequences

Harm Caused By Drugs In The Uk.

What is addiction?

Addiction is compulsive drug use despite harmful consequences, characterised by an inability to stop using a drug, failure to meet work, social, or family obligations and (depending on the drug) tolerance and withdrawal

What is dependence?

  • Dependence refers to a physical adaptation to a substance — i.e Tolerance/withdrawal seen in opioid, benzodiazepine, alcohol usage
  • So one can be dependent and not addicted
  • NOTE: Dependence is more focused on what happens to an individual

when you stop taking the drug

NOTE: Tolerance means you need to take more drug to get the same effect

Describe the difference between addiction and dependence.

Addiction refers to the act of compulsive substance use despite adverse consequences but dependence refers to the fact that an individual has a physiological adaptation to a particular substance

Recall two addictive behaviours.

  • Gambling disorder
  • Internet gaming disorder

Describe how a drug can become more addictive.

  • Faster brain entry → more “rush” and more addiction
  • NOTE: Coca leaves ‹ Coca paste ‹ Cocaine powder ‹ Crack (smoked) — Increasing levels of drug addiction are due to increased speed of brain entry, not pharmacology of a drug

Recall the three factors that are involved in alcohol/drug use and addiction.

  • Social, environmental factors — e.g whether the drug is legal in the country
  • Personal factors — e.g genetic, personality traits
  • Drug factors — i.e pharmacokinetics, speed of entry

Describe the general stages in drug addiction from initial exposure to addiction.

  • Pre existing vulnerability — i.e family history, age
  • Drug exposure — Compensatory neuroadaptations to maintain brain function
  • Sustained recovery OR Cycles of remission and relapse

Describe what is meant by the “tolerance paradox” with regard to drug addiction.

  • The tolerance paradox describes the difficulties faced by a previously substance-addicted individual in remission who relapses and takes the substance again
  • The difficulty they face is that they are unable to tolerate as high of a dose as they could previously tolerate when they were addicted, so the same dosage could be potentially lethal to them when they are in remission

[Extra notes] THE TOLERANCE PARADOX.

The tolerance paradox in the context of drug addiction refers to the contradictory effects of drug tolerance, where increased drug use leads to both diminished effects and heightened risks at the same time.

How It Works: 1. Tolerance Development : As a person repeatedly uses a drug, their body adapts, requiring higher doses to achieve the same effect. 2. Increased Consumption : To counteract tolerance, users often take larger doses, which raises the risk of overdose. 3. Paradoxical Vulnerability : While the user becomes more tolerant to the drug's desired effects (e.g., euphoria), they may not develop the same level of tolerance to its dangerous effects (e.g., respiratory depression with opioids). 4. Relapse Risk : If a person abstains for a while, their tolerance drops. If they return to their previous high dose, their body may no longer handle it, increasing overdose risk. Real-World Example: A heroin user who has developed a high tolerance may not feel as much of a "high" but still experiences severe withdrawal symptoms. If they stop using for a while and then relapse at their previous dose, their tolerance may have decreased, leading to overdose and even death. This paradox highlights the complexities of addiction treatment and the dangers of escalating drug use. Describe how alcohol interacts with the stimulatory and inhibitory systems within the brain.

  • Alcohol works by altering the balance between the stimulatory and inhibitory systems within the brain
  • It reduces the potency of the stimulatory system, while increasing the potency of the inhibitory system

Recall the main receptor involved in the stimulatory system within the brain for alcohol and its homogenous ligand.

  • NMDA receptor
  • Its ligand is glutamate

Describe the role of glutamate.

Glutamate is the primary excitatory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system, and it plays a crucial role in learning, memory, mood regulation, and pain signalling

Recall the main receptor involved in the inhibitory system within the brain for alcohol and its homogenous ligand.

  • GABA-A receptor
  • Its ligand is GABA

Describe the role of GABA.

GABA reduces neuronal excitability by blocking signals and promoting a calming effect, crucial for regulating brain activity, sleep, and mood

Describe the effects of the way alcohol interacts with the stimulatory and inhibitory systems of the brain. Blocks excitatory system

  • Impaired memory (alcoholic blackouts) Boosts inhibitory system
  • Anxiolysis (less anxiety)
  • Sedation

Alcohol On The Stimulatory And Inhibitory Systems.

Describe the neuroadaptations that occur in the brain as a result of chronic alcohol exposure.

The brain tries to overcome the effects of the alcohol induced NMDA receptor blockade and so it up-regulates the number of NMDA receptors, while down-regulating the number of GABA-A receptors — This leads to a massive increase in activity of the glutamate system, with a simultaneous deficiency of the GABA system

Describe how anxiety can arise after chronic alcohol exposure.

  • During the period shortly after alcohol is removed (hangover), there is a massive increase in activity of the glutamate system, with a simultaneous deficiency of the GABA system as the alcohol is cleared out of the body, but the brain does not respond as quickly
  • This can lead to a phenomenon known as “hangxiety”

Describe how this anxiety can be treated.

  • Patients are treated with benzodiazepines to boost GABA function
  • NOTE: Benzodiazepines work by increasing GABA activity

Recall the three “models of addiction”.

  • Reward deficiency (positive reinforcement)
  • Overcoming adverse state (negative reinforcement) — i.e withdrawal, anxiety
  • Impulsivity/compulsivity
  • NOTE: These models overlap to some extent

Recall the major neurotransmitter involved in the brain’s reward system.

  • Dopamine
  • NOTE: Natural rewards such as food, sex increase levels of dopamine. Some drugs of abuse also increase dopamine levels — i.e cocaine, alcohol

Recall where the dopamine reward system is located.

  • Ventral striatum
  • Pathways range from the ventral tegmental area (VTA) → frontal cortex (mesocortical pathway and ventral tegmental area (VTA) → ventral striatum (mesolimbic) regions
  • These dopamine pathways have been referred to as the ‘pleasure- reward-motivation’ system

Nigrostriatal Pathway.

Recall two modulators of the dopamine reward system and the effect they have.

  • μ(mu)-Opioids, Glutamate - Amplify reward system
  • GABA-B - Suppress reward system
  • Cannabinoids - Amplify reward system, mixed effects

Describe how addiction is described based on reward.

Addiction has been conceptualised as a ‘reward deficient’ state — This leads to increased substance use to compensate for a blunted natural reward system

Describe how cocaine and amphetamine specifically interact with the dopamine reward system pathway.

  • Cocaine and amphetamine block the reuptake of dopamine by locking onto the dopamine transporters
  • Amphetamine also enhances the release of dopamine

Describe how drugs and alcohol interact with the dopamine reward system pathway.

Drugs like nicotine and alcohol can increase the firing rate of the presynaptic dopamine neurons

Describe how the activity of the reward system pathway can be assessed.

  • Activity of the reward system pathway can be assessed using the monetary incentive delay task
  • This is performed to assess whether or not those with addictions have problems with responding to normal rewards such as money — i.e if they have a deficient/blunted reward system

Describe how the monetary incentive delay task works.

  • It works by assessing the functional activity of the brain in an MRI scanner
  • A person is initially informed on the screen whether or not they can win money in the next few seconds. This is the cue and the person will have to respond to a target to win this money — Usually, there should be increased activity in the reward system pathway following this cue
  • There is then a delay, and then the target appears — Usually, there should also be increased activity in the reward system pathway following the appearance of the target
  • The more reward that a person gets, the more activity there is in the ventral striatum area of the brain

Monetary Incentive Delay Task.

Describe how the monetary incentive delay task can be used to assess the reward system in abstinent addicts.

In abstinent addicts, dependents or those with polydrug use there is evident blunted activation of reward system compared with controls — This is consistent with the ‘reward deficiency’ theories of addiction

Results Of Monetary Incentive Delay Task.

Describe the effect that low activity levels of the dopamine reward pathway can have on likelihood for relapse.

In abstinent addicts, those with blunted response in the brain to ‘anticipation of reward’ are more likely to relapse — This is consistent with the ‘reward deficiency’ theories of addiction, because these individuals are looking for a stronger stimulus to activate their reward pathways

Recall the three “stages of addiction”.

  • Binge/intoxication
  • Withdrawal/negative affect
  • Preoccupation/anticipation - “craving”
  • NOTE: It is like a cycle
