Overview

Which vertebral level is the renal hilum level with?

L1 vertebra

Urinary Tract.

Describe the general role of the kidneys.

To remove waste products of metabolism, excess water and salts from the blood and maintain its pH

Describe the role of the ureters.

  • To convey urine from the kidneys to the urinary bladder. Approximately how long is each ureter?
  • Each one is about 25cm long, its upper half lies in the abdomen and lower half in the pelvis. Approximately what is the diameter of each ureters?
  • The ureters are approximately 3mm in diameter

Describe the structure of the layers of the ureters.

The ureters have 3 layers of tissue — Outer fibrous tissue, middle muscle layer and inner epithelium layer

Layers Of The Ureteric Wall.

Describe the blood supply of the ureters.

Renal/lumbar/gonadal/common iliac, internal iliac and superior vesical arteries with corresponding venous drainage — different sections of the ureter are supplied by different arteries

Arteries Of The Lumbosacral Region.

Describe the lymphatic drainage of the left ureter.

Left ureter drains into left para-aortic nodes

Para-Aortic Lymph Nodes.

Describe the lymphatic drainage of the right ureter.

Right ureter drains into right paracaval and interaortocaval lymph nodes

Paracaval And Interaortocaval Lymph Nodes.

What is the role of the cisterna chyli?

  • It is an expansion of the lymph tissue that forms the thoracic duct
  • It is formed by the unison of the intestinal duct and left+right lumbar ducts

Describe the nerve supply of the ureters.

Autonomic nervous system — controls the contractions of ureter to pass urine down

Describe the course of the ureters.

  • Ureters descend in front of the tips of transverse spinous processes, cross sacro-iliac joint, then forwards next to rectum/ vagina Are the kidneys retroperitoneal or intraperitoneal?
  • Retroperitoneal

Describe the positional location of the bladder within the pelvis.

The bladder sits anteriorly within the pelvis

Describe the arterial supply of the kidneys.

Renal arteries, which are direct branches off the aorta on each side

Describe the venous drainage of the kidneys.

Renal veins, which directly feed into the inferior vena cava (IVC) on each side

Arterial Supply And Venous Drainage Of The Kidneys.

Describe the course of lymphatic fluid from the lower urinary tract.

The majority of the lymphatic fluid from the lower urinary tract will drain into para aortic nodes, which drain into the lumbar trunk → cisterna chyli → thoracic duct

Abdominal Lymphatic Drainage.

Describe how the position of the kidneys change during and after embryological development.

  • The kidneys are derived from the metanephric mesenchyme which differentiates into the kidneys when it comes into contact with the ureteric bud
  • There is a migration of this differentiated structure up the retroperitoneum to reach L1 vertebral level and form the kidneys

Positional Adjustment Of Kidneys During Development.

Recall two anatomic variations of the kidney.

  • Single kidney — 1% of the population
  • Horseshoe kidney
  • Ectopic kidney

What is horseshoe kidney?

  • This is a genetic disorder that is characterised by the left and right kidneys being joined together and not being separated as they should be
  • The kidney is also often positioned lower than it should be

What is ectopic kidney?

An ectopic kidney is a birth defect where one or both kidneys are not in their normal location within the abdomen, but are found elsewhere — they may instead be found in the pelvis, chest, or even the opposite side of the body

Horseshoe Kidney.

Recall two anatomic variations of the ureters.

  • Partial duplication
  • Complete duplication

What is ureteric duplication?

This is a congenital condition where a single kidney has two ureters instead of one

Recall the two forms of ureteric duplication.

  • Partial duplication
  • Complete duplication

Describe the difference between the two forms of ureteric duplication.

  • In partial duplication, there are two ureters that emerge from the kidney, however t hey join up together to form one ureter that drains into the bladder
  • In complete duplication, there are two ureters that emerge from the kidney, and they do join up together, so two ureters drain into the bladder

Ureteric Duplication.

What is meant by a “hold up point” within the ureter?

These are locations within the ureter that constrict and block urine flow or where kidney stones can get trapped if they are dislodged from the kidney — these are painful and cause ipsilateral renal dysfunction

Recall three common “hold-up points” within the ureter.

(see lecture diagram / notes)

Where the renal pelvis joins the top of the ureter - pelvic ureteric junction (PUJ, or UPJ)

  • Pelvic brim, crossing the iliac vessels
  • As it passes through the bladder wall; uretero-vesical junction (UVJ, or VUJ)

Sites Of Kidney/Ureteric Pain.

What is the main function of the urinary bladder?

The urinary bladder is the reservoir of urine

What type of epithelium lines the urinary bladder?

Transitional epithelium

Describe how the musculature of the urinary bladder is adapted to perform its function.

  • Within the bladder, the smooth muscle can increase in length up to eight times, which allows it to have the property of adaptive relaxation
  • This means as the bladder fills, the smooth muscle becomes even longer and the tension within it reduces
  • This makes sense as the bladder is a storage mechanism

Describe how the location of the urinary bladder can change.

When empty, the bladder is in the pelvis, when distended it is an abdomino-pelvic organ. The dome of the bladder moves upwards — This is due to its special property, adaptive relaxation. No matter

how much fluid is within the bladder, the pressure remains the same as the smooth muscle adjusts to change its volume

(see lecture diagram / notes)

Describe the anatomical structure of the urinary bladder.

  • An empty bladder is a 4-sided pyramid in shape, has 4 angles (apex, neck and 2 lateral angles) and 4 surfaces (base/posterior surface, 2 infero-lateral surfaces and a superior surface)
  • NOTE: This structure of the bladder typically refers to how it exists ex- vivo. In-vivo, the bladder is surrounded by a lot of other structures, so this structure is fairly irrelevant

Surfaces Of The Bladder.

What is the function of the internal sphincter in the bladder?

It is smooth muscle at the neck of the bladder that provides involuntary control to prevent urination

When it contracts, it closes the bladder to prevent urination

(see lecture diagram / notes)

What is the function of the external sphincter in the bladder?

It is striated muscle that provides voluntary control to prevent urination

When it contracts, it closes the bladder to prevent urination Describe the difference between the male and female external urethral sphincters.

When the male external urethral sphincter contracts, it constricts and shuts the urethra and completely prevents the flow of urine

through it

(see lecture diagram / notes)

When the female external urethral sphincter contracts, it kinks and angulates the urethra and prevents the flow of urine through it

(see lecture diagram / notes)

Male Vs. Female Urethra.

Describe the blood supply of the urinary bladder.

  • Arterial - superior and inferior vesical branches of internal iliac artery
  • Venous - drained by vesical plexus which drains into internal iliac vein

Describe the lymphatic drainage of the urinary bladder.

Internal iliac nodes and then paraaortic nodes

Describe the nerve supply of the urinary bladder.

  • Autonomic nervous system Is the bladder retroperitoneal or intraperitoneal?
  • Retroperitoneal

Pelvic Vasculature.

Describe the course of the urethra in the female urinary tract.

Urethra carries urine from the bladder to the external urethral meatus in the vaginal vestibule

What is the vaginal vestibule?

  • This is the part of the vagina that is located within the labia minora (inner labia) and it houses the external urethral meatus Approximately how long is the female urethra?
  • 3-4cm

Describe the action of the external urethral sphincter in females.

It is skeletal muscle that can provide tonic contraction and also voluntary “guarding” — Voluntary guarding is what prevents stress incontinence, when we sneeze or cough for example

What is the innervation of the female external urethral sphincter?

Pudendal nerve

Describe the blood supply of the female urinary tract.

Internal pudendal arteries and inferior vesical branches of the vaginal arteries with corresponding venous drainage

Describe the lymphatic supply of the female urinary tract.

Proximal urethra into internal iliac nodes, distal urethra to superficial inguinal lymph nodes

Describe the nerve supply of the female urinary tract.

  • Vesical plexus (proximal)
  • Pudendal nerve (distal)

Female Urinary Tract.

Explain the use of the term “genito-urinary tract.”

  • The term genito-urinary tract is used for males
  • This is due to the convergence of the ejaculatory ducts into the prostate, which is located inferior to the bladder

Recall an additional muscle group found within the genito-urinary tract that is not found within the female urinary tract.

The bladder neck; a sphincter which stays shut except when voiding

Bladder Neck.

Describe the nervous control of the bladder neck.

Sympathetic nervous system (SNS)

Describe the structure of the prostate gland.

4x3x2cm, conical shape, 2 (lateral) lobes, surrounds the proximal urethra

Describe the role of the prostate gland.

Secretes 75% of the seminal fluid which liquifies coagulated semen

after deposition in the female genital tract Describe the action of the external urethral sphincter in males.

  • It is skeletal muscle that can provide tonic contraction and also voluntary “guarding” — Voluntary guarding is what prevents stress incontinence, when we sneeze or cough for example
  • It opens for ejaculation

What is the innervation of the male external urethral sphincter?

Pudendal nerve

Describe the blood supply of the genito-urinary tract.

  • Prostate - inferior vesical artery
  • Urethra - bulbourethral artery and internal pudendal artery with corresponding venous drainage

Describe the lymphatic supply of the genito-urinary tract.

Prostatic and membranous urethra drain to obturator and internal iliac nodes, spongy urethra to deep and superficial inguinal nodes

Describe the nerve supply of the genito-urinary tract.

  • Vesical plexus (proximal)
  • Pudendal nerve (distal)

What is retrograde ejaculation?

This is when semen is released backward into the bladder instead of being ejaculated through the urethra

Genito-Urinary Tract.

What is micturition?

Micturition is the act of urinating

What is the micturition cycle?

This describes the stages that are involved before, during and after micturition

Recall the two components of the micturition cycle.

  • Storage
  • Voiding

Describe what is happening in the storage phase of the micturition cycle.

  • Bladder relaxed, serving as reservoir
  • Outlet contracted, preventing leaks

Describe what is happening in the voiding phase of the micturition cycle.

  • Bladder contracting, expelling the urine.
  • Outlet relaxed, permitting flow.
  • Bladder should empty fully (‹50 ml “post void residual”)

Recall how many times we urinate per day.

6 pees daily, ~ 20 secs each = 2 mins per day spent voiding

Which structure is responsible for the controlling the switch from storage to voiding in the micturition cycle?

Pontine micturition centre

What is the pontine micturition centre?

This is a key region of the brainstem located in the pons that is responsible for controlling urination

What controls the pontine micturition centre and why?

  • Prefrontal cortex
  • This is because the brainstem doesn’t receive information on when it is appropriate for us to urinate (i.e in public/private), so the prefrontal cortex helps to provide information on when the time is appropriate

Describe the neuronal changes that occur following the switch from storage to voiding.

  • Prefrontal cortex permits the pontine micturition centre in the brainstem to change from storage mode to voiding
  • This activates the parasympathetic nucleus (bladder contraction), and inhibits Onuf’s nucleus (sphincter relaxation)

What is Onuf’s nucleus?

  • Onuf's nucleus is a distinct group of somatic motor neurons located in the ventral part of the anterior horn of the sacral region of the spinal cord
  • It is the origin of the nerves that supply the external urethral sphincter muscles — i.e pudendal
  • Its inhibition hence leads to micturition, as the external urethral sphincter will stop contracting, and relax

Neuronal Control Of Micturition.

Recall six regions of central nervous system (CNS) that are involved in micturition.

  • Frontal cortex
  • Pontine micturition centre
  • Periaqueductal gray (PAG)
  • Thoracic spinal cord
  • Sacral spinal cord
  • Limbic cortex

Describe how the frontal cortex is involved in micturition.

Decides actions of bladder and sphincter muscles based on planning ahead, social appropriateness, etc

Describe how the pontine micturition centre is involved in micturition.

Co-ordinates spinal centres (thoracic and sacral centres); storage switches to voiding only if permitted

What is the periaqueductal gray (PAG)?

This is a region of gray matter located adjacent to the cerebral aqueducts of the brainstem

Describe how the periaqueductal gray (PAG) is involved in micturition.

Receives sensory information from viscera (subconscious), decides

what goes to cortex (conscious sensation) Describe how the thoracic spinal cord is involved in micturition.

Sympathetic nucleus (bladder neck)

Describe how the sacral spinal cord is involved in micturition.

  • Parasympathetic nucleus (detrusor)
  • Onuf’s nucleus (sphincter)

Describe how the limbic cortex is involved in micturition.

Emotional and fear reactions — i.e urinating oneself when scared

Physiology Of Micturition.

Describe the control of micturition in infants.

In infants, micturition is a local spinal reflex in which bladder empties upon reaching a critical pressure

Describe the difference between the control of micturition in adults and infants.

  • In infants, micturition is a local spinal reflex in which bladder empties on reaching a critical pressure
  • In adults, voiding can be initiated or inhibited by higher centre control of the external urethral sphincter keeping it closed until it is appropriate to urinate

Describe the role of the parasympathetic nervous system (PNS) when innervating the pelvic organs.

The PNS innervates the detrusor muscle

Describe the role of the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) when innervating the pelvic organs.

The SNS innervates the bladder neck (in men), testicles and kidneys

Describe the origin and end of the pudendal nerve.

  • The pudendal nerve originates from the fusion of three spinal nerves, S2, S3 and S4
  • It then gives off three branches: — Inferior rectal nerve — Perineal nerve — Dorsal nerve of penis/clitoris

Describe the role of the pudendal nerve when innervating the pelvic organs.

  • Penis (erectile function)
  • Vaginal vestibule (clitoris)

Recall an autonomic receptor drug target for the bladder neck and the effects at the target.

α-adrenergic (α-1)- alpha blocker, eg tamsulosin — prevents contraction, allows for smooth urination

Recall two autonomic receptor drug target for the detrusor muscle and the effects at the targets.

  • Cholinergic M3/ M2, Antimuscarinic, eg oxybutynin, solifenacin — Decreased bladder contractions → Increased Storage capacity
  • β-adrenergic, β3 agonist eg mirabegron — Promote relaxation → more storage, less urgency

Recall an autonomic receptor drug target for the erectile tissue and the effects at the target.

nitrergic. PDE5 inhibitor — PDE5 inhibitors promote smooth muscle relaxation, which allows more blood to flow into the penis — This is useful for treating erectile dysfunction

Pelvic Organ Nerve Supply.

What is urinary incontinence?

Urinary incontinence is any involuntary loss of urine

What is stress urinary incontinence?

This is a complaint of involuntary leakage on effort or exertion, or on sneezing or coughing — i.e increase of intra-abdominal pressure

Recall the common demographic affected by stress urinary incontinence.

More common in older women

Recall the prevalence of stress urinary incontinence.

(see lecture diagram / notes)

Can affect up to 40% of women, with 1 in 5 women over 40 having some degree of stress incontinence Recall three risk factors for stress urinary incontinence.

  • Ageing
  • Obesity
  • Smoking
  • Pregnancy
  • Route of delivery

Describe the pathology behind stress urinary incontinence.

Impaired bladder and urethral support and impaired urethral closure

Stress Urinary Incontinence.

Recall the symptoms of stress urinary incontinence.

Involuntary leakage from urethra with exertion/effort or sneezing or coughing

Describe the investigations done for stress urinary incontinence.

  • History and examination; descent of pelvic floor on vaginal examination, positive stress test (visible loss of urine on inspection).
  • Urodynamics - urinary leakage during an increase in intrabdominal pressure in the absence of a detrusor contraction

Describe the management of stress urinary incontinence.

  • Non-surgical: physiotherapist teaching pelvic floor muscle exercises.
  • Surgical: a sling placed to support the urethra, using the anterior vaginal wall to support the urethra (colposuspension), periurethral bulking injection

What is the urodynamics test?

This is a diagnostic test used to test for stress urinary incontinence

Describe what happens during a urodynamics test.

  • Blue lines represent a cough, black line at the bottom represents urine leakage
  • Patient is told to cough three times in quick succession. Even in stress incontinence, there should be some voluntary guarding initially, however the reflex isn’t quick enough to return, and so there is some urine leakage upon the second/third coughs

What is overactive bladder?

This is urinary urgency, usually with urinary frequency and nocturia, with or without urgency urinary incontinence

Recall another name for overactive bladder.

Urgency incontinence

Describe the prevalence of overactive bladder.

Overall prevalence of 16.6% in men and women over 40

Recall three risk factors for overactive bladder.

  • Age
  • Prolapse
  • Increased BMI
  • Bladder irritants (caffeine, nicotine)

Describe the pathology behind overactive bladder.

  • Not well understood
  • Involuntary "overactive" detrusor (bladder wall) muscle contractions — Cause can be idiopathic or neurogenic (loss of central nervous system inhibitory pathways)

Urodynamics - Overactive Bladder.

Recall the symptoms of overactive bladder.

  • Urgency, frequency, nocturia and urgency incontinence
  • Impact on quality of life (QOL) due to sleep disruption, anxiety and depression

Recall three investigations done for overactive bladder.

  • Assess for enlarged prostate in males and prolapse in women
  • Exclude infection with urine dip/MSU
  • Bladder diary
  • Bladder scan (post void residual)
  • [Urodynamics]

Recall three possible management options for overactive bladder.

  • Behavioural/lifestyle changes
  • Bladder retraining
  • Antimuscarinic drugs
  • Beta-3 agonist
  • Bladder injections with botox
  • Neuromodulation
  • Augmentation cystoplasty — enlargement of bladder, increases its capacity

What is the role of the prostate?

Secretes 75% of the seminal fluid which liquifies coagulated semen

after deposition in the female genital tract What is benign prostatic hyperplasia?

This is non malignant growth or hyperplasia of prostate tissue, and it is a common cause of lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) in men

Describe the incidence of benign prostatic hyperplasia.

  • Increases with advancing age
  • 50-60% for males in their 60’s, increasing to 80-90% for those over 70yrs of age

How can benign prostatic hyperplasia be detected?

Outward enlargement can be felt with rectal exam

Recall a risk factor for benign prostatic hyperplasia.

Hormonal effects of testosterone on prostate tissue
