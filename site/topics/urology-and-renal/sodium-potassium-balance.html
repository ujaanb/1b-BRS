Overview

What is osmolarity?

  • Osmolarity is a measure of the concentration of all solute particles in a solution — i.e a cumulative concentration of all the solutes. — It is measured in osmoles/litre
  • NOTE: The greater the number of dissolved particles, the greater the osmolarity

What is an osmole?

  • 1 Osmole = 1 mole of dissolved particles per litre — The greater the number of dissolved particles, the greater the osmolarity
  • 1 mole of NaCl = 2 moles of particles in solution — This is because NaCl dissociates into Na+ and Cl- in water Table showing how osmolarity is measured.
  • The key principle is that each ionic compound is split into its constituents and each constituent is counted as an osmole.

Plasma Vs Urine Osmolarity.

Describe how the regulation of water and salt balance are inter-related.

  • Water and salt balance within the body is regulated by semi- permeable membranes around cells that allow water to move in and out of cells
  • Increased salt presence outside of cells will increase water presence in that same area — i.e via H2O efflux
  • Likewise, decreased salt presence outside of cells will also decrease water presence in that area

What is extracellular fluid?

Extracellular fluid (ECF) refers to all body fluid outside the cells of any multicellular organism

Describe how the osmolarity and volume of the extracellular fluid (ECF) are related.

  • Concentration (osmolarity) = number of milliosmoles / volume
  • Hence, osmolarity is measured in mOsm/L

Recall the normal plasma osmolarity range.

285-295 mOsm/L

Recall the most prevalent and important solute in the extracellular fluid (ECF).

Sodium (140 mmol/L) — It generally dictates the volume of the extracellular fluid (ECF)

Recall three other solutes in the extracellular fluid (ECF).

  • Chloride (105 mmol/L)
  • Bicarbonate (24 mmol/L)
  • Potassium (4 mmol/L)
  • Glucose (3-8 mmol/L)
  • Calcium (2 mmol/L)
  • Protein (1 mmol/L)

Describe the relationship between dietary sodium and body weight.

  • Increased dietary sodium → Increased total body sodium
  • Increased total body sodium → Increased osmolarity (not possible due to autonomic water-salt balance regulation)
  • Increased osmolarity → Increased water intake and retention — technically, it is actually osmolality that induces thirst
  • Increased water intake and retention → Increased ECF volume
  • Increased ECF volume → Increased body weight
  • NOTE: This follows the same pattern for decreased dietary sodium

Describe the relationship between total body sodium and blood pressure.

  • Increased ECF volume induced by increased sodium and water intake → Increased blood volume and pressure
  • NOTE: This follows the same pattern for decreased dietary sodium

What is euvolaemia?

Euvolaemia is a medical term that means having the normal amount of blood and fluids in the body

Recall which part of the brain is involved in the regulation of sodium intake.

Lateral parabrachial nucleus

Recall the two neurotransmitters that work to inhibit sodium intake.

  • Serotonin
  • Glutamate

Recall the two neurotransmitters that work to increase sodium appetite.

  • GABA
  • Opioids

Describe how the regulation of sodium intake works.

  • At euvolemic (normal) sodium levels, intake of sodium is inhibited by serotonin and glutamate
  • At low sodium levels, appetite for sodium is increased by GABA and opioids

Describe how sodium can be appetitive or aversive.

  • Low levels of sodium in food can be tasty and appetitive
  • High levels of sodium in food can be aversive

Appetitive Or Aversive Nature Of Sodium.

Describe the fate of most of our sodium when it enters the renal system.

Most of the sodium that enters the renal system is actually reabsorbed from the nephrons

Recall the percentage of sodium that is reabsorbed in the nephron.

~99%

Describe how sodium is reabsorbed in the nephron.

  • ~67% is absorbed in the proximal convoluted tubule
  • ~25% is absorbed in the thick ascending limb
  • ~5% is absorbed in the distal convoluted tubule
  • ~3% is absorbed in the collecting duct
  • Hence, ~1% of sodium is excreted back into the urine

Fate Of Sodium In The Nephron.

What is renal plasma flow (RPF)?

The volume of blood plasma that flows into the kidney per unit of time

What percentage of renal plasma flow enters the tubular system for filtration?

  • ~20%
  • Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR) = Renal Plasma Flow * 0.2
  • This means if 600 mL/min plasma reaches the kidneys: — 120 mL/min is filtered into Bowman’s space — 480 mL/min continues through the peritubular capillaries as unfiltered plasma

Describe why it is not helpful to have a high glomerular filtration rate (GFR) with regards to sodium.

  • Having a high glomerular filtration rate (GFR) leads to a higher amount (not percentage) of sodium being excreted from the body
  • Since sodium is essential for us to maintain our water balance, this is not helpful

Describe the relationship between glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and renal plasma flow (RPF).

  • Initially at lower blood pressures, glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and renal plasma flow are directly proportional
  • However, as you get to higher renal plasma flow levels, the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) begins to plateau and it no longer increases
  • This is effective as in high blood pressure states such as exercise, you don’t want to excrete too much sodium as it will affect the salt-water balance of the body

Gfr And Rpf.

What is the macula densa?

The macula densa is a group of cells in the kidney that detect changes in salt levels in the urine

Increased Tubular Sodium And The Macula Densa.

Describe the relationship between sodium excretion and glomerular filtration rate (GFR).

Increasing glomerular filtration rate (GFR) leads to increased volumes of sodium being excreted

Describe the relationship between tubular sodium and the macula densa.

  • When sodium levels within the tubular system increase, the macula densa cells uptake NaCl via their Na+/Cl-/K+ triple transporter and produce more ATP, which is broken down to adenosine
  • This leads to contraction of the afferent arterioles, reduced perfusion and reductions in glomerular filtration rate (GFR)

Other than increasing reabsorption of sodium, describe an alternative way of increasing sodium retention.

  • Decreasing filtration of sodium — i.e reducing amount of sodium going from blood to glomerulus
  • NOTE: GFR = RPF x 0.2. If the renal plasma flow is lowered, the GFR will also be lowered

Describe how filtration of sodium can be reduced, with reference to the renal blood supply.

  • This is done by lowering the pressure within the afferent arteriole, which reduces blood flow into the glomerular capillaries
  • This lowers glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure, decreasing the pressure gradient between glomerular capillaries and Bowman’s space
  • As a result, GFR decreases, and less sodium is filtered

Describe how afferent arteriole pressure within the glomerular capillaries is reduced.

Sympathetic activity directly stimulates contraction of the smooth muscle cells within the afferent arteriole

Describe three effects of sympathetic activity on sodium filtration.

  • Sympathetic activity directly stimulates contraction of the smooth muscle cells within the afferent arteriole — This reduces renal plasma flow and hence GFR
  • It also directly stimulates uptake of sodium into the proximal convoluted tubule cells — NOTE: The PCT cells facilitate sodium reabsorption
  • It also stimulates juxtaglomerular (JGA) cells to produce angiotensin II which stimulates the cells of the proximal convoluted tubule to uptake sodium

Recall an effect of angiotensin II on sodium retention.

  • Angiotensin II leads to the production of aldosterone
  • This aldosterone production leads to increased sodium retention

Explain the effect of aldosterone on sodium retention.

Aldosterone directly stimulates the cells of the distal convoluted tubule (DCT) and collecting duct to uptake sodium — i.e for reabsorption

Recall another stimulatory factor for sodium retention other than sympathetic activity.

Low tubular Na+

Describe how low tubular Na+ levels increases sodium retention.

  • Low tubular Na+ levels are detected by the macula densa cells of the juxtaglomerular apparatus
  • The macula densa responds by reducing ATP/adenosine release, which reduces afferent arteriole constriction, signalling to juxtaglomerular cells to increase renin secretion
  • This increased renin production by the juxtaglomerular cells leads to angiotensin II and aldosterone production
  • These molecules increase sodium retention within the proximal convoluted tubule, distal convoluted tubule and collecting ducts

Recall the main triggers involved in propagating sodium retention within the renal system.

  • Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone system
  • Sympathetic activity also

Recall the main molecule involved in propagating sodium excretion within the renal system.

Atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP)

Describe how atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) decreases sodium retention.

  • It inhibits afferent arteriole smooth muscle contraction to increase renal plasma flow
  • It decreases renin production by juxtaglomerular cells
  • It directly inhibits uptake of sodium into the proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) cells, distal convoluted tubule (DCT) cells, collecting duct cells

Describe how sodium excretion is altered in low sodium conditions.

  • Low sodium levels induce renin production which leads to angiotensin II and aldosterone production — via tubulo-glomerular feedback using macula densa
  • Sympathetic activity also induces afferent arteriole constriction, angiotensin II production from JGA cells and PCT reabsorption
  • Both of these induce vasoconstriction as well as increased sodium retention

Describe how sodium excretion is altered in high sodium conditions.

  • High sodium levels reduce renin production which also reduces angiotensin II and aldosterone production
  • Both of these molecules (angiotensin II and aldosterone) induce vasoconstriction as well as increased sodium retention respectively, so reduced levels of them leads to increased sodium excretion

Low Vs High Sodium Levels.

Recall what type of hormone aldosterone is.

Steroid hormone

Recall where aldosterone is produced.

Zona glomerulosa of the adrenal cortex

Recall two things that directly stimulates the production of aldosterone.

  • Angiotensin II
  • Reduction in blood pressure — detected by baroreceptors

Describe how angiotensin II actually induces aldosterone production.

  • Angiotensin II leads to the production of aldosterone synthase
  • This enzyme is use to catalyse the final two steps of aldosterone production from cholesterol

Describe three major effects of aldosterone.

  • Increased sodium reabsorption — controls reabsorption of 35g Na+/day
  • Increased potassium secretion
  • Increased hydrogen ion (H+) secretion

Describe the effects of aldosterone excess.

Hypokalaemic alkalosis — i.e too little potassium, too little H+

Recall the two main channels used by aldosterone to increase sodium levels.

  • Epithelial sodium channel (ENaC)
  • Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase pump

Recall the location of the epithelial sodium channel (ENaC).

  • Apical membrane of principal cells in the distal convoluted tubule (DCT) and collecting duct
  • NOTE: Apical membrane is the membrane on the side of the nephron, not the blood

Describe how the epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) works.

The ENaC allows for passive sodium entry from the tubular filtrate into principal cells, driven by the Na⁺ gradient

Describe how the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase pump works.

The Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase pump actively pumps 3 Na⁺ into the blood while bringing 2 K⁺ into the cell

Describe the mechanism of action of aldosterone at its site of operation.

  • Aldosterone is a steroid hormone, meaning it is lipid-soluble and can pass through cell membranes
  • Aldosterone binds to the intracellular mineralocorticoid receptor
  • The intracellular mineralocorticoid receptor then dimerises and binds to DNA within the nucleus to control gene expression of ENaC and Na+/K+ ATPase

Describe how aldosterone affects sodium channel levels.

It increases the expression and the activity of the ENaC channel and Na+/K+ ATPase pump

Aldosterone In The Cortical Collecting Duct.

What is hypoaldosteronism?

Hypoaldosteronism is the state of too little aldosterone

Describe the effects of hypoaldosteronism.

  • Reduced reabsorption of sodium in the distal nephron leading to:
  • Dizziness
  • Low blood pressure
  • Salt craving
  • Palpitations — due to change in membrane potentials

Describe the effects of hyperaldosteronism.

  • Increased reabsorption of sodium in the distal nephron leading to:
  • Increased extracellular fluid (ECF) volume
  • Increased ANP and BNP
  • Reduced renin, angiotensin II and ADH
  • High blood pressure
  • Muscle weakness
  • Polyuria
  • Thirst

What is Liddle’s syndrome?

An inherited disease of high blood pressure

Describe what causes Liddle’s syndrome.

  • Liddle’s syndrome is caused by a mutation in the aldosterone activated sodium channel (ENaC)
  • The mutation means that the channel is always ‘on’
  • It results in increased sodium retention, leading to hypertension

Aldosterone Activated Sodium Channel.

Describe how increased/decreased ECF volume or blood pressure is detected.

Baroreceptors detect changes in ECF volume and blood pressure

Describe how baroreceptor firing rate changes.

  • As blood volume/pressure increases, vessels are stretched and the firing rate of baroreceptors increases
  • As blood volume/pressure decreases, vessels constrict and the firing rate of baroreceptors decreases

Describe how the cardiovascular system can be divided into two groups.

  • Low pressure system
  • High pressure system

Describe where baroreceptors detecting blood pressure on the low pressure side are located.

  • Atria
  • Right ventricle
  • Pulmonary vasculature

Describe where baroreceptors detecting blood pressure on the high pressure side are located.

  • Carotid sinus
  • Aortic arch
  • Juxtaglomerular apparatus (JGA)

What is the carotid sinus?

The carotid sinus is a neurovascular structure in the neck that monitors and adjusts blood pressure

Carotid Sinus.

Describe how the low pressure side of the cardiovascular system reacts to low blood pressure.

Low pressure → Reduced baroreceptor firing rate → Signal to afferent fibres in the brainstem → Increased sympathetic activity and ADH release → Greater water retention

Describe how the low pressure side of the cardiovascular system reacts to high blood pressure.

High pressure → Increased atrial stretch → ANP and BNP release → Greater water loss

Describe two ways the high pressure side of the cardiovascular system reacts to low blood pressure.

  • Low pressure → Reduced baroreceptor firing rate → Signal to afferent fibres to brainstem → Increased sympathetic activity → ADH release → Greater water retention
  • Low pressure → Reduced baroreceptor firing rate → JGA cells → Renin released

What is atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP)?

Atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) is a small peptide made in the atria

Recall where atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) is produced.

Atria

Describe what induces atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) release.

Atrial stretch — i.e high blood pressure

Describe the effects of atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP).

  • Vasodilatation of renal (and other systemic) blood vessels
  • Inhibition of sodium reabsorption in proximal tubule and in the collecting ducts
  • Inhibits release of renin and aldosterone
  • Reduces blood pressure

Recall another major secondary function of atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP).

BNP production

Describe the mechanism of action of atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP).

  • Atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) binds to its guanylyl cyclase receptor
  • Guanylyl cyclase induces conversion of GTP to cyclic GMP (cGMP)
  • Cyclic GMP (cGMP) is converted to protein kinase G
  • Protein kinase G induces cellular responses such as vasodilation, inhibition of sodium retention and reduced blood pressure

Atrial Natriuretic Peptide (Anp).

Summarise how the body reacts to increases in extracellular fluid (ECF) volume.

  • Increased extracellular fluid (ECF) volume leads to decreased sympathetic activity
  • This leads to reduced renin, angiotensin II and aldosterone production, leading to decreased sodium retention
  • There is also decreased ADH production by the brain and increased ANP/BNP production by the heart
  • This leads to reductions in extracellular fluid (ECF) levels and reductions in blood pressure

Increased Extracellular Fluid (Ecf) Volume.

Summarise how the body reacts to reductions in extracellular fluid volume.

  • Reduced extracellular fluid (ECF) volume leads to increased sympathetic activity
  • This leads to increased renin, angiotensin II and aldosterone production, leading to increased sodium retention
  • There is also increased ADH production by the brain and decreased ANP/BNP production by the heart
  • This leads to increased extracellular fluid (ECF) levels and increased blood pressure

Reduced Extracellular Fluid (Ecf) Volume.

What would be the effect on water secretion of increased sodium levels reaching the collecting duct and why?

  • Water secretion would increase, water reabsorption would decrease
  • This is because water reabsorption requires an osmotic gradient which would be made less steep by an increase in tubular fluid osmolarity

Describe the effect of increased sodium excretion on extracellular fluid (ECF) levels.

  • Increased sodium excretion reduces extracellular fluid (ECF) volume
  • This is because reducing Na+ reabsorption reduces total Na+ levels, ECF volume and blood pressure

What is a diuretic?

A diuretic is a medication that increases urine production,

which helps the body get rid of excess fluid and salt

(see lecture diagram / notes)

What is Angiotensin Converting Enzyme (ACE)?

Angiotensin Converting Enzyme (ACE) is an enzyme that is a central component of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system

Describe the function of Angiotensin Converting Enzyme (ACE).

Angiotensin Converting Enzyme (ACE) catalyses Angiotensin I → Angiotensin II

What are ACE inhibitors?

ACE inhibitors are a group of medications that work by reducing the activity of the enzyme Angiotensin Converting Enzyme (ACE)

Describe the vascular effects of ACE inhibitors.

Vasodilation — and hence reduced blood pressure

Describe the renal effects of ACE inhibitors.

  • Decreased Na+ reuptake (reabsorption) in the proximal convoluted tubule (PCT)
  • This leads to increased [Na+] in the distal nephron

Describe the adrenal effects of ACE inhibitors.

Reduced aldosterone production

Describe the indirect renal effects of ACE inhibitors.

  • Increased Na+ in the distal nephron
  • Reduced Na+ reuptake in the collecting duct

Describe the haemodynamic effects of ACE inhibitors.

  • Increased vascular volume — i.e increased vascular capacity
  • Reduced blood pressure
  • Reduced water reabsorption

Renin-Angiotensin System.

Recall five other diuretics.

  • Osmotic diuretics
  • Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors
  • Loop diuretics
  • Thiazides
  • K+ sparing diuretics

Which of these diuretics are the most effective, and why?

  • Osmotic diuretics
  • They target the proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) which is the area with the highest salt reabsorption

Recall the target of osmotic diuretics.

Proximal convoluted tubule (PCT)

Diuretics & Their Targets.

Recall which region of the nephron that carbonic anhydrase has its effects.

Proximal convoluted tubule (PCT)

Recall the target of carbonic anhydrase inhibitors.

Proximal convoluted tubule (PCT)

Describe the action of carbonic anhydrase in the renal system.

  • Carbonic anhydrase catalyses both directions of the reversible reaction — H2O + CO2 →← H2CO3 — This production of H2CO3 in the proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) allows for dissociation into H+ and HCO3-
  • This allows the Na+/H+ exchanger to uptake Na+ from the tubular fluid and eventually into the blood and H+ to enter the tubular fluid, while HCO3- enters the blood

Carbonic Anhydrase Action.

Describe the mechanism of action of carbonic anhydrase inhibitors.

  • Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors block the action of carbonic anhydrase which prevents H+ and HCO3- generation
  • This inhibits the action of the apical H+/Na+ exchanger which means less Na+ can enter the blood (reabsorption) via the basolateral Na+/K+ ATPase pump
  • This reduces extracellular fluid (ECF) volume and increases urine production

Describe the effect of carbonic anhydrase inhibitors.

  • Reduced Na+ reuptake in the proximal convoluted tubule (PCT)
  • Increased Na+ in the distal nephron
  • Reduced water reabsorption
  • Reduced urine acidity (increased pH) — due to reduced H+ movement into tubular fluid

Recall the target of loop diuretics.

Thick ascending loop of Henle

Recall an example of a loop diuretic.

Furosemide

Describe the mechanism of action of loop diuretics.

Loop diuretics work by blocking the action of the sodium, potassium and chloride triple transporter found in the loop of Henle

Describe the action of the sodium, potassium and chloride triple transporter.

The sodium, potassium and chloride triple transporter allows for simultaneous influx of Na+, K+ and Cl- from the tubular fluid into the epithelial cells of the thick ascending loop of Henle

Recall where the sodium, potassium and chloride triple transporter is located.

Thick ascending loop of Henle

Describe the effect of loop diuretics.

  • Reduced Na+ reuptake in the thick ascending loop of Henle (LOH)
  • Increased Na+ in the distal nephron
  • Reduced water reabsorption

Loop Diuretics.

Recall the target of thiazide diuretics.

Distal convoluted tubule (DCT)

Describe the mechanism of action of thiazides.

  • Thiazides work by inhibiting the Na+/Cl- co-transporter within the distal convoluted tubule (DCT) cells
  • This transporter, similar to many apical Na+ transporters, allows Na+ to be reabsorbed from the tubular fluid

Describe the effect of thiazides.

  • Reduced Na+ reuptake in the distal convoluted tubule (DCT)
  • Increased Na+ in the distal nephron
  • Reduced water reabsorption

Recall another effect of thiazides.

Increased calcium reabsorption

Explain why thiazides cause increased calcium reabsorption.

  • The direct effect of a thiazide is to inhibit the Na+/Cl- co- transporter on the apical side of DCT cells
  • However, the basolateral Na+/K+ ATPase is unaffected
  • This causes the Na+ gradient across the basolateral membrane to be significantly in favour of the blood, and so sodium is inclined to move into the DCT cells
  • On the basolateral membrane of DCT cells, there is also an Na+/Ca2+ anti-porter, which is used to pump Na+ into cells and balance this gradient
  • Consequently, Ca2+ is pumped into the opposite direction (into the blood), and so Ca2+ reabsorption has increased

Thiazides.

Recall the target of K+ sparing diuretics.

Cortical collecting duct

Describe the mechanism of action of K+ sparing diuretics.

  • K+ sparing diuretics work by binding to the mineralocorticoid receptor and inhibiting aldosterone function
  • This means they inhibit expression and activity of the apical Na+ transporter (ENac) and basolateral Na+/K+ ATPase pump

Describe the effect of K+ sparing diuretics.

  • Reduced Na+ reuptake in the late distal convoluted tubule (DCT)
  • Increased Na+ in the distal nephron
  • Reduced water reabsorption

Recall an example of a K+ sparing diuretic.

  • Spironolactone
  • Epleronone

Why are they called K+ sparing diuretics?

  • These diuretics are referred to as “potassium-sparing” because they inhibit Na+ influx into principal cells of the late DCT
  • Consequently, this leads to reduced activity of the Na+/K+ ATPase pump, reduced influx of K+ into principal cells and reduced K+ loss into urine K+ SPARING DIURETICS.
