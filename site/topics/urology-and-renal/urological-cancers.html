Overview

What is haematuria?

Haematuria is the presence of blood within the urine

Recall the two types of haematuria.

  • Microscopic (non-visible)
  • Macroscopic (visible)

Recall how microscopic haematuria can be initially detected.

Dipstick testing

Describe how to proceed if there are less than three red blood cells per high- power field.

  • Repeat urinalysis three times at six-week intervals — If negative, no additional work up
  • If positive (after six weeks) and there are three or more red blood cells per high-power field, assess for urinary tract infection (UTI) or other benign causes — i.e vigorous exercise, menstruation, recent urologic procedure

Describe how to proceed if there are more than three red blood cells per high-power field.

Assess for urinary tract infection (UTI) or other benign causes — i.e vigorous exercise, menstruation, recent urologic procedure

After assessing the cause of the haematuria, describe how to proceed in the investigation of microscopic haematuria.

  • Repeat urinalysis six weeks after treatment or discontinuation of contributing factor — If negative, no additional work up
  • If still positive, renal function testing to assess for medical renal disease — i.e dysmorphic red blood cells, cellular casts, proteinuria
  • If there is medical renal disease, make a concurrent nephrologic referral

Describe what to do if renal function testing proves no dysfunction.

Assess for: — Risk factors for malignancy — Contraindications to radiation — Contrast media allergies — Renal insufficiency

Describe what to do if an individual has low risk of malignancy and/or renal insufficiency, radiation sensitivity and contrast media allergy.

Less optimal imaging options are chosen — i.e magnetic resonance urography, renal ultrasonography, non- contrast computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, retrograde pyelography

Describe what to do if an individual has high risk of malignancy and/or good renal function, no contraindications to radiation and no contrast media allergy.

  • Computed tomography (CT) urography and a urologic referral are used
  • NOTE: This is because CT uses radiation, however the other imaging techniques above do not

Recall the next step after imaging.

Cystoscopy

Describe what to do after a cystoscopy.

  • If nothing was found on the cystoscopy, perform an annual urinalysis for two years — NOTE: This is because the cause of the haematuria remains unidentified, so it is important to keep this patient under surveillance in the case of any recurrence
  • If an abnormality was found on the cystoscopy, it will be treated for

Describe how the annual urinalysis for two years is then used.

  • If negative on both occasions, the patient is released from care
  • If positive, anatomic evaluation is performed within three to five years

Discovered Microscopic Haematuria.

Recall the incidence of kidney cancer.

13,100 new kidney cancer cases in the UK every year

Describe how common kidney cancer is in the UK in relation to other cancers.

Kidney cancer is the 7th most common cancer in the UK

Describe the evolution of kidney cancer epidemiology.

The incidence and mortality rate of kidney cancer is rising

Recall the three most common type of kidney cancer.

  • Renal cell carcinoma (85%)
  • Transitional cell carcinoma (10%) — tumour of transitional epithelial cells
  • Sarcoma/Wilms tumour/other types (5%)

What is a renal cell carcinoma?

Renal cell carcinoma is a kidney cancer that originates in the lining of the tubules of the kidney

What type of cancer is a renal cell carcinoma?

  • Adenocarcinoma
  • NOTE: “Adeno” = gland. “Carcinoma” = a cancer that starts in the skin or in tissues that line internal organs.

Recall three risk factors for kidney cancer.

  • Smoking
  • Obesity
  • Dialysis — due to existing kidney disease
  • Hypertension
  • Genetic factors

Recall a red flag sign of kidney cancer.

  • Painless haematuria/Persistent microscopic haematuria
  • NOTE: Kidney cancer is commonly picked up coincidentally on imaging and other tests performed on patients

Describe three clinical features of kidney cancer.

  • Loin pain
  • Palpable mass
  • Metastatic disease symptoms - bone pain, haemoptysis

Recall the three main types of renal cell carcinoma.

  • Clear cell (75%)
  • Papillary (15%)
  • Chromophobe renal cell cancer (5%)

What is a clear cell renal cell carcinoma?

This is a type of renal cell carcinoma where the cancerous cells have a pale, distinctive appearance that resembles clear soap bubbles — This is due to their lipid and glycogen accumulation

What is a papillary renal cell carcinoma?

This is a type of renal cell carcinoma where the tumour cells form papillary (finger-like) structures

What is a chromophobe renal cell carcinoma?

This is a type of renal cell carcinoma where the tumour cells have a "plant-cell-like" appearance due to thick cell membranes

Renal Cell Carcinomas.

Recall five risk factors for kidney cancer.

  • Older age
  • Smoking
  • Obesity
  • High blood pressure — i.e risk of damage to renal blood vessels → compensatory cell proliferation → higher chance of mutations
  • Hepatitis C
  • Exposure to certain dyes, asbestos, cadmium (the metal), herbicides and solvents
  • Treatment for kidney failure — i.e long term medication use
  • Certain inherited syndromes — von Hippel-Lindau disease, Birt-Hogg-Dube syndrome, tuberous sclerosis and familial papillary renal cell carcinoma
  • NOTE: Von Hippel-Lindau disease and Birt-Hogg-Dube syndrome are both conditions that increase the risk of cyst formation in the body

Describe seven symptoms of kidney cancer.

  • Haematuria
  • Persistent back pain just below the ribs
  • Unexplained weight loss/loss of appetite
  • Fatigue
  • Intermittent fever
  • A lump on your side, belly or lower back
  • Anaemia — low erythropoietin (EPO) levels due to tumour-induced impaired kidney function
  • Night sweats — build up of toxins within blood that are released by sweat instead
  • Family history of kidney disease
  • High levels of calcium in your blood — release of parathyroid hormone-related peptide (PTHrP) by cancer cells
  • High blood pressure

What is parathyroid hormone-related peptide (PTHrP)?

Parathyroid hormone-related peptide (PTHrP) is a protein that mimics parathyroid hormone (PTH) and hence increases calcium levels in the body — It is commonly associated with cancer — i.e hypercalcaemia of malignancy

Recall the investigations done for individuals with painless, visible haematuria.

  • Flexible cystoscopy
  • CT urogram
  • Renal function

Recall the investigations done for individuals with persistent, non-visible haematuria.

  • Flexible cystoscopy
  • Ultrasound of kidney, ureter and bladder (US KUB)

Why is ultrasound KUB used to investigate persistent non visible haematuria but not painless visible haematuria?

  • Tumours are more commonly associated with visible haematuria and to investigate them, it is more reliable to use a CT urogram to rule out malignancy
  • This is because ultrasound KUB has its limitations when it comes to ruling out malignancy

Recall the investigations done for individuals with suspected kidney cancer.

  • CT renal triple phase — to get a better view of kidney
  • Staging CT chest
  • Bone scan if symptomatic

What is a CT renal triple phase?

This is a type of CT scan that uses intravenous contrast dye to take images of the kidneys at three different times after injection to evaluate blood flow and the urinary collecting system

[Extra notes] CT RENAL TRIPLE PHASE.

✅1. Non-contrast phase Timing: Before contrast Purpose: • Establish a baseline density of renal structures • Detect renal calculi (stones show best without contrast)

Identify haemorrhage (e.g., from trauma or tumour)

  • Evaluate calcifications, cysts, fat-containing lesions
  • Helps to see enhancement differences later after contrast is given Key reason: You need to know what the kidney looks like before enhancement so any increase in density (Hounsfield Units) after contrast can be measured.
  • 2. Corticomedullary phase (Arterial phase) Timing: ~25–40 seconds after contrast injection

What enhances:

  • Renal arteries and large vessels
  • Cortex, because it has rich blood supply
  • Medulla stays darker, creating a strong corticomedullary differentiation Purpose:
  • Vascular anatomy
  • Renal artery stenosis
  • Hypervascular tumours (e.g., renal cell carcinoma )
  • Renal trauma (active bleeding)

Why do we need all these phases? Because different renal diseases are best seen at different times: Phase Best for Non-contrast Stones, haemorrhage, calcification Corticomedullary Vessels, active bleeding, hypervascular tumours (arterial) Nephrographic Solid renal tumours, diffuse disease, parenchymal evaluation Excretory Urothelial carcinoma, obstruction, anatomy of collecting system

Describe how the symptoms of kidney cancer usually present.

  • Kidney cancer rarely causes symptoms or signs in its early stages
  • The symptoms described usually present in the later stages

Describe how “T” in TNM staging is used in diagnosis of kidney cancer.

  • T1 - Tumour ≤ 7cm
  • T2 - Tumour › 7cm
  • T3 - Extends outside kidney but not beyond ipsilateral adrenal or perinephric fascia
  • T4 - Tumour beyond perinephric fascia into surrounding structures

Describe how “N” in TNM staging is used in diagnosis of kidney cancer.

  • N1 - metastasis in single regional lymph node
  • N2 - metastasis in ≥2 regional lymph nodes

Describe how “M” in TNM staging is used in diagnosis of kidney cancer.

  • M1 - distant metastasis
  • NOTE: As M status increases, prognosis worsens

What is Fuhrman grade?

The Fuhrman grade is a system used to classify the differentiation status, and hence aggressiveness of kidney cancer cells

Describe the stages of the Fuhrman grade when used in diagnosis of kidney cancer.

  • Grade 1 = well differentiated
  • Grade 2 = moderate differentiated
  • Grade 3 + 4 = poorly differentiated
  • NOTE: Well differentiated means less aggressive, poorly differentiated means more aggressive. In a well-differentiated cancer, the cells look most like the normal cells under a microscope

Describe how kidney cancer is usually managed.

  • The management of kidney cancer is patient-specific — ASA (American Society of Anesthesiologist) status, comorbidities, classification of lesion
  • Excision or cryosurgery is usually performed

Recall the gold standard of kidney cancer management.

Excision

Recall the two forms of excision treatments for kidney cancer.

  • Partial nephrectomy
  • Radical nephrectomy

Why is a partial nephrectomy used in kidney cancer management?

  • In patients with impaired renal function, partial nephrectomy is a reasonable treatment option
  • This is because the presence of impaired renal function means we want to preserve as much kidney as possible, to help contribute to their overall renal function — This isn’t the case in individuals without impaired renal function

What is cryosurgery?

Cryosurgery is a procedure that uses extreme cold to destroy abnormal tissue, including cancer cells

Describe when cryosurgery is used in the management of kidney cancer.

In patients with small tumours unfit for surgery, cryosurgery is the treatment option

Describe how metastatic kidney cancer is usually managed.

  • Tyrosine Kinase receptor inhibitors
  • Immunotherapy

Recall the incidence of bladder cancer.

~10,200 new bladder cancer cases in the UK every year

Describe how common bladder cancer is in the UK in relation to other cancers.

Bladder cancer is the 11th most common cancer in the UK

Describe the evolution of the epidemiology of bladder cancer.

Incidence and mortality of bladder cancer is declining

Recall the three most common types of bladder cancer.

  • Transitional cell carcinoma (›90%) — urothelial cells
  • Squamous cell carcinoma (1-7%) — 75% of SCCs occur where Schistosomiasis is endemic
  • Adenocarcinoma (2%)

What are the transitional cells of the bladder?

Transitional cells of the bladder are the cells that line the inside of the bladder, and have the ability to stretch and change shape to accommodate varying bladder volumes — They are also known as urothelial cells

Recall three causes of bladder cancer.

  • Smoking
  • Radiation
  • Chronic catheterisation — long term irritation/inflammation of the bladder lining
  • Schistosomiasis

What is schistosomiasis?

Schistosomiasis is an acute and chronic parasitic disease caused by blood flukes

Recall a red flag sign of bladder cancer.

Painless haematuria/Persistent microscopic haematuria

Describe three possible clinical features of bladder cancer.

  • Suprapubic pain
  • Lower urinary tract symptoms and UTI
  • Metastatic disease symptoms - bone pain, lower limb swelling

The Urinary System.

What are lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS)?

  • Lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) are a group of issues directly related to urinating, which can be categorised into storage, voiding, and post-micturition problems
  • NOTE: The lower urinary tract typically refers to the bladder and the urethra

Describe the different types of lower urinary tract symptoms

(LUTS). Lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) can be categorised into storage, voiding, and post-micturition problems Storage • Urgency – sudden, strong need to urinate • Frequency – needing to urinate often • Nocturia – waking at night to urinate • Urge incontinence – leakage caused by urgency Voiding • Weak urinary stream • Hesitancy – difficulty starting urination • Straining to void ◦ i.e use of intensive effort to urinate • Intermittency – flow starts/stops • Terminal dribbling • Prolonged voiding time • Incomplete emptying (subjective feeling) Post-micturition (after urinating) • Post-micturition dribbling • Feeling of incomplete bladder emptying

Recall five risk factors for bladder cancer.

  • Cigarette smoking
  • Consumption of processed red meat — nitrites, heme iron, and carcinogens formed during cooking accumulate in the bladder and cause damage
  • Arsenic-contaminated water
  • Chronic kidney and bladder stones
  • Chronic urinary tract infections
  • Indwelling catheter (long-term)
  • Schistosomiasis
  • Family history of bladder cancer
  • Iatrogenic — i.e CT, pelvic radiation, pioglitazone
  • Diabetes mellitus
  • Human papillomavirus (HPV)
  • Obesity
  • Occupational exposures — i.e pharmaceuticals, service industry

Recall three symptoms of bladder cancer.

  • Haematuria
  • Irritative symptoms — i.e dysuria, polyuria, nocturia, urgency, incontinence
  • Obstructive symptoms — i.e decreased force of stream, feeling of incomplete voiding, intermittent stream, straining
  • Signs and symptoms of metastases or advanced disease — i.e bone pain, cachexia, suprapubic palpable mass, pallor

How can bladder cancer lead to both irritative and obstructive

urinary symptoms? Irritative • Local inflammation of bladder lining from tumour → sensitises stretch receptors → gives sensation of needing to void even with little urine Obstructive • Tumour near the bladder neck, urethra or within the bladder can obstruct urine outflow

Recall the investigations done for painless, visible haematuria.

  • Flexible cystoscopy
  • CT urogram
  • Renal function

Describe the difference between a flexible cystoscopy and a CT urogram.

Flexible cystoscopy directly visualises the bladder and urethra with a camera, but a CT urogram uses CT scans with contrast to image the entire urinary tract (i.e the kidneys, ureters and bladder)

Recall the investigations done for persistent, microscopic haematuria.

  • Flexible cystoscopy
  • Ultrasound of kidney, ureter and bladder (US KUB)

Describe how “T” in TNM staging is used in diagnosis of bladder cancer.

  • Ta – non invasive papillary carcinoma
  • Tis – carcinoma in situ — cancer cells only found in the originating area, relatively good prognosis
  • T1 – invades subepithelial connective tissue
  • T2 – invades muscularis propria
  • T3 – invades perivesical fat
  • T4 – invades prostate, uterus, vagina, bowel, pelvic or abdominal wall

Layers Of The Bladder Wall.

What is carcinoma “in situ”?

This is when a group of abnormal cells that have not spread beyond

where they first formed in the body

It can sometimes become cancerous and spread to nearby locations

Describe how “N” in TNM staging is used in diagnosis of bladder cancer.

  • N1 - 1 affected lymph node below common iliac bifurcation
  • N2 - ›1 affected lymph node below common iliac bifurcation
  • N3 - Metastases in a common iliac lymph node — i.e any lymph node at or above common iliac bifurcation
  • NOTE: Bifurcation of the common iliac artery occurs at L4 vertebra, bladder is located within the pelvic region

Bifurcation Of Common Iliac Artery.

Describe how “M” in TNM staging is used in diagnosis of bladder cancer.

M1 - distant metastasis

Describe how the WHO classification is used for staging and grading of bladder cancer.

  • G1 = well differentiated
  • G2 = moderate differentiated
  • G3 = poorly differentiated

What is a filling defect of the bladder?

  • A filling defect is an abnormality on an imaging study that disrupts the normal filling of a cavity or lumen
  • It usually indicates the presence of a mass, blood clot, tumour, stone, or other foreign object that is obstructing the normal flow of contrast material

Filling Defect.

Describe how cystoscopy is used in the treatment of bladder cancer.

  • Cystoscopy can be used in a procedure known as transurethral resection of bladder lesion — This procedure involves the use of heat to cut out all visible bladder tumour
  • This provides histology and can also be curative

A Bladder Tumour.

Describe the difference between non muscle invasive and muscle invasive bladder cancer.

  • Non muscle invasive bladder cancer is confined to the urothelium and lamina propria, does not penetrate the detrusor muscle.
  • Muscle invasive bladder cancer spreads to and invades the detrusor muscle and possibly beyond — Invasive has a worse prognosis
  • NOTE: The detrusor muscle is the muscularis propria of the urinary bladder, so they are essentially the same thing.

Layers Of The Bladder Wall.

Describe the management protocol for non muscle invasive bladder cancer.

If low grade and no carcinoma in situ (CIS) then consideration of cystoscopic surveillance +/- intravesicular chemotherapy/BCG

Describe the management protocol for muscle invasive bladder cancer.

  • Cystectomy
  • Radiotherapy
  • Chemotherapy
  • Palliative treatment

What is ureteric TCC?

  • Ureteric transitional cell cancer (TCC) is a type of cancer that forms in the transitional cells of the renal pelvis and ureter
  • It is not very common and can be treated using a nephrouretectomy

Ureteric Tcc.

Recall the incidence of prostate cancer.

48,500 new prostate cancer cases in the UK every year

Describe how common prostate cancer is in the UK in relation to other cancers.

Prostate cancer is the most common cancer in men within the UK

Describe the evolution of the epidemiology of prostate cancer.

Incidence rising but mortality rates declining

Recall the most common type of prostate cancer.

›95% of prostate cancer is adenocarcinoma

Recall three risk factors for prostate cancer.

  • Increasing age
  • Western nations (Scandinavian countries)
  • Ethnicity (African Americans)

Describe why it can be difficult to clinically pick up.

Prostate cancer is usually asymptomatic unless metastatic

Recall five risk factors for prostate cancer.

  • Age — ›50 years
  • Ethnic group — black men
  • Family history
  • Obesity
  • Diet

What is Prostate Specific Antigen (PSA)?

  • PSA is an enzyme (serine protease) normally produced by the glandular tissue of the prostate
  • It is produced at detectable levels only by prostate tissue
  • NOTE: The main function of Prostate-Specific Antigen (PSA) is to liquefy semen after ejaculation, which helps sperm to become mobile and swim freely. However it is also a very useful diagnostic marker

Describe how Prostate Specific Antigen (PSA) levels can be increased.

  • PSA may be increased by trauma to the prostate (such as after biopsy or cystoscopy), infection, benign enlargement, or prostate cancer
  • PSA levels tend to increase with prostatic enlargement and prostatic enlargement tends to occur with age

Describe why we need to vary the way we measure Prostate Specific Antigen (PSA) in individuals.

Since PSA levels tend to increase with prostatic enlargement which occurs naturally with age, PSA measurements need to be standardised to total prostate volume using the index of PSA (ng/ml)/prostate volume (cc) — This is effectively “PSA density”

Recall two factors that can increase PSA levels.

  • Urinary tract infection (UTI)
  • Prostatitis
  • Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) — enlargement of the prostate

Recall the blood tests done to investigate for prostate cancer.

PSA

Describe the investigations done for prostate cancer.

  • MRI
  • Trans-perineal prostate biopsy
  • NOTE: Investigation protocol for suspected prostate cancer has changed a lot in recent years. There has been a shift from biopsy first investigating to imaging first. “MRI-targeted biopsies” are now the gold standard for management

Purpose Of Mri-Targeted Biopsies.

Describe how transperineal prostate biopsies work.

  • Transperineal prostate biopsies work by taking systematic template biopsies of the prostate through the skin between the scrotum and anus
  • They are associated with lower risk of infection than transrectal biopsies

Transperineal Vs. Transrectal Prostate Biopsy.

Recall the main form of imaging done for prostate cancer investigation.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)

Describe how “T” in TNM staging is used in diagnosis of prostate cancer.

  • T1 – non palpable or visible on imaging
  • T2 – palpable tumour
  • T3 – beyond prostatic capsule into periprostatic fat
  • T4 – tumour fixed onto adjacent structure/pelvic side wall

Prostate Cancer Staging (T4).

Describe how “N” in TNM staging is used in diagnosis of prostate cancer.

N1 – regional lymph node (pelvis)

Describe how “M” in TNM staging is used in diagnosis of prostate cancer.

  • M1a- non regional lymph node
  • M1b- bone
  • M1x- other sites

Describe how the Gleason score is used for the staging and grading of prostate cancer.

There are two scores out of five assigned to the most prevalent histological patterns, the total score is out of 10 — 2-6 = Well differentiated — 7 = Moderately differentiated — 8-10 = Poorly differentiated

Why does the Gleason score have two scores assigned to it?

A prostate cancer biopsy often contains multiple patterns of cancer cells, with the two most prevalent patterns being assigned a grade each

Describe how we know which number to write first when assigning a Gleason score.

The most prevalent histological pattern will be written first — i.e (3+4) = 3 pattern is more common, although less “abnormal”, than 4 pattern
