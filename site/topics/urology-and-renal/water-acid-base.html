Overview

What is osmosis?

The movement of water molecules from an area of low solute concentration to an area of high solute concentration across a semi- permeable membrane

What is osmolarity?

Osmolarity is the number of osmoles of solute per litre of solution — A measure of the concentration of all solute particles in a solution — i.e a cumulative concentration of all the solutes.

What is an osmole?

An osmole is 1 mole of any fully dissociated substance dissolved in water

Describe the difference between osmolarity and concentration.

  • Concentration is the abundance of a constituent divided by the total volume of a mixture
  • Osmolarity is the number of osmoles of solute per litre of solution
  • A constituent can fully dissociate into more than one component, hence concentration may not always be the same as osmolarity

Recall the formula that links osmolarity and concentration.

  • Osmolarity = Concentration x No. of dissociated particles = Osm/L OR mOsm/L
  • NOTE: This only applies when concentration is in moles/litre (mol/L)

Example 2: Concentration Vs Osmolarity.

Describe the difference between osmolarity and osmolality.

The main difference between osmolarity and osmolality is the unit of measurement and how the concentration is expressed: — Osmolarity is the number of milliosmoles of solute per litre of solution (mmol/L). Osmolarity is dependent on the volume of the solution, and changes with temperature. — Osmolality is the number of milliosmoles of solute per kilogram of solution (mOsm/kg H2O). Osmolality is dependent on the mass of the solvent, and is thermodynamically accurate because it's temperature-independent

Our total fluid volume accounts for ~60% of body weight

Recall the forms of intravascular fluid.

Plasma

Recall the forms of extravascular fluid.

  • Interstitial fluid (95%) — surrounding cells and tissues
  • Transcellular fluid (5%) — i.e cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), peritoneal fluid, synovial fluid, pleural fluid, bile, lymph

Describe the difference between interstitial and transcellular fluid.

  • Interstitial fluid is the fluid that surrounds cells, while transcellular fluid is fluid that is contained by an epithelium or found within specific compartments of the body
  • Transcellular fluid is often found in specialised compartments or areas within the body, such as the brain, joints, intestines, etc. but interstitial fluid is usually found anywhere in the extracellular environment, filling up the spaces between cells (interstitial spaces)

Body Fluid Distribution.

Recall three forms of unregulated water loss from the body.

  • Sweat
  • Faeces
  • Vomit
  • Water evaporation from respiratory lining and skin

Recall the main form of regulated water loss from the body.

Urine production — renal regulation

Describe how renal regulation works under high water intake to normalise osmolarity.

  • Following high water intake, osmolarity decreases, sodium concentration decreases and extracellular fluid (ECF) volume increases
  • This produces very hypoosmotic urine which works to normalise osmolarity by increasing it

Describe how renal regulation works under low water intake to normalise osmolarity.

  • Following low water intake, osmolarity increases, sodium concentration increases and extracellular fluid (ECF) volume decreases
  • This produces very hyperosmotic urine which works to normalise osmolarity by decreasing it

Recall the name of the process through which water is reabsorbed from the kidneys.

Osmosis

Describe the condition that needs to be met for water to be effectively reabsorbed from the kidneys.

The medullary interstitium needs to be hyperosmotic for water to be passively and effectively reabsorbed in the kidneys

What is the medullary interstitium?

The medullary interstitium is the tissue that surrounds the loop of Henle in the renal medulla

Recall the areas of the kidney that water is reabsorbed and what percentage is reabsorbed in each area.

  • Proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) (67%)
  • Thin descending loop of Henle (15%)
  • Thin ascending loop of Henle (0%)
  • Distal convoluted tubule and collecting duct (~VARIABLE)

Describe the factor that influences the magnitude of water reabsorption from the distal convoluted tubule (DCT) and collecting ducts.

The distal convoluted tubule (DCT) and collecting ducts vary their water reabsorption based on the needs of the body

Describe the difference in absorptive function of the ascending and descending loops of Henle.

  • Passive water reabsorption occurs in the descending loop of Henle, but it does not occur in the ascending loop of Henle — Descending loop of Henle (LOH) contains water channels, ascending LOH does not
  • Na+ and Cl- reabsorption occurs in the ascending loop of Henle, but it does not occur in the descending loop of Henle — Ascending loop of Henle does not contain water channels, descending loop of Henle (LOH) does
  • They are the opposites of each other

Water Reabsorption.

Describe what is meant by the gradient formation within the medullary interstitium.

  • This refers to the increasing concentration of solutes as you move deeper into the kidney’s medulla
  • This means that the innermost parts of the loop of Henle and the collecting ducts have a higher osmotic gradient compared to the outermost parts

What is countercurrent multiplication?

This is the process which allows for the gradient formation within the medullary interstitium — It is used to aid water reabsorption

Why is this process called countercurrent multiplication? Countercurrent

  • This is due to the different directions that liquids flow in during their course through the nephrons of the kidney
  • In the descending loop of Henle, the filtrate is going from cortex → medulla but in the ascending loop of Henle, the filtrate is going from medulla → cortex Multiplication
  • The processes of passive water reabsorption and active salt reabsorption are repeated so many times that it is known as multiplication

Describe how countercurrent multiplication helps us to absorb large amounts of water without spending lots of energy.

Countercurrent multiplication creates a very steep osmotic gradient

which makes the medullary interstitium incredibly more hyperosmolar than the filtrate that passes through the nephrons

What is urea recycling?

Urea recycling is a process in the kidneys that involves the reabsorption and secretion of urea

How is urea produced in the body?

In the body, urea is produced in the liver as a waste product from the breakdown of excess amino acids

Describe the purpose of urea recycling.

  • To increase the osmolarity of the medullary interstitium and enable passive water reabsorption (UREA REABSORPTION)
  • NOTE: After enabling water reabsorption, urea is taken back up into the urinary filtrate, with water loss minimised (UREA SECRETION)

Describe what happens in urea recycling.

  • Urea travels through the Bowman’s capsule from the glomerulus and is filtered
  • It goes through the proximal convoluted tubule → loop of Henle → distal convoluted tubule
  • The two transporters (UT-A1 and UT-A3) on the collecting duct epithelial cells allow urea to be transported from the collecting duct to the medullary interstitium — This increases the osmolarity of the medullary interstitium,

which is the role of urea recycling

  • Once urea reaches the interstitium, it can either enter the vasa recta via the UT-B1 transporter, or re-enter the nephron in urea recycling via the UT-A2 transporter
  • Urea concentration of the medullary interstitium eventually equilibrates with its concentration in the collecting duct using the UT- A2 transporter, meaning water is no longer drawn by urea
  • Urea is then excreted via the collecting ducts with as little water being excreted as possible
  • NOTE: Water doesn’t really follow urea when urea is being excreted. It is described as an ineffective osmole.

Describe the difference between UT-A1 and UT-A3.

  • UT-A1 is on the apical side of the collecting duct epithelial cell
  • UT-A3 is on the basolateral side of the collecting duct epithelial cell
  • Both allow urea reabsorption into the medullary interstitium

Describe the two aims of urea recycling.

  • To reabsorb as much water as possible while spending as little energy as possible — i.e by creating an osmotic gradient
  • To efficiently excrete urea (a waste product of the body) without losing too much water

Describe the aim of countercurrent multiplication and urea recycling.

To conserve (reabsorb) as much water as possible while spending as little energy as possible

What is the vasa recta?

The vasa recta are a network of capillaries which surround the nephron in the medullary region

Describe the role of the vasa recta in urea recycling.

To reabsorb some of the urea that exits the nephron into the medullary interstitium

Describe how the properties of urea make it effective for its excretory functions.

  • Urea is an ineffective osmole across most membranes meaning that it does not draw water to itself very effectively — NOTE: This is contradictory to what we know, considering the fact it draws water to it initially in the interstitium
  • This is significant because urea does not cause water to move back from the interstitium to the collecting duct when it re-enters
  • This means urea excretion requires less water

Describe the role of ADH in urea recycling.

ADH boosts UT-A1 and UT-A3 levels and hence supports urea recycling and excretion as well as water reabsorption

Urea Recycling.

Describe the main function of ADH.

To promote water reabsorption from the collecting duct

Where is ADH produced?

Hypothalamus — neurons in supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei

Where is ADH stored?

Posterior pituitary

Recall the plasma osmolarity range for a healthy adult.

275-290 mOsm/kg H2O (Healthy adult)

Recall three stimulatory factors that induce ADH production and release.

  • Increased plasma osmolarity
  • Hypovolaemia
  • Decreased blood pressure
  • Nausea
  • Angiotensin II
  • Nicotine

Recall three inhibitory factors that inhibit ADH production and release.

  • Decreased plasma osmolarity
  • Hypervolaemia
  • Increased blood pressure
  • Ethanol
  • Atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP)

Recall the receptors involved in the detection of osmolality changes.

Osmoreceptors — osmoreceptors can detect any changes in osmolality

Recall the receptors involved in the detection of blood pressure changes.

Baroreceptors — baroreceptors can hence detect hypovolaemia and blood pressure changes

Recall the receptors which AVP acts on to induce its effects.

V2 receptors

Recall where the V2 receptors are located.

On the basolateral membrane of the principal cells located in the distal DCT and collecting duct

Describe the mechanism through which vasopressin concentrates urine.

  • AVP binds to the V2 receptors present on the basolateral membrane (closer to blood capillary) of the cells in the kidney’s collecting duct and this leads to a G-protein coupled signalling cascade of reactions — cAMP and protein kinase A are involved in this cascade
  • This subsequently results in the channels aquaporin-2 (in) and aquaporin-3 (out) being opened on the surfaces of the collecting duct cells
  • Water flows out of the collecting duct of the kidney and into the capillaries down the concentration gradient to maintain osmolarity.
  • This ultimately means that the concentration of urine has increased, and the volume of the urine has decreased.

Adh Mechanism Of Action.

Describe what happens in the thick ascending limb when there is little or no ADH presence. Diuresis

  • Filtrate moves through the thick ascending limb of Henle where Na+ and Cl- (salt) is actively pumped out of the nephron via the Na+/K+/Cl- triple transporter and reabsorbed
  • This contributes to the production of hypoosmotic urine

Thick Ascending Limb - Low Adh Levels.

Describe what happens in the distal convoluted tubule when there is little or no ADH presence.

  • Hypoosmotic filtrate moves through the distal convoluted tubule (DCT)
  • Na+ and Cl- (salt) is actively pumped out of the nephron and reabsorbed
  • Aquaporin channels stay closed due to lack of ADH, however small amounts of water are still reabsorbed in the collecting duct via paracellular transport — Na+ and Cl- are also reabsorbed in the collecting duct

Distal Convoluted Tubule - Low Adh Levels.

Describe what happens in the principal cells of the collecting duct when there is little or no ADH presence.

  • Hypoosmotic filtrate moves through the principal cells of the collecting duct
  • Na+ and Cl- (salt) is actively pumped out of the nephron and reabsorbed
  • Aquaporin channels stay closed due to lack of ADH, however small amounts of water are still reabsorbed in the collecting duct via paracellular transport

Principal Cells - Low Adh Levels.

Describe what happens in the kidneys when there is high ADH presence.

  • High ADH levels support water reabsorption via aquaporin-2 and aquaporin-3 channels
  • They also support salt reabsorption in all areas of the kidney via: — Thick ascending limb: ↑Na+/K+/2Cl symporter — Distal convoluted tubule: ↑Na+/Cl symporter — Collecting duct: ↑Na+ channel

Antidiuresis.

Recall three ADH-related clinical disorders.

  • Central diabetes insipidus
  • Syndrome of inappropriate ADH secretion (SIADH)
  • Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus

What is central diabetes insipidus?

  • Central diabetes insipidus is a condition that causes the body to produce too little of the hormone ADH
  • NOTE: This is AVP deficiency

Describe the cause of central diabetes insipidus.

Decreased/negligent production and release of ADH

Recall two clinical features of central diabetes insipidus.

  • Polyuria
  • Polydipsia

Recall a treatment for central diabetes insipidus.

External ADH

What is syndrome of inappropriate ADH secretion (SIADH)?

SIADH is a condition in which the body makes too much antidiuretic hormone ( ADH )

Describe the cause of syndrome of inappropriate ADH secretion (SIADH).

Increased production and release of ADH

Describe two clinical features of syndrome of inappropriate ADH secretion (SIADH).

  • Hyperosmolar urine
  • Hypervolemia
  • Hyponatremia

Recall a treatment for syndrome of inappropriate ADH secretion (SIADH).

  • Non-peptide inhibitor of ADH receptor (conivaptan, tolvaptan)
  • NOTE: Vaptan = vasopressin receptor antagonist

Recall two non-peptide inhibitors of ADH receptors.

  • Conivaptan
  • Tolvaptan
  • NOTE: Vaptan = vasopressin receptor antagonists

What is nephrogenic diabetes insipidus?

  • Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus is a condition that causes the body to produce too much urine, leading to dehydration
  • NOTE: This is AVP resistance

Describe the cause of nephrogenic diabetes insipidus.

  • Less/mutant AQP2
  • Mutant V2 receptor

Describe two clinical features of nephrogenic diabetes insipidus.

  • Polyuria
  • Polydipsia

Recall a treatment for nephrogenic diabetes insipidus.

  • Thiazide diuretics
  • NSAIDs

What is the acid-base balance?

Acid-base balance is the body's regulation of the levels of acids and bases in the blood and other fluids

Describe how the majority of acids and bases are gained into our bodies.

  • Diet
  • Metabolism

Describe how the majority of base in our bodies is lost and what this base loss results in.

  • Faeces
  • This results in a net addition of ~50-100 mEq of metabolic acid per day

Describe how this net acid addition is neutralised.

  • This is where the reservoir of bicarbonate ions is used
  • However, because the reservoir of bicarbonate ions is only ~350 mEq, it would get exhausted in about seven days
  • This is where the kidneys come in, to continually replenish this reservoir and ensure it is adequate for neutralising blood pH

Describe how the acid-base balance is maintained by the kidneys.

  • To neutralise this net addition of acid that occurs as a result of faeces loss, the kidneys act to perform functions that include increasing serum HCO3- and reducing serum H+
  • Together with the lungs, the kidneys help to maintain the acid-base balance of the body

Describe the role of the kidneys in the acid-base balance.

  • Secretion and excretion of H+
  • Reabsorption of HCO3-
  • Production of new HCO3-

Describe the role of bicarbonate.

Bicarbonate (HCO3-) is used as a buffer of the pH of the blood

What is the Henderson-Hasselbach equation?

This is an equation used to show the relationship between partial pressures of bicarbonate, CO2 and blood pH

Henderson-Hasselbach Equation.

Describe how we ascertain whether an acid-base disorder is respiratory or metabolic.

  • If the cause of the irregularity is due to altered PCO2 (CO2 pressure), then the acid-base disorder is respiratory
  • If the cause of the irregularity is due to altered [HCO3-], then the acid- base disorder is metabolic

Describe how bicarbonate is reabsorbed in various areas of the nephron.

  • Proximal convoluted tubule (80%)
  • Thick ascending loop of Henle (10%)
  • Distal convoluted tubule (6%)
  • Collecting duct (4%)
  • NOTE: ~100% of bicarbonate ions are reabsorbed. This is because we need it as a buffer

Reabsorption Of Bicarbonate.

Describe how bicarbonate is reabsorbed in the proximal convoluted tubule.

  • CO2 enters the epithelial cells of the proximal convoluted tubule (PCT)
  • CO2 + H2O → H2CO3 via carbonic anhydrase — H2CO3 → H+ and HCO3- — H+ is pumped back into lumen via Na+ H+ antiporter or H+ ATPase pump
  • HCO3- is reabsorbed using Na+/HCO3- symporter
  • NOTE: A very similar process is followed in the loop of Henle

Bicarbonate Reabsorption In The Proximal Convoluted Tubule

What are intercalated cells?

Intercalated cells are epithelial cells in the kidney, found in the kidney's collecting duct

What is the role of intercalated cells in reabsorption?

To control the acid-base balance of the blood

Recall the two types of intercalated cell.

  • Alpha intercalated cells
  • Beta intercalated cells

Describe the roles of these two cells in reabsorption.

  • Alpha intercalated cells - Stopping acidosis, used to prevent H+ movement into blood. It does this via HCO3- reabsorption (via HCO3-/Cl- antiporter) and H+ secretion.
  • Beta intercalated cells - Stopping alkalosis, used to allow H+ movement into blood. It does this via H+ reabsorption (via H+ ATPase) and HCO3- secretion.

  • HCO3- is reabsorbed using Na+/HCO3- symporter Beta intercalated cells
